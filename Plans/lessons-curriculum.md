# Lessons Curriculum (course arcs SSOT)

The hand-curated list of interactive courses + their lesson arcs. `/write-lesson` Pass 0 reads a course's arc HERE: which lessons, each lesson's focus + the signature widget(s) it should use, its `curriculum_id`, and access. This complements the roadmap (`www/roadmap-curriculum.js`, RM2): RM2 lists lesson TITLES per track section; this file holds the interactive-COURSE arcs (slug + focus + widget) that RM2 does not. A "complex lesson" can be a 1-lesson course. Widgets are SELECTED from `_build/lesson-visual-catalog.md`; a needed-but-absent widget is hand-built first (never faked).

Format per course:
`## <course_id>  (track, curriculum_id, landing, access)` then a numbered lesson list, each: `<slug> - <focus> - widgets: <ids>`. An entry may carry an owner-approved catalog title right after the slug: `<slug> - title: "<title>" - <focus> - widgets: <ids>`. `Scripts/batch_lessons.py` passes it to every writing and reviewing stage, to be used verbatim.

## t-test  (track: scientist; curriculum_id 4.2.1; landing T-Test-Course.html; access: free)
1. The-t-test-from-scratch - ONE complex lesson teaching hypothesis testing from scratch via the t-test. Arc within the lesson: (a) the question - is a difference real or noise; (b) the sampling distribution of the mean under H0; (c) the t-statistic as a signal-to-noise ratio (define every symbol in MathJax); (d) the p-value as a tail area under H0 (the signature interactive); (e) one-sample vs two-sample (Welch); (f) effect size + sample size + power, and why a small p is not a big effect; (g) doing it in R with t.test(); (h) misuses (p-hacking, multiple comparisons, assuming normality). widgets: null-distribution (signature: drag the observed t, watch the p-value tail), plus a means/sampling illustration. Outcome: the learner computes and correctly READS a p-value and knows when the test (mis)applies.

Arc within the lesson: (a) plain LLM vs an agent that can act; (b) tools - giving the model functions to call; (c) the ReAct loop: Thought -> Action -> Observation, repeating until it can Answer (the signature interactive, stepped through a worked trace); (d) when to stop + guard rails (max steps, validation); (e) building one in R with ellmer (register a tool, run the loop); (f) failure modes (hallucinated tool calls, loops, prompt injection) and how to defend. widgets: agent-loop (signature: step the ReAct trace), process-flow (the build recipe). # Publishing Handbook lesson courses (landing `tutorials/publishing.html`)

One lesson per reviewer objection, grouped into courses by the mechanism they share, so a

## handbook-assumptions  (track: scientist; curriculum_id 4.5.2; landing tutorials/publishing.html; access: free)
1. Assumptions-Lesson-1 - Non-normal residuals: drag skewness up and watch interval coverage barely move, then find the sample size where it does. Why the Central Limit Theorem makes this the mildest of the seven, and what a QQ plot can and cannot tell you. widgets: assumption-dial (signature, assumption="normality"), residual-plot
2. Assumptions-Lesson-2 - Unequal variance: break equal variance and watch 95% coverage collapse while R-squared sits still. The interval is the casualty; Welch and robust standard errors are the repair. widgets: assumption-dial (assumption="heteroskedasticity"), residual-plot
3. Assumptions-Lesson-3 - Non-independent observations: raise the intraclass correlation and see nominal 95% intervals fall well below 95%. Four standard errors compared on the same clustered data. widgets: assumption-dial (assumption="independence"), cluster-icc-sim (signature)
4. Assumptions-Lesson-4 - Autocorrelated residuals: add serial correlation and read how far the standard error understates. Durbin-Watson, and why the fix is the standard error rather than the coefficient. widgets: assumption-dial (assumption="autocorrelation"), residual-plot
5. Assumptions-Lesson-5 - Multicollinearity: correlate two predictors and watch the interval widen while coverage HOLDS at 95%. Collinearity makes an estimate imprecise, not wrong, and the interval already says so. widgets: assumption-dial (assumption="multicollinearity"), decorrelation
6. Assumptions-Lesson-6 - Nonlinear relationships: bend the truth away from a line and watch prediction fail at the edges of the range while R-squared barely moves. A line can be a fine summary and a bad prediction. widgets: assumption-dial (assumption="linearity"), spline-smoother
7. Assumptions-Lesson-7 - Proportional hazards: make the hazard ratio drift over follow-up and see one number fail to describe an effect that changes. Schoenfeld residuals, and what to report instead. widgets: assumption-dial (assumption="proportional-hazards"), km-curve, hazard-ratio

## res-probability  (track: researcher; curriculum_id 4.10; landing Probability-and-Estimation-Course.html; access: free)  [roadmap §1]

2. The-Four-Distributions-Inference-Runs-On - Normal, t, chi-squared and F introduced as the distributions OF things a researcher computes, not shapes to memorise: the normal for a mean, the t for a mean over its own estimated standard error, chi-squared for a sum of squared normals, F for a ratio of two variance estimates. ## res-estimation  (track: researcher; curriculum_id 4.20; landing Estimation-and-the-Bootstrap-Course.html; access: pro)  [roadmap §2]
1. Bias-Variance-and-the-Cost-of-an-Estimator - What makes one estimator better than another, made concrete by putting two competing estimators of the same quantity side by side and scoring both. Bias as the gap between an estimator's long-run average and the truth, variance as how much it moves from sample to sample, and mean squared error as the sum that trades them against each other. The uncomfortable result that a biased estimator can beat an unbiased one on MSE, shown rather than asserted, and why that is not a paradox. widgets: bias-variance, bias-variance-target
2. Maximum-Likelihood-from-the-Curve-Up - Maximum likelihood taught as a picture instead of a formula handed down. Every candidate value of the parameter is scored by how well it explains the data in hand, the scores form a curve, and the estimate is its peak. Found three ways that agree: by eye on the curve, by grid search, and by optimise(). Then the closed form falls out as a shortcut for a thing the reader has already done the slow way, which is the only order that makes the formula memorable. widgets: likelihood-curve, chart-plotter
3. Matching-Moments-to-Estimate-a-Parameter - The method of moments: set the sample mean equal to the distribution's mean, solve, done. Worked for a Poisson, an exponential and a gamma, where it is quick and needs no optimiser. Then the comparison that matters: on the same data, where method of moments and maximum likelihood agree exactly, where they differ, and why the moment estimator is the one that falls apart first when the model is slightly wrong. widgets: likelihood-curve, chart-plotter
4. How-Much-Information-a-Sample-Carries - Fisher information as a reading of the same likelihood curve the reader already knows: how sharply it falls away from its peak. A sharp peak means nearby values are firmly ruled out and the standard error is small; a flat one means the data barely distinguish them. The information computed by differentiating twice, numerically and then in closed form, and the Cramer-Rao bound introduced as what it is, a floor no unbiased estimator can get under, checked by simulation against an estimator that reaches it. widgets: likelihood-curve, bias-variance
5. Two-Bootstrap-Intervals-and-When-They-Differ - The percentile bootstrap and the BCa interval on the same resamples, so the difference is visible rather than described. Why the percentile interval is biased when the statistic's sampling distribution is skewed, what the bias correction and acceleration terms are actually correcting, and the honest finding that at very small samples neither is close to its nominal coverage. Includes the coverage check that proves it, rather than a reassurance that it is fine. widgets: bootstrap-sample, ci-coverage
6. Estimating-Error-by-Leaving-One-Out - The jackknife: recompute the statistic n times, each time with one observation removed, and read the spread of those values as a standard error. Built by hand in base R, set against the bootstrap on the same data, and used for the thing it is still genuinely best at, which is spotting the single observation that is carrying an estimate. Where it fails, on medians and other non-smooth statistics, is shown rather than listed. widgets: bootstrap-sample, leverage-point
7. Standard-Errors-for-Transformed-Estimates - The delta method: once you have a standard error for an estimate, how to get one for a function of it, without starting over. The rule that the standard error gets multiplied by the absolute slope of the transformation, derived from a one-term Taylor expansion and then checked against a bootstrap on the same data. The worked case is the one researchers actually hit, turning a rate into a mean and an odds into a probability, where the interval must be transformed rather than its endpoints recomputed. widgets: likelihood-curve, bootstrap-sample

## res-anova  (track: researcher; curriculum_id 4.50; landing ANOVA-and-Multiple-Comparisons-Course.html; access: pro)  [roadmap §5]

1. Comparing-Several-Groups-at-Once - Why you cannot answer "do these four teaching methods differ" with six t-tests, shown by simulating four identical groups and counting how often at least one pair looks significant. Then the one-way ANOVA built from its parts: variation between group means against variation within groups, assembled by hand as two sums of squares before `aov()` is called, so the F statistic arrives as a ratio the reader computed rather than a number a function returned. What a significant F does and does not tell you, which is that something differs and not which thing. widgets: null-distribution, chart-plotter
2. Two-Factors-and-Their-Combined-Effect - Two-way ANOVA on a design with both a treatment and a block, where the second factor is the one that makes the first one readable. Main effects computed from marginal means by hand, the sums-of-squares table reproduced line by line so every number in `summary(aov())` has a known origin, and the thing that surprises people: adding a second factor that genuinely matters makes the first factor's test MORE sensitive, because the noise it was competing against has been named and removed. Balanced and unbalanced designs contrasted, with Type I against Type II sums of squares shown changing the answer on the unbalanced one. widgets: facet-grid, styled-table
3. When-an-Effect-Depends-on-Another - Interaction as a sentence before it is a term: the effect of this depends on that. Two groups crossed with two conditions, plotted four ways, with the parallel-lines case set against the crossed-lines case so the reader can see what the interaction term is measuring. The trap that follows: once an interaction is in the model, a main effect is no longer "the effect of the factor" but its effect at one reference level, which is the single most common misreading of an ANOVA table. Simple effects computed at each level as the honest alternative. widgets: facet-grid, ols-fit
4. Fixed-or-Random-Which-Factor-Is-Which - The question is not statistical, it is what you want to generalize to, and the arithmetic follows from the answer. Four specific doses you chose are fixed; twenty randomly selected clinics are random. The same dataset analysed both ways so the reader sees the standard errors and the denominator of F change, and sees that treating a random factor as fixed produces intervals that are too narrow because they only describe the clinics you happened to sample. Where this becomes the mixed model of section 9 is named, not built. widgets: cluster-icc-sim, shrinkage-pool
5. Asking-a-Specific-Question-of-Your-Groups - A planned contrast is a weighted combination of group means that encodes a question you had before the data arrived, which is why it costs less than a post-hoc search. Contrast weights written by hand for three real questions (control against the mean of three treatments, a linear trend across doses, one treatment against another), each tested, then Tukey's all-pairs comparison run on the same data so the reader can compare what the two approaches cost in power. Orthogonality explained as what makes contrasts non-overlapping questions. widgets: styled-table, null-distribution
6. Controlling-the-Family-Wise-Error-Rate - What a 5% error rate per test becomes across twenty tests, derived and then simulated so the number is a count rather than a formula. Bonferroni built in one line and shown to be exactly as conservative as advertised, Holm built as its step-down improvement and shown to reject strictly more while controlling the same quantity, and the price of both measured in missed true effects on simulated data where the truth is known. Why controlling the family-wise rate is the right goal for a confirmatory study and the wrong one for a screen. widgets: multiplicity-sim, chart-plotter
7. When-False-Discovery-Rate-Beats-Bonferroni - A different promise: not "probably no false positives anywhere" but "of the things I flagged, this fraction are wrong". Benjamini-Hochberg implemented by hand, sorting the p-values and comparing each to its own threshold, on a thousand-test screen where two hundred effects are real. Set against Bonferroni on the identical data so the reader sees BH find most of the real effects while Bonferroni finds a handful, and sees the cost, which is that some flagged findings are false and the method says so up front. When each is the honest choice. widgets: multiplicity-sim, styled-table
8. Marginal-Means-You-Can-Actually-Report - The number a reader wants is rarely a coefficient; it is a group's predicted mean with the other variables held somewhere sensible. Estimated marginal means computed by hand from the fitted model, by building the prediction grid and averaging over it, so the reader learns what "adjusted mean" means before any helper is used. Then `emmeans` as the shortcut, with its contrast and confidence-interval output read line by line. Why marginal means from an unbalanced design differ from raw group means, which is the whole point of reporting them. **Package note: `emmeans` is UNPROVEN in the browser. Load it in Pass 0; if it will not load, the hand-built grid carries the entire lesson and the package appears as named code the reader can run locally.** widgets: styled-table, facet-grid

## res-linear-models  (track: researcher; curriculum_id 4.60; landing Linear-Regression-Properly-Course.html; access: pro)  [roadmap §6]

1. What-Holding-Other-Variables-Constant-Really-Means - The phrase every multiple regression is explained with, made literal. A coefficient recovered two ways on the same data: once from the full fit, and once by regressing the outcome on the residuals of one predictor after the others, which is what "holding constant" is doing arithmetically. Then the consequence people miss: the coefficient on a variable changes when you add a correlated one, and neither value is wrong, because they answer different questions. Simpson's paradox on a small real dataset as the version that changes sign. widgets: ols-fit, correlation-heatmap
2. The-Five-Assumptions-Ranked-by-What-They-Cost - Linearity, independence, equal variance, normal residuals and no influential outliers, each violated on purpose by simulation with the damage measured in interval coverage rather than described. The ranking is the lesson: dependence is catastrophic, non-constant variance is serious for intervals and harmless for the fit, non-normal residuals are nearly free at moderate n, and the whole exercise is what makes a diagnostic plot worth reading. Why a significance test for an assumption is a poor decision rule, since it answers a question about the sample size as much as about the assumption. widgets: assumption-dial, residual-plot
3. Diagnosing-Collinearity-Without-Panicking - The variance inflation factor built from its definition, by regressing each predictor on the others and reading one over one minus R-squared, so VIF is a quantity rather than a threshold. Two correlated predictors introduced gradually, with the coefficient estimates jumping around while the fitted values and the predictions stay stable, which is exactly what collinearity does: it makes individual coefficients imprecise without making the model wrong. Why the interval already tells you this, why dropping a variable to fix VIF usually changes the question, and the cases that genuinely need action. widgets: correlation-heatmap, ols-fit
4. Robust-Standard-Errors-and-When-They-Are-Not-Enough - Heteroskedasticity simulated so the damage is a coverage count, not a worry: the fit stays unbiased, the default standard error does not. Sandwich estimators built from the formula and checked against `sandwich`, with coverage restored. Then the case that matters more and is treated as the same problem far too often: clustered data, where heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors do NOT help and cluster-robust ones do, followed by the small-number-of-clusters case where even those are optimistic. The decision rule at the end is earned by the three simulations above it. widgets: cluster-icc-sim, robust-weights
5. Weighting-Observations-That-Deserve-Less-Say - Weighted least squares as the answer when you know the variance structure instead of merely suspecting it, worked on data where each row is a group mean of known size, so the correct weights are available and the improvement over ordinary least squares is measurable. The estimator derived as minimizing a weighted sum of squares, implemented by hand, then by `lm(weights=)`. Generalized least squares introduced as the same move for correlated errors, and the honest warning: made-up weights are worse than no weights, because they shift the estimate as well as the standard error. widgets: robust-weights, ols-fit
6. The-One-Point-Carrying-Your-Regression - Leverage, residual and influence told apart, which most treatments blur. A point far out in x has leverage and may be harmless; a point with a big residual may have no influence; influence is the product, and Cook's distance is how you measure it. Built by refitting the model n times with one row dropped each time and plotting how much the coefficient moves, so the reader sees that leave-one-out influence is literally what the statistic approximates. What to do about an influential point, which is almost never to delete it silently. widgets: leverage-point, residual-plot
7. Transformations-That-Earn-Their-Keep - A log, a square root and a reciprocal applied to the same skewed outcome, with the diagnostic improvement and the interpretability cost both shown, because a transformation that fixes a residual plot and makes the coefficient unexplainable is rarely a good trade. The log-log model's coefficient read as an elasticity, the log-linear model's as a percentage change, both derived rather than asserted. Then the trap: the mean of a log is not the log of the mean, so back-transforming a prediction understates it, and the correction is shown. widgets: transform-shaper, residual-plot
8. From-Coefficients-to-Predictions-People-Understand - The last mile of a regression. A fitted model turned into the three things a reader actually wants: a predicted value with an interval, a marginal effect at a meaningful covariate setting, and a plot of the predicted outcome across the range of one predictor with the rest held fixed. The confidence interval for a mean set against the prediction interval for a new observation, with the reason the second is much wider derived from the variance it includes. Widget note: `regression-intervals` reads no cfg, so it illustrates the narrowing confidence band against the flat prediction band with its own canned data; the lesson's own numbers come from runnable R beside it. widgets: regression-intervals, pdp-curve

## res-model-strategy  (track: researcher; curriculum_id 4.70; landing Regression-Modeling-Strategies-Course.html; access: pro)  [roadmap §7]

1. Why-Stepwise-Selection-Lies-to-You - Stepwise selection run on data where every predictor is pure noise, which reliably returns a model with several significant predictors and a respectable R-squared. That demonstration is the lesson. Then why it happens, stated precisely: the p-values reported after a search are computed as though no search occurred, so they are wrong by construction, and the coefficients that survive are biased away from zero because they were selected for being large. What to do instead, which is to choose predictors on subject-matter grounds and spend the degrees of freedom you have on the ones you kept. widgets: coef-path, null-distribution
2. Fitting-a-Curve-Without-Guessing-Its-Shape - Restricted cubic splines explained as what they are: a few fixed basis columns added to an ordinary linear model, so the fit stays a `lm()` and every tool from section 6 still applies. The basis matrix printed and plotted so it stops being magic, knots placed at quantiles as the default that needs no tuning, and the restriction at the tails motivated by showing an unrestricted polynomial going wild outside the data. Three, four and five knots compared on the same relationship, with the honest finding that the choice rarely matters much and the number of knots is not the interesting decision. widgets: spline-smoother, ols-fit
3. Choosing-Interactions-Before-You-Look - The combinatorial problem stated plainly: ten predictors offer forty-five two-way interactions, and testing all of them guarantees findings. So interactions get chosen the way predictors do, from subject-matter reasoning, and the lesson walks three real cases where an interaction was predicted in advance and one where a tempting one should be refused. The degrees-of-freedom accounting made explicit, and the pre-specified-interaction test contrasted with the same test found by searching, on identical data, so the difference in what the p-value means is visible. widgets: facet-grid, coef-path
4. Paying-a-Penalty-to-Get-a-Better-Model - Ridge and lasso introduced as the same idea as the bias-variance trade from section 2, now applied to coefficients: accept some bias, buy a large reduction in variance, come out ahead on prediction error. The penalty path computed and plotted so the reader watches coefficients shrink toward zero as the penalty grows, lasso's ability to reach exactly zero contrasted with ridge's inability, and the penalty chosen by cross-validation rather than by eye. The cost named honestly: a penalized coefficient is no longer an unbiased estimate of anything, so inference on it needs care. widgets: coef-path, shrinkage-pool
5. Measuring-How-Optimistic-Your-Model-Is - The number from the data you fitted on is always too good, and this lesson measures by how much rather than warning about it. Apparent R-squared compared with a bootstrap-optimism estimate computed by hand: resample, refit, score on the original data, average the gap. Cross-validation run on the same model for comparison, with the two agreeing. Then the point that makes it matter: the optimism is large exactly when the model was selected or tuned on the same data, which ties this directly back to lesson 1. widgets: cv-folds, learning-curve
6. How-Many-Events-per-Variable-You-Can-Afford - A sample-size rule derived rather than quoted. Models fitted at descending events-per-variable ratios on simulated data with a known truth, with coefficient bias, interval coverage and calibration all degrading together, so the reader sees where a model stops being trustworthy and why the familiar rule of thumb sits where it does. Calibration introduced here as the thing that breaks first and is reported least: a model can rank cases well and still give predicted probabilities that are systematically too extreme. widgets: calibration-curve, learning-curve
7. Presenting-a-Model-So-It-Survives-Review - The write-up, as in section 3 lesson 6 but for a model rather than an estimate. One fitted model presented four ways: a coefficient table, partial-effect plots across each predictor's range, a nomogram, and a plain-language paragraph. What belongs in each, which numbers a reviewer will ask for and should already be there (the validation estimate, the calibration, the handling of missing data, the pre-specification), and the sentence to write about a predictor whose effect the data could not pin down. widgets: pdp-curve, process-flow

## res-glm  (track: researcher; curriculum_id 4.80; landing Generalized-Linear-Models-Course.html; access: pro)  [roadmap §8]

1. Logistic-Regression-from-the-Odds-Up - Built in the order that makes it stick: a probability, then odds, then log odds, each computed on the same small table so the reader can convert between them by hand before any model is fitted. Why a linear model on a zero-or-one outcome gives predictions outside zero and one, demonstrated. Then the logistic fit, with the coefficient read as a log odds ratio, exponentiated to an odds ratio, and converted to a change in probability at a stated covariate setting, which is the only one of the three a non-specialist reader will understand. widgets: logistic-curve, ols-fit
2. Three-Link-Functions-and-Why-the-Choice-Rarely-Matters - Logit, probit and complementary log-log plotted on the same axes, where logit and probit are nearly indistinguishable over the range where data usually live and cloglog is visibly asymmetric. The same dataset fitted with all three, producing near-identical predicted probabilities and quite different coefficients, which is the lesson: the link changes the scale the coefficients live on, not usually the conclusion. When cloglog is the principled choice rather than a curiosity, which is when the outcome is a time-to-event in disguise. widgets: glm-family-shapes, logistic-curve
3. Modeling-Counts-with-Poisson-Regression - Poisson regression as the natural model when the outcome is "how many", with the log link motivated by what it does rather than by convention: it makes the model multiplicative, so a coefficient is a rate ratio. Fitted on a real count outcome, with the coefficient exponentiated and read as a percentage change in the rate. The distribution's defining property restated from section 1, that variance equals mean, and the check for it computed here so lesson 4 has somewhere to start. widgets: count-dist, glm-family-shapes
4. When-Your-Counts-Are-Too-Variable-for-Poisson - Overdispersion diagnosed as a number, the ratio of residual deviance to degrees of freedom, and then shown to matter: a Poisson model on overdispersed data gives standard errors that are far too small, with the coverage measured. Three repairs compared on the same data, which are a quasi-Poisson correction, a negative binomial fit, and cluster-robust standard errors, with what each assumes made explicit. The negative binomial's extra parameter explained as a variance that grows faster than the mean, and why it usually fits count data from the real world better. widgets: wrong-family-fit, count-dist
5. Rates-Offsets-and-Unequal-Exposure - The mistake that makes count models wrong in a way nothing diagnoses: comparing counts from windows of different size. Clinics observed for different numbers of months, villages of different populations, machines run for different hours. The offset derived from the log link, so that including `log(exposure)` with its coefficient fixed at one turns a count model into a rate model, shown algebraically and then checked numerically against the same model fitted to rates directly. Why an offset is not the same as a covariate, and what goes wrong if you let its coefficient float. widgets: count-dist, styled-table
6. Outcomes-with-an-Order-and-Outcomes-Without - An ordinal outcome (a five-point severity scale) and a nominal one (which of four treatments was chosen) separated by the question each asks. The proportional-odds model built as a set of cumulative splits, with the single shared slope being exactly the assumption the name refers to, plotted so the reader can see what is being assumed and then check it. Multinomial logistic introduced as the model that assumes nothing about order and pays for it in parameters. The common error of running ordinary regression on a Likert item, with the damage shown. widgets: ordinal-cumlogit, logistic-curve
7. Too-Many-Zeros-and-the-Two-Ways-to-Model-Them - Counts with far more zeros than any Poisson or negative binomial can produce, and the two stories that explain them, which are genuinely different and lead to different models. The zero-inflated model says some zeros come from a separate never-at-risk process; the hurdle model says the decision to be non-zero and the size once non-zero are two stages. Both fitted by hand as a mixture and a two-part likelihood respectively, on data simulated from each, so the reader sees each model recover its own truth and misfit the other. Which story a given dataset supports, and how to tell. widgets: count-dist, wrong-family-fit
8. Checking-a-GLM-with-Simulated-Residuals - Why a residual plot from a GLM looks alarming even when the model is right: the residuals are not expected to be normal or homoscedastic, so the habits from section 6 mislead. Simulated quantile residuals built from first principles, by simulating many datasets from the fitted model and asking where each observed value falls in its own predicted distribution, which makes a correctly specified model's residuals uniform no matter the family. Implemented by hand, then shown catching the three faults from lessons 4, 5 and 7 on data where each was injected. **Package note: `DHARMa` is UNPROVEN and appears nowhere in the repo. The hand-built version is the lesson; name the package as the production shortcut.** widgets: residual-plot, ppc-overlay

## res-mixed  (track: researcher; curriculum_id 4.90; landing Mixed-Effects-and-Longitudinal-Models-Course.html; access: pro)  [roadmap §9]

1. Why-Grouped-Data-Breaks-Ordinary-Regression - Thirty clinics, ten patients each, and an ordinary regression that reports an interval far too narrow because it believes it has three hundred independent observations when it has closer to thirty. Simulated so the failure is a coverage count. Then the three analyses a researcher might reach for, which are ignoring the grouping, averaging within group, and adding the group as a fixed factor, each shown to answer a different question and two of them to answer the wrong one. The mixed model previewed as the one that uses both levels of information at once. widgets: cluster-icc-sim, shrinkage-pool
2. Random-Intercepts-and-Random-Slopes - The model written out before it is fitted, so the reader knows which quantities are being estimated: one intercept per group drawn from a distribution whose variance is the thing estimated, and then optionally one slope per group the same way. Fitted with `lmer()`, with the output's variance components mapped line by line onto the model just written. Random intercepts against random slopes compared on data where the effect genuinely varies by group, with the fixed-effect interval widening appropriately once that variation is modelled rather than ignored. **`lme4` is proven runnable; `glmmTMB` is not, so keep it to a named mention.** widgets: shrinkage-pool, facet-grid
3. Modeling-Change-Within-a-Person - Repeated measurements on the same subjects, where the grouping factor is the person and the interesting coefficient is a slope over time. A growth-curve model fitted with a random intercept and a random slope for time, so each subject gets their own trajectory, plotted against the population average. Why the within-subject comparison is more precise than the between-subject one, derived from the variance decomposition rather than asserted, and the trap of reading a population slope as if it described every individual. widgets: facet-grid, chart-plotter
4. Reading-Variance-Components-and-the-ICC - The numbers most people skip past, made into the answer to a question. Between-group variance and within-group variance computed by hand from group means, their ratio formed into the intraclass correlation, and that number interpreted two ways: as the correlation between two observations from the same group, and as the fraction of total variation the grouping explains. The design consequence computed directly: the effective sample size of a clustered study, and why adding more patients to the same thirty clinics stops helping. widgets: cluster-icc-sim, shrinkage-pool
5. GEE-or-Mixed-Model-Two-Different-Questions - Two methods that fit the same data and estimate different things, which is why "which is better" has no answer. A mixed model estimates a subject-specific effect; GEE estimates a population-averaged one, and for a nonlinear link those two numbers genuinely differ, which is shown numerically on the same dataset rather than described. GEE's working correlation structure explained as something that need not be right for the estimate to be consistent, which is its main attraction, and the mixed model's advantage named: it gives you the variance components lesson 4 just made useful. widgets: shrinkage-pool, styled-table
6. Correlated-Residuals-Inside-a-Group - What is left after the random effects: observations close in time within a subject are still more alike than distant ones, and a random intercept alone does not capture that. An autoregressive residual structure added, with the improvement measured, and the consequence for the fixed-effect standard error shown. Why this matters most for long series per subject and barely at all for three visits, demonstrated by varying the number of measurements, so the reader gets a rule for when to bother rather than another thing to always do. widgets: assumption-dial, chart-plotter
7. What-to-Do-When-Your-Mixed-Model-Will-Not-Converge - The most common practical failure in this section, treated as a diagnosable problem rather than bad luck. Four causes reproduced on purpose: a variance component that is genuinely zero, a random-effects structure too complex for the number of groups, predictors on wildly different scales, and near-perfect separation. Each one's warning message shown and mapped to its cause, then fixed, in that order, which is also the order to try things in. Why few studies means a meta-regression that cannot support more than one covariate. widgets: forest-plot, ols-fit
4. Reading-a-Forest-Plot-and-a-Funnel-Plot - The two standard pictures, built rather than consumed. A forest plot assembled from the components of lesson 1, one row per study with its estimate, its interval sized by precision, and the pooled diamond at the bottom, so the reader learns that the box sizes are weights. A funnel plot built as effect against precision, with the symmetric cloud that is expected under no bias, and then the same plot after small null studies are removed, which produces the characteristic gap. Each visual read for the three things it reveals and the two it cannot. widgets: forest-plot, funnel-plot
5. Detecting-What-Was-Never-Published - Publication bias treated as a missing-data problem, which connects it straight to section 15 lesson 6: the studies that are absent are absent for a reason related to their results, which is MNAR. Simulated by generating a hundred studies of a known small effect and keeping only the significant ones, then meta-analysing the survivors, which overstates the effect by a factor the lesson reports. Egger's regression and the trim-and-fill adjustment both implemented and applied, with the honest verdict that they detect asymmetry rather than bias and can be fooled by genuine heterogeneity. widgets: funnel-plot, forest-plot
6. The-Garden-of-Forking-Paths - Why a field of careful researchers, none of them cheating, produces a literature that does not replicate. The simulation that makes it undeniable: one dataset, a researcher making a handful of individually defensible choices (which outliers to exclude, which covariates to adjust for, which outcome to treat as primary), and a false-positive rate far above 5%. Then the distinction that matters, between a hypothesis specified in advance and one that crystallized while looking, and why the second kind of p-value does not mean what it says. This is the diagnosis; lessons 7 to 10 are the treatments. widgets: multiplicity-sim, spec-curve
7. Pre-Registration-and-Open-Science-in-Practice - The response to lesson 6, made concrete and unromantic. What a registration actually contains, written out as a template the reader can reuse, and what distinguishes a registration that constrains the analysis from one that is too vague to constrain anything, with a weak and a strong example of the same study side by side. Registered reports, data and code sharing, and the exploratory-against-confirmatory split within a single paper, which is the practical way to keep discovery without laundering it as confirmation. What to do, and write, when you deviate from the plan. widgets: doc-structure, review-triage
8. A-Manuscript-That-Rebuilds-Itself - Reproducibility as a property you build in rather than an audit you survive. One analysis assembled as a document that contains its own code, so every number and figure in the text is generated at render time and cannot drift from the data. Then the two problems that remain and the tools that address them: the order of operations, where a pipeline recomputes only what changed and makes the dependency graph explicit, and the package versions, where an environment lockfile records what the results were actually produced with. The failure this prevents, a result nobody can regenerate including its author, is the reason the section ends here. widgets: repro-repair, process-flow
9. Reporting-Standards-and-the-Checklist-That-Helps - The reporting checklists treated as a reviewer's questions arriving early rather than as bureaucracy. What CONSORT asks of a trial, STROBE of an observational study, and PRISMA of a review, with the items that are most often missed identified and each one traced back to the lesson in this track that produced the number it asks for. One short results section written badly and then rewritten against the checklist, so the improvement is visible rather than described. Which numbers a careful reader needs in order to reuse your work in the meta-analysis of lesson 2. widgets: doc-structure, styled-table
10. Specification-Curve-Analysis - The constructive answer to lesson 6: instead of defending one analysis, run all the defensible ones and report the distribution. A specification curve built by enumerating the reasonable combinations of choices, estimating each, sorting them, and plotting them above a matrix showing which choices produced which estimate, so the reader can see whether the result depends on one fork. Then the inference layer, which tests whether the observed curve is more extreme than curves generated under the null. The honest limit: a multiverse over choices you thought of says nothing about the one you did not. widgets: spec-curve, multiplicity-sim

# Product Data Scientist track (roadmap track `product-ds`, curriculum prefix 3)

Split out of the Researcher track 2026-11-26. Rationale, the role research
behind the arc, migration plan and verification checklist:
`Plans/product-data-scientist-track.md`.

Designed from the ROLE rather than the subject. The hiring evidence says the
experimentation-and-metric-design round is the biggest elimination point in a
product data science loop, and that weak product sense is the most-cited
rejection reason, while candidates over-prepare SQL. Sections 1 to 4 and 10
target exactly that gap.

Section 1 is free IN FULL; sections 2 onward are Pro, no tasters. That is the
standard site rule (CLAUDE.md section 6), not the researcher rule, and it is
encoded in `Scripts/batch_lessons.py` and `S(1, true, ...)` in
`www/roadmap-curriculum.js`. All three must agree.

BUILD ORDER: sections 1, 2, 3, 4 and 10 first (40 lessons). They are the two
interview rounds that eliminate candidates, they contain almost no reusable
material, and together they are sellable on their own. Sections 5 to 9 follow
as the depth upgrade and are roughly half relocations.

Relocations come from `ds-experimentation`, `ds-causal` and
`ds-causal-decisions`. Nothing moves out of Applied Statistician.
