Long-Term Effects: A Hands-On Interactive Course

A two-week experiment measures a two-week effect. Sometimes that is the answer, and sometimes it is the opposite of the answer: a change that lifts clicks this fortnight and costs retention next quarter will look like a win every single time you measure it the usual way.

The course begins by enumerating the reasons short and long-term effects differ, because they are a short and knowable list rather than a mystery, and because the direction of the gap is usually predictable once you know which reason applies.

Novelty and primacy come next, and they are treated as the pair they are: one makes the first week too good and the other makes it too bad. Both are simulated, with the test that tells you which one you are looking at.

Then the two ways to measure a long-run effect without waiting a year. A surrogate index predicts the distant outcome from near-term signals, and the course states the validity conditions and then violates them so the estimate visibly breaks. A long-term holdout simply waits, and the lesson is honest about the revenue and the goodwill that costs.

It closes on two measurement ideas product teams reach for constantly and usually get wrong. Retention is a survival curve, with censoring and a hazard, and a single day-30 number hides the shape that decides the business. And cannibalisation, where a launch moves volume rather than creating it, needs a design that can tell the two apart.

Each lesson is a guided, interactive experience: you run real R in the page, drive live simulations, answer checkpoints as you go, and write code yourself. Nothing to install.

Who this is for

Anyone whose launches are judged on a two-week readout but lived with for years: product data scientists, growth teams and anyone who owns retention. Survival and Event History Analysis goes much deeper on the time-to-event machinery this course uses for retention. You should be comfortable with a regression and a survival curve.

What you will be able to do

  • List the reasons a long-term effect differs from a short-term one, and predict the direction
  • Tell a novelty effect from a primacy effect with a test rather than a guess
  • Judge whether a surrogate metric is valid for the outcome you care about
  • Design a long-term holdout and state what it costs
  • Read retention as a survival curve rather than a single-day number
  • Design a study that separates cannibalisation from genuine growth
