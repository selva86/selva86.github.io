Counting Outcomes: Binomial and Poisson

Today let's understand two of the most common ways to count outcomes in data: the binomial and the Poisson.

Let's say you run Lindero, a small online store. Two things happen there every hour that you might want to count. At checkout, 20 customers reach the payment page, and some of them buy. In the support inbox, emails arrive through the day, with no cap on how many can land in an hour.

Both are counts. But they behave completely differently, and mixing them up leads to the wrong formula and the wrong answer.

Here is what a count distribution generally looks like: bars for what actually happened, and a line for what a model expects.

Checkout has a fixed number of chances to buy, 20 of them, every hour. The inbox has no such limit, zero emails or fifty are both possible. The first kind of count is called binomial. The second is called Poisson.

Here is what each one actually looks like for Lindero, side by side.

RInteractive R
# A first look at Lindero's two counters: checkout and the support inbox n <- 20; p <- 0.3; lambda <- 4 par(mfrow = c(1, 2)) barplot(dbinom(0:n, n, p), names.arg = 0:n, col = "steelblue", main = "Checkout: buyers per hour", xlab = "buyers out of 20", ylab = "probability") barplot(dpois(0:12, lambda), names.arg = 0:12, col = "darkorange", main = "Inbox: emails per hour", xlab = "emails", ylab = "probability")

  

  





Press Run and look at the two charts. The left one stops dead at 20, because checkout cannot produce more buyers than it had visitors. The right one keeps going past 12, because nothing in the inbox says it has to stop there. That difference, a hard stop against no stop at all, is the whole reason checkout and the inbox need two different distributions, not one.







A trial with a fixed count: the binomial setup



Start with checkout, the fixed count side of Lindero's business.



Every hour, exactly 20 customers reach the checkout page. That number is fixed before the hour even starts, it does not depend on how many of them end up buying. Each one of those 20 either buys or does not, two outcomes and nothing in between. And Lindero's historical conversion rate says each customer buys with probability 0.3, independent of what the customer before them did.



Put those three facts together and you get the four conditions a binomial distribution needs:



    


  • A fixed number of trials, n. Here n = 20.
    • 


  • Each trial has exactly two outcomes: buy, or do not buy.
    • 


  • Each trial carries the same probability of success, p. Here p = 0.3.
    • 


  • The trials are independent, so one customer's decision does not change the next customer's odds.
    • 





When all four hold, the count of successes, call it X, follows a binomial distribution, written X ~ Binomial(20, 0.3).



Plot every possible value of X at once and you get the full shape of that distribution.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Plot every possible count of buyers out of 20 checkouts, each with its probability
n <- 20
p <- 0.3
buyers <- 0:n
prob_buyers <- dbinom(buyers, n, p)
barplot(prob_buyers, names.arg = buyers, col = "steelblue",
        xlab = "number of buyers out of 20", ylab = "probability",
        main = "Binomial(20, 0.3): buyers per hour at Lindero's checkout")

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





That chart is the binomial distribution for Lindero's checkout. It peaks around 6 buyers, tapers off on both sides, and never goes past 20, because 20 is as many buyers as that hour can possibly produce.







dbinom(): the exact probability of one count



The chart you just built is really 21 separate numbers, one probability for each possible count of buyers, from 0 to 20.



R gives you each of those numbers directly. dbinom(x, n, p) returns the probability of getting exactly x successes out of n trials, each with probability p.



Ask it for the chance that exactly 6 of Lindero's 20 customers buy, and see where that bar sits on the same chart.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Highlight the exactly-6-buyers bar on the same chart, and read its probability
n <- 20
p <- 0.3
buyers <- 0:n
prob_buyers <- dbinom(buyers, n, p)
bar_colors <- ifelse(buyers == 6, "firebrick", "steelblue")
barplot(prob_buyers, names.arg = buyers, col = bar_colors,
        xlab = "number of buyers out of 20", ylab = "probability")

p_exactly_6 <- round(dbinom(6, n, p), 4)
p_exactly_6
#> [1] 0.1916

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





So there is about a 19.2% chance that exactly 6 of the 20 customers buy in a given hour. Not the single most likely count, but close to it, which is why that bar sits right near the peak.



One more thing worth checking. X can only take 21 values, 0 through 20, and one of those 21 outcomes is guaranteed to happen every hour.



Add up all 21 probabilities and confirm they total exactly 1.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Add up every possible count's probability and confirm it totals 1
total_prob <- sum(dbinom(0:n, n, p))
total_prob
#> [1] 1

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





They do. That is a useful check whenever you build a probability distribution: every value it can take, added up, has to come to 1.



You can also see the 0.1916 figure build up empirically instead of from the formula.



Simulate 20 independent checkout visits many times, each buying with probability 0.3, and watch how often 6 or more of them buy.




Run it a few thousand times and the share reaching 6 or more buyers should settle in close to 58.4%, the same number you get by adding up dbinom(6:20, n, p), every probability from 6 buyers up to 20.







The mean and variance of a binomial



Looking at the chart, the bars pile up highest somewhere around 6 buyers. Is 6 exactly right, or just close?



For a binomial distribution, the expected number of successes is the number of trials times the probability of success, n times p.



Compute that for Lindero's checkout.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compute the expected number of buyers two ways
n <- 20
p <- 0.3
buyers <- 0:n
mean_formula <- n * p
mean_formula
#> [1] 6

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Exactly 6. You can also reach that number the long way, by weighting every possible count by its own probability and adding them up, the same way you would compute a mean from any distribution.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compute the same expected count by weighting every value by its own probability
mean_weighted <- sum(buyers * dbinom(buyers, n, p))
mean_weighted
#> [1] 6

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Both routes land on 6, because n times p is just a shortcut for exactly that weighted sum.



The spread around that mean has its own formula too, the variance: n times p times (1 minus p).




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compute the variance of the number of buyers out of 20
variance <- n * p * (1 - p)
variance
#> [1] 4.2

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





So Lindero's checkout averages 6 buyers an hour, with a variance of 4.2. Those two numbers, n * p and n * p * (1 - p), are everything you need to describe a binomial's centre and spread. The Poisson, coming up next, needs something simpler still.







Quick check: binomial or not?



Before moving to the Poisson side of the inbox, check that the four binomial conditions actually hold up. Which of these describes a genuine binomial setup?









Right. All four conditions hold: n = 20 is fixed before the hour starts, every visit ends in buy or does not buy, each one carries the same 0.3 probability, and one customer's decision never changes another's. That is exactly Binomial(20, 0.3).
Not this one, and not the two before it either. The inbox count has no fixed n, which checkout always has. Pulling reviews without replacement changes the odds of a five-star review on every draw, since the folder shrinks and its mix changes, unlike checkout's constant 0.3. And asking a team that just heard the lead's opinion breaks independence, since each answer can shift because of another's, which checkout's customers never do. A binomial needs all four conditions at once: a fixed n, two outcomes per trial, one constant probability, and trials that do not influence each other. Lindero's checkout is the only one here that keeps all four.









A count with no fixed ceiling: the Poisson setup



Now turn to Lindero's support inbox.



Unlike checkout, there is no n here. No fixed number of emails was ever going to arrive this hour. Zero emails is possible, and so is fifty, there is no built-in ceiling the way checkout's 20 visitors capped the buyer count.



What Lindero does know, from history, is the average rate: 4 emails land in the inbox per hour. That one number, lambda, is all this kind of count needs. When a count has no fixed ceiling, arrives at a steady average rate, and each arrival does not depend on the others, it follows a Poisson distribution, written Y ~ Poisson(4), where Y is the number of emails in an hour and lambda = 4 is the average rate.



Plot every possible value of Y and you get its shape too.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Plot every possible count of emails in an hour, each with its probability
lambda <- 4
emails <- 0:12
prob_emails <- dpois(emails, lambda)
barplot(prob_emails, names.arg = emails, col = "darkorange",
        xlab = "number of emails in an hour", ylab = "probability",
        main = "Poisson(4): emails per hour at Lindero's support inbox")

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





That is the Poisson distribution for Lindero's inbox. It peaks around 4 emails, which makes sense since that is the average rate, and it tapers off from there. Notice it does not stop anywhere the way the binomial chart stopped at 20. This chart was only plotted up to 12 because the bars past that point get too small to see, not because 13 emails is impossible.







dpois(): the exact probability of one count, with no ceiling



Just like dbinom() gave you one exact probability out of a binomial, dpois(x, lambda) gives you one exact probability out of a Poisson.



Ask it for the chance of exactly 4 emails, right at the average.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Highlight the exactly-4 and exactly-0 bars, and read their probabilities
lambda <- 4
emails <- 0:12
prob_emails <- dpois(emails, lambda)
bar_colors <- ifelse(emails == 4, "firebrick", ifelse(emails == 0, "forestgreen", "darkorange"))
barplot(prob_emails, names.arg = emails, col = bar_colors,
        xlab = "number of emails in an hour", ylab = "probability")

p_exactly_4 <- round(dpois(4, lambda), 4)
p_exactly_4
#> [1] 0.1954

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





About 19.5%. Even though 4 is the average, it is still just one of many possible counts, so its own probability is nowhere near certain.



Now ask for the other end, a totally quiet hour with zero emails.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compute the chance of a totally quiet hour, zero emails
p_zero <- round(dpois(0, lambda), 4)
p_zero
#> [1] 0.0183

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





About 1.8%, rare but not impossible, a quiet hour like that happens roughly once every 55 hours.



Here is the difference from dbinom() that matters. dbinom(x, 20, 0.3) could never be asked about x = 21, there is no 21st customer to buy. But dpois() has no such wall: dpois(50, 4) or dpois(100, 4) both hand back a tiny but real probability. The possible counts for a Poisson run 0, 1, 2, 3, and onward forever, even as the probability keeps shrinking toward 0 the further out you go.







The mean and variance of a Poisson



Earlier you worked out the binomial's mean and variance with two separate formulas, n * p for the centre and n * p * (1 - p) for the spread. The Poisson works differently.



Its mean is just lambda. For Lindero's inbox, that is 4 emails an hour, nothing to compute, lambda already is the average.



Here is the part that is easy to miss. Its variance is also lambda, the same number, 4, describing both the centre and the spread at once.



Check both the way you checked the binomial's mean earlier, by weighting every possible count by its own probability.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compute the Poisson's mean and variance by weighting every count by its probability
lambda <- 4
mean_pois <- sum(0:30 * dpois(0:30, lambda))
mean_pois
#> [1] 4

var_pois <- sum((0:30 - lambda)^2 * dpois(0:30, lambda))
var_pois
#> [1] 4

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Both come out to 4. One number, lambda, pins down the whole distribution's centre and its spread at once. This property, where a distribution's variance always equals its mean, has a name: equidispersion, equal dispersion. It is a specific, checkable claim about the Poisson, not just a coincidence for Lindero's inbox.







Why the Poisson is the binomial's large-n, small-p limit



Here is a question worth sitting with. The binomial needs a fixed n and the Poisson does not, so how can the two possibly be related?



They are, and seeing it only takes one trick. Take Lindero's inbox, averaging 4 emails an hour, and carve that hour into 3600 one-second slots. In any given second, at most one email can plausibly land, so call each second a trial: an email arrives, or it does not.



That gives you a fixed n, 3600 trials. And if 4 emails land per hour on average, spread evenly across 3600 seconds, each second's own chance of an email is tiny: p = 4 / 3600.



That is a genuine binomial, Binomial(3600, 4/3600), built entirely out of the same inbox.



Compare its probabilities to the Poisson(4) you already built, count by count.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compare the fine-grained binomial to the Poisson across k = 0 to 8
lambda <- 4
n_big <- 3600
p_small <- lambda / n_big
k <- 0:8
binom_probs <- dbinom(k, n_big, p_small)
pois_probs <- dpois(k, lambda)

plot(k, pois_probs, type = "b", pch = 16, col = "darkorange", lwd = 2,
     xlab = "k", ylab = "probability", main = "Binomial(3600, 4/3600) vs Poisson(4)")
lines(k, binom_probs, type = "b", pch = 1, col = "steelblue", lwd = 2, lty = 2)
legend("topright", legend = c("Poisson(4)", "Binomial(3600, 4/3600)"),
       col = c("darkorange", "steelblue"), pch = c(16, 1), lty = c(1, 2))

comparison <- data.frame(
  k = k,
  binomial = round(binom_probs, 5),
  poisson  = round(pois_probs, 5)
)
print(comparison, row.names = FALSE)
#>  k binomial poisson
#>  0  0.01827 0.01832
#>  1  0.07318 0.07326
#>  2  0.14648 0.14653
#>  3  0.19542 0.19537
#>  4  0.19548 0.19537
#>  5  0.15638 0.15629
#>  6  0.10422 0.10420
#>  7  0.05952 0.05954
#>  8  0.02974 0.02977

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Look at the k = 4 row: 0.19548 for the fine-grained binomial against 0.19537 for the Poisson, a gap of only about 0.0001. Every row from 0 to 8 lines up the same way, each pair agreeing to within roughly 0.0002 of each other, and the two curves on the chart sit almost on top of each other.



That is not a coincidence, and it is not specific to 3600 seconds either. As n grows and p shrinks together, keeping their product n * p pinned at lambda, the binomial's variance, n * p * (1 - p), keeps getting closer to its mean, n * p. Once p is small enough, (1 - p) sits close enough to 1 that n * p * (1 - p) is practically the same number as n * p, and that is exactly equidispersion, variance equal to mean, the property the Poisson had back at the last step.



So the Poisson is not a separate idea bolted onto the binomial. It is what a binomial turns into once n grows large enough and p shrinks small enough that counting individual trials stops being the point, and all that is left is the average rate.







When variance stops matching the mean: overdispersion



Equidispersion, variance equal to mean, sounded like a clean rule two steps back. Real data is not always so polite.



Here are 24 real hourly counts logged from Lindero's support inbox.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Check whether Lindero's real hourly counts still look Poisson
lindero_hours <- c(2,3,4,3,5,4,3,9,10,8,6,4,3,2,3,4,5,9,10,8,4,3,2,3)
sample_mean <- mean(lindero_hours)
sample_mean
#> [1] 4.875

sample_var <- var(lindero_hours)
sample_var
#> [1] 6.983696

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The mean, 4.875 emails an hour, is close enough to the lambda = 4 used for Lindero's inbox throughout. But the variance, 6.98, is nowhere close to the mean. If these counts truly were Poisson, their variance would have to equal 4.875, the same number as their mean, exactly like you checked at the last step.



See how far off that leaves it.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Express the variance as a multiple of the mean
dispersion_ratio <- round(sample_var / sample_mean, 2)
dispersion_ratio
#> [1] 1.43

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The variance runs 1.43 times the mean. That gap has a name too: overdispersion, more spread than a Poisson allows. Lindero's actual hourly counts are noisier than the clean lambda = 4 picture suggested, probably because some hours are quiet mornings and others catch a burst of complaints all at once, rather than emails trickling in at one steady rate every hour.



This is what equidispersion really teaches you. It is not a formula to assume, it is a claim to check against your own data before trusting a Poisson model at all.



The widget below runs the same check on a different, simulated support inbox.




On the Poisson tab, the orange line is what a Poisson model expects at each count, and the grey bars are what that inbox actually produced. The bars run taller at zero and longer in the tail than the orange line predicts, the same overdispersion pattern Lindero's real 24 hours just showed you, just on a different inbox.







Practice: pick the distribution, then its variance



One more scenario to test everything so far at once.



Lindero opens a 30-minute daily live-chat window. Each day, roughly 40 chats come through that window, and Lindero logs how many get escalated to a manager, each escalating independently with probability 0.05. Which distribution does that daily escalation count follow, and does its variance have to equal its mean?








Right. n = 40 is fixed, p = 0.05 is constant, and each chat's escalation is independent of the others, so this is Binomial(40, 0.05). Its mean is 40 * 0.05 = 2 and its variance is 40 * 0.05 * 0.95 = 1.9. The two do not match, and for a binomial they are not supposed to.
Neither of those is right. A low probability per trial does not by itself make a count Poisson: the Poisson limit shown earlier needed n growing large and p shrinking toward 0 together, with n times p held fixed, and 40 trials is nowhere near that limit. This is still a fixed n = 40, constant p = 0.05 binomial, and its variance, 40 * 0.05 * 0.95 = 1.9, does not equal its mean, 40 * 0.05 = 2. Equidispersion, variance equal to mean, is a property of the Poisson, not a rule every count obeys.









Your turn: the chance of at least one email



Lindero's inbox still runs at lambda = 4 emails an hour. What is the chance that at least one email arrives in a given hour? You already have the piece you need from a few steps back: dpois(0, lambda), the chance of a totally quiet hour.



"At least one" is everything except a quiet hour, so subtract that quiet-hour chance from 1.




Right: about 0.9817. In an inbox averaging 4 emails an hour, a totally quiet hour is rare, so at least one email is close to a sure thing.Write the quiet-hour probability from a few steps back, subtracted from 1: 1 - dpois(0, lambda).
Show answer
# At least one email arrives whenever "zero emails" does not happen
p_at_least_one <- round(1 - dpois(0, lambda), 4)
p_at_least_one
#> [1] 0.9817




So about 98.2% of hours see at least one email. Quiet hours are the rare exception, not the rule, matching the dpois(0, lambda) = 0.0183 chance found earlier.

Reading a count before you model it



Look back at everything Lindero's two counters produced today.



Checkout gave you a binomial: a fixed n, a constant p, and two separate formulas, n * p for the mean and n * p * (1 - p) for the variance, that never have to agree with each other. The support inbox gave you a Poisson: no fixed ceiling, one number lambda covering both the mean and the variance, a property called equidispersion. And splitting that same inbox into 3600 one-second slots showed the Poisson is not a separate idea at all, it is what a binomial becomes once n grows large and p shrinks small enough that n * p settles on a steady rate.



Two tests carry everything you need going forward. First, decide whether a count is binomial or Poisson by asking whether it has a fixed n and a constant p, or an open-ended count at one steady rate instead. Second, once you suspect a Poisson, check its equidispersion against real data rather than assuming it, the way Lindero's real 24 hours came back overdispersed, a variance of 6.98 against a mean of 4.875.



Both dbinom() and dpois() hand back an exact probability for one count, in one line, no table in the back of a book required. That is the habit worth keeping: before reaching for either formula, ask what kind of count you are actually looking at.
