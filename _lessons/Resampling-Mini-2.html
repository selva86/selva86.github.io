Bootstrap confidence intervals: for any statistic

Today let's understand how to put a confidence interval around any statistic, even one that has no formula for it.

Two hundred household incomes sit in front of you, and from them you compute one number: the median. On its own, that number doesn't say how far off it could be. What you want next to it is a range.

For a mean, that range comes from a formula, one line of algebra. Reach for the same thing around a median and there's no formula to reach for. Nobody derived one, because a median doesn't move from sample to sample the way a mean does.

The bootstrap gets you the range anyway, without any formula. Let your 200 incomes stand in for the households you can't go out and resurvey. Draw 200 incomes again from that stand-in population, one at a time, letting a household turn up twice or get skipped entirely. Recompute the median on that draw. Do that thousands of times over, and the medians collect into a range you can read straight off: the middle 95% of them is your interval.

That's the whole recipe. Everything from here on is running those four steps for real, starting with the income numbers themselves.

Why there's no formula for a median's confidence interval

A confidence interval for a mean has a known recipe: take the standard error of the mean, multiply it by a critical value from the normal distribution, and add and subtract that from the sample mean. That recipe exists because statisticians worked out, on paper, exactly how a sample mean behaves from sample to sample.

No equivalent result exists for a median, or for a trimmed mean, a correlation, or a 90th percentile. Each one behaves differently from sample to sample, and deriving a fresh formula for every one of them is not realistic.

Here are 200 simulated household incomes, right-skewed the way real income data usually is: most households cluster in the middle, a handful earn a lot more, and that stretches out the top of the distribution. Press Run to build the sample and look at its size and its median.

set.seed(2026) fixes the random number generator, so running this gives you the exact same 200 incomes shown here, every time.



The sample median is $51,966.50. That is a fact about these particular 200 households. What's still missing is a range around it: how far the true median income, the one you would get from surveying every household, might plausibly sit from this number. Building that range from these same 200 incomes comes next.







The plug-in principle: treat your sample as the population



Here's the idea that makes the whole bootstrap work.



You would love to draw a second sample of 200 households from the real population and see how much its median differs from your first sample's median. Do that a few thousand times and you would know exactly how much a median wobbles from sample to sample, which is precisely what a confidence interval needs. The catch: you only got to survey once. There is no real population sitting there for you to sample from again.



So the bootstrap substitutes something you do have: your own 200 incomes. It treats that sample as if it were the population, and instead of a fresh sample from reality, it draws a fresh sample from your data. This is called the plug-in principle: since the real population is unobserved, the sample plugs in for it.



For that substitute sample to behave like a real second survey, it has to be drawn WITH replacement: every time you draw a household, you note it down and put it back, so it stays eligible to be drawn again. Sample the same 200 income values WITHOUT replacement and you would just get the same 200 numbers back in a different order, no new information at all. Draw one resample this way and compare its median to the original.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Draw one resample of 200, with replacement, and compare its median to the original
set.seed(5)
resample1 <- sample(income, size = 200, replace = TRUE)

median(resample1)
#> [1] 51773.5

median(income)
#> [1] 51966.5

length(unique(resample1))
#> [1] 132

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





sample(income, size = 200, replace = TRUE) draws 200 values from the 200 incomes, one at a time, putting each one back before the next draw. Only 132 of the 200 incomes turn up in this resample, so the other 68 slots are repeats: some households were drawn two, three, or more times, which is exactly why some original households don't appear in the resample at all.



Notice that this one resample already gives a different median, $51,773.50 against the original's $51,966.50. That difference isn't a mistake. It's a real, honest measurement of how much the median can move when the 200 households you happened to survey change slightly, which is exactly the wobble a confidence interval needs to capture.







What one resample with replacement looks like



That earlier resample of 200 incomes is hard to see all at once, 200 numbers scrolling past. So here is the exact same operation on a small, visible scale: 12 households instead of 200, resampled with replacement, so you can watch which ones repeat and which ones drop out.




Every household has the same chance of being drawn on each pick, so over many resamples, roughly a third of them end up skipped entirely, shown in grey above. That isn't a flaw. It's exactly what "with replacement" means: a household that has already been drawn goes straight back in, free to be drawn again, which is the only way some households can repeat while others get left out.







Quick check: what resampling with replacement actually does








Right. The resample always matches the original size; replacement is what lets a household repeat, and lets another one be skipped.
Skipping some households does not shrink the resample. It is always drawn to the same size as the original, 200 here; the skipped slots are simply filled by households drawn more than once.









From one resample to a confidence interval



One resample gave one median. To see how much the median actually wobbles, repeat the resample-and-recompute step thousands of times and collect every result.



Do that 2,000 times and you get 2,000 medians, one from each resample. That collection of 2,000 numbers is called the bootstrap distribution of the median. It is your best available estimate of how the median would vary if you really could survey the population again and again.



The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of that collection, the values with 2.5% of the 2,000 medians below them and 2.5% above them, mark off the middle 95% of it. That middle 95% is the 95% percentile bootstrap confidence interval. Here is that recipe as code.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Resample 2,000 times, recompute the median each time, and read off the middle 95%
set.seed(11)
boot_medians <- replicate(2000, {
  resample <- sample(income, size = 200, replace = TRUE)
  median(resample)
})

ci_median <- quantile(boot_medians, probs = c(0.025, 0.975))
ci_median
#>  2.5% 97.5% 
#> 47271 56067 

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





replicate(2000, {...}) runs the code inside the braces 2,000 times and collects every result into boot_medians. Each run draws its own fresh resample and computes its own median, exactly like the single resample from earlier, just 2,000 times over. quantile() then finds the two values that bound the middle 95% of those 2,000 numbers.



So the 95% confidence interval for the median household income is $47,271 to $56,067. Nothing about this recipe was specific to the median. Swap median() for mean(), var(), or a 90th percentile, and the same four lines, resample, recompute, repeat, read the percentiles, give you a confidence interval for that statistic instead. That is the point: one recipe for every statistic.







Is the bootstrap distribution symmetric, or skewed?



Reading off the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles works best when the bootstrap distribution is roughly centred on the sample statistic and shaped the same on both sides. Not every statistic's bootstrap distribution looks that way. Build one more bootstrap distribution from the same 200 incomes, this time recomputing the variance instead of the median on each resample.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Resample 2,000 times and recompute the variance each time, on the same income data
set.seed(11)
boot_var <- replicate(2000, var(sample(income, size = 200, replace = TRUE)))

round(range(boot_var))
#> [1]  430543402 1077086228

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





boot_var is built exactly the same way as boot_medians: resample, recompute, repeat 2,000 times. Only the function inside replicate() changed, from median() to var().



Here is the full distribution of the 2,000 bootstrap medians. Toggle it between a histogram and a boxplot.




And here is the full distribution of the 2,000 bootstrap variances, the same two views.




The medians come out close to symmetric: one hump, with roughly as much spread on either side of the middle. The variances come out right-skewed: most of the 2,000 values sit in a tight cluster, but a long tail of unusually large bootstrap variances stretches the distribution out on the high side. A percentile interval that just reads off the 2.5% and 97.5% points assumes that first, symmetric shape. The second shape is exactly where the percentile interval becomes unreliable.







Where the percentile interval falls short



The percentile interval, built by reading off the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles directly, assumes the bootstrap distribution is centred on the sample statistic and roughly symmetric. The variance's bootstrap distribution was not: it came out right-skewed.



For a skewed or biased bootstrap distribution, statisticians reach for a different interval called BCa, short for bias-corrected and accelerated. Instead of always reading the 2.5% and 97.5% points, BCa adjusts which percentiles it reads based on two things measured from the bootstrap distribution itself: how far its centre sits from the original sample statistic, called the bias, and how much the statistic's spread changes as the statistic itself changes, called the acceleration.



When neither correction is needed, BCa reads the same percentiles as the plain method. When the distribution looks like the variance's histogram, it reads different ones, and lands closer to the truth. In R, this is boot::boot.ci(type = "bca"), one line once you already have the bootstrap replicates.



BCa fixes a lot, but not everything. Here is a limit no correction can remove. Take just the 10 smallest incomes in the sample and bootstrap their maximum.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Bootstrap the maximum of the 10 smallest incomes: can the interval ever exceed the sample?
small_incomes <- income[1:10]
small_incomes
#>  [1] 65727 31989 55362 50054 38523 16760 37354 32858 54726 42015

max(small_incomes)
#> [1] 65727

set.seed(31)
boot_max <- replicate(2000, max(sample(small_incomes, size = 10, replace = TRUE)))
ci_max <- quantile(boot_max, probs = c(0.025, 0.975))
ci_max
#>  2.5% 97.5% 
#> 50054 65727 

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Look at the upper bound: $65,727. That is the exact observed maximum of small_incomes, not close to it, equal to it. Every resample here is drawn from those same 10 incomes, so no resample, and no maximum of a resample, can ever be bigger than the largest value already sitting in the sample.



BCa cannot fix this, because the problem isn't the shape of the bootstrap distribution, it's that the bootstrap can never produce a value that isn't already in the sample. Whenever your statistic is a minimum, a maximum, or another boundary value, keep that limit in mind no matter which interval method you reach for.







Practice: pulling the percentile method together









Right. Skew or bias in the bootstrap distribution is exactly the situation the percentile method assumes away and BCa corrects for.
It is not about likelihood. No resample, drawn from the original 10 values, can ever contain a number bigger than what is already there, no matter how the draws land. BCa cannot fix this either: it corrects for skew and bias in the bootstrap distribution, not for values the sample never contained. And resampling without replacement at the same size just returns the original 200 values in a new order, so its median never changes and there is no spread to build an interval from.

You can now bootstrap a confidence interval for any statistic



You now have one recipe that works for a median, a variance, a percentile, or anything else you can compute from a sample: resample with replacement at the same size as your data, recompute the statistic, repeat it 2,000 times, and read the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles off the result.



You also know its two limits. When the bootstrap distribution comes out skewed or biased, like the variance's did, switch from the plain percentile interval to BCa, which adjusts which percentiles it reads instead of always using 2.5% and 97.5%. And when your statistic is a minimum, a maximum, or another value bounded by what is already in the sample, remember that no resample can ever go beyond it, no matter which interval method computes it.



The next time a formula does not exist for the number you need to report, you already know what to do: resample, recompute, repeat, and read the percentiles.
