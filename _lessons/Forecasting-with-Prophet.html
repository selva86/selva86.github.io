How Prophet fits trend, seasonality and holidays

Today let's understand Prophet, a forecasting model that splits a series into a trend, a repeating season and a set of holiday effects, fits each one separately, and adds them back together.

Here is the running example. Victoria, the Australian state, records its electricity grid demand every half hour. Add those readings up into a daily total and take 120 consecutive days, November 1, 2012 through February 28, 2013, real numbers from the tsibbledata package. Over those 120 days demand averaged 220.0 GWh a day, as low as 161.1 GWh on its quietest day and as high as 294.5 GWh on its busiest.

Here is the whole series, one point for each day.

Notice three things in that line. It drifts a little higher through the back half of the window than the front. It rises and falls on a roughly weekly rhythm. And a handful of days drop sharply below their neighbours, for no reason a calendar-free eye could name.

The three components Prophet adds together

Prophet writes demand on day \(t\) as one sum of three parts plus whatever is left over:

\[ y(t) = \text{trend}(t) + \text{seasonality}(t) + \text{holidays}(t) + \varepsilon(t) \]

trend(t) is the slow drift: whether demand is generally climbing or falling as the months pass. seasonality(t) is what an ordinary week looks like, the repeating rise and fall from Monday through Sunday. holidays(t) is which specific calendar dates break that ordinary pattern, a public holiday for instance. \(\varepsilon(t)\) is the error, everything on that day the other three do not explain.

These four pieces add together, they never multiply. So a big holiday effect on a low-trend day still just subtracts from whatever trend and season already put there, it does not scale them up or down.

Before fitting anything, ground trend and swing in the series' own numbers.

RInteractive R
# Build the running example: 120 days of real Victorian electricity demand library(tsibble) library(tsibbledata) library(dplyr) library(lubridate) daily <- tsibbledata::vic_elec |> index_by(Date = as_date(Time)) |> summarise(Demand = sum(Demand) / 1000, Holiday = any(Holiday), .groups = "drop") |> mutate(Demand = round(Demand, 1)) d <- daily |> filter(Date >= as_date("2012-11-01"), Date <= as_date("2013-02-28")) # See the series' own average level and how far it typically swings round(mean(d$Demand), 1) round(sd(d$Demand), 1) #> [1] 220 #> [1] 28.6

  

  





220 GWh a day is the level trend(t) drifts around. 28.6 GWh is roughly how far a typical day sits from that level, once you mix in every week's rise and fall together with every holiday's drop. trend(t), seasonality(t) and holidays(t) each pull one piece of that 28.6 GWh swing away from the rest, starting with trend(t).







The piecewise-linear trend and what a changepoint is



trend(t) is not one straight line for the whole 120 days. Prophet builds it out of straight segments joined end to end, and each joint is called a changepoint: a point where the slope is allowed, not required, to change.



By default Prophet places up to 25 candidate changepoints spread across the first 80% of the history it is given, day 1 through day 96 here. Most of them end up doing nothing at all, their slope change comes out at zero, and the trend only actually bends at the few that do.



A Prophet fit on these 120 days shows exactly that. Here is its trend column, plotted day by day.




Framed as the trend a local Prophet fit produced, since Prophet does not run on this page: this trend climbs from 209.0 GWh on day 1 to 214.4 on day 31 (December 1) to 218.5 on day 54 (December 24), a gentle rise of about 0.18 GWh a day. Past day 54 it climbs faster, 222.5 by day 69 (January 8), 224.7 by day 76 (January 15), and 238.9 by day 120 (February 28), roughly 0.31 GWh a day from there on, nearly double the earlier slope. That change in steepness around Christmas is a changepoint doing real work.







changepoint_prior_scale: how much bend the trend is allowed



Every one of the 25 candidate changepoints gets its own slope-change number, called delta. Prophet does not estimate these deltas freely. It puts a prior on them, a Laplace distribution centred at zero:



\[ \delta_j \sim \text{Laplace}(0, \tau) \]



\(\tau\) is changepoint_prior_scale, and it is the width of that Laplace distribution. A narrow width pulls almost every delta back to exactly zero, so the trend stays close to one straight line. A wide width lets deltas wander further from zero, so more changepoints end up bending the trend.



A local fit at the default \(\tau = 0.05\) leaves 5 of the 25 deltas above 0.001 in size: 0.0073 on December 24, 0.0236 on December 28, 0.0190 on January 1, 0.0046 on January 5 and 0.0026 on January 8. That cluster of five is exactly the stretch where the last step's chart bent faster. Push \(\tau\) down to 0.001 and none of the 25 move at all, a perfectly straight line. Push it up to 0.5 and 8 of the 25 move, one of them by as much as 0.474, a visibly wiggly trend.



The widget below does not run on Victoria's demand. It fits its own generic scatter, with a slider that sets how flexible the fit is allowed to be, but the trade-off it shows, stiff to a good fit to a wiggly overfit, is the same one changepoint_prior_scale controls on the trend above.




Somewhere between 0.001 and 0.5 sits the changepoint_prior_scale that lets the trend follow a real bend, like the one at Christmas, without also chasing noise in a week that has none.







Quick check: what a bigger changepoint_prior_scale does



At 0.05, 5 of the 25 changepoints move. At 0.5, 8 of them move, one by as much as 0.474. Raise changepoint_prior_scale from 0.05 toward 0.5: what actually changes?









Right. Going from 0.05 to 0.5 took the count of changepoints with a real slope change from 5 of 25 to 8 of 25, and the largest single delta grew from 0.0236 to 0.474.
changepoint_prior_scale sets the width of the Laplace prior on each slope change, nothing else. It never adds changepoints (that count is fixed at 25 by n.changepoints) and it never changes how far back Prophet looks for them (that window is fixed at the first 80% of the history by changepoint_range). A wider prior lets existing deltas move further from zero, in either direction, which bends the trend more, not less.









Weekly seasonality as a Fourier series



seasonality(t) has to represent one repeating shape, an ordinary week, using numbers a model can estimate. Prophet does this the same way fable's fourier() does: a sine and cosine pair for each harmonic, added together to trace the week's rise and fall.



See that weekly rhythm directly, by averaging demand for each day of the week across the whole 120 days.

That is seasonality(t) doing its job: explaining the week-to-week wobble already visible back in the cover chart.







Holidays as their own additive component



holidays(t) exists because a public holiday is not explained by trend or by an ordinary weekday pattern. It is a specific date, and demand drops on it for a reason a calendar carries, not a reason the week's rhythm carries.



Five real Victorian public holidays fall inside these 120 days: Melbourne Cup Day (November 6), Christmas Day (December 25), Boxing Day (December 26), New Year's Day (January 1) and Australia Day observed (January 28). Mark them apart from the other 115 days on the same series.




All 5 marked days sit near the bottom of the whole cloud of points, well under the 220.0 GWh mean: 190.5, 161.1, 169.5, 175.9 and 191.2 GWh. A local Prophet fit assigns a single holiday effect of -46.93 GWh to every one of them, on top of whatever trend(t) and seasonality(t) that date already carries. That one number is holidays(t): the same subtraction, applied only on days the calendar names.







Specifying, fitting and reading a Prophet model in R



Fitting Prophet means handing it two tables: the series itself, and a table of the dates to treat as holidays. Here is the call, framed to run locally, since Prophet needs a Stan toolchain this page does not have.



# Fit Prophet on the 120-day series with the 5 real holiday dates supplied
library(prophet)

df <- data.frame(ds = d$Date, y = d$Demand)

holidays_df <- data.frame(
  holiday = "public_holiday",
  ds = as.Date(c("2012-11-06", "2012-12-25", "2012-12-26", "2013-01-01", "2013-01-28")),
  lower_window = 0,
  upper_window = 0
)

m <- prophet(df, holidays = holidays_df)
fcst <- predict(m, df)
prophet_plot_components(m, fcst)



df needs exactly two columns, ds for the date and y for the series, Prophet's own naming convention. holidays_df lists each holiday's date under ds, with lower_window and upper_window both at 0, meaning the effect applies only to that exact day, not the days around it. Prophet fits by finding the maximum a posteriori estimate, a single best-fitting set of numbers found by numerical search, rather than the full likelihood an ARIMA fit works out.



prophet_plot_components() draws three stacked panels, trend, weekly and holidays, and every number in them is one you have already seen in this lesson.




Read the table top to bottom and the whole additive model is right there: trend sets the baseline, weekly nudges it up or down by day of week, and holidays subtracts a further 46.93 GWh on exactly the 5 days that need it.

Where Prophet wins, where the fable toolkit wins



A coefficient table is not a verdict on accuracy. To compare Prophet against this fable model honestly, refit both on an earlier stretch of the data and score them on days neither one was allowed to see.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Hold out the last 14 days and refit on the 106 days before them
train <- d |> filter(Date <= as_date("2013-02-14"))
test <- d |> filter(Date > as_date("2013-02-14"))

fit_train <- train |>
  model(fit = TSLM(Demand ~ trend(knots = as_date("2012-12-28")) + fourier(period = 7, K = 3) + Holiday))
fc <- forecast(fit_train, new_data = test)

# Root mean squared error of the 14-day forecast against the real demand
rmse <- sqrt(mean((fc$.mean - test$Demand)^2))
round(rmse, 2)
#> [1] 27.57

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





106 days train the model, the 14 days from February 15 to 28 test it, and none of the 5 real holidays fall inside that window. On this fold the fable piecewise model scores an RMSE of 27.57 GWh. A local Prophet refit on the same 106 training days, scored on the same 14 test days, comes back at 29.65 GWh, close, and slightly in fable's favour here.



That single number does not settle which tool is generally better, only which one won this one fold. Prophet's real advantages sit elsewhere: a holiday list handed in as plain dates instead of hand-built dummy columns, an automatic search over 25 candidate changepoints instead of one knot chosen by eye, and an interface built for someone who has never fit an ARIMA model. The fable toolkit's advantages are just as real: a transparent coefficient table you can read line by line, no external Stan toolchain to install, and one consistent model() interface shared by every model fable fits.







Reading the holdout comparison honestly



fable scored 27.57 GWh on the 14-day holdout, Prophet scored 29.65 GWh. Should that be read as fable generally beating Prophet on this kind of series?









Right. The test window runs February 15 to 28, and every real holiday in this example, November 6 through January 28, falls before it. A single 14-day fold with no holidays in it says nothing about how either model handles the one component this lesson spent three steps on.
A single 14-day holdout that happens to contain no holidays cannot settle a general verdict, especially when holidays are the one component this lesson spent three steps on and this window tests neither model on it. Widen the window, or score several holdout folds instead of one, before trusting a 2.08 GWh gap this far.

What you can do now



You can now state Prophet's additive model, \(y(t) = \text{trend}(t) + \text{seasonality}(t) + \text{holidays}(t) + \varepsilon(t)\), and name what each of its three parts explains.



You can explain changepoint_prior_scale as the width of a Laplace prior on each changepoint's slope change: narrow keeps the trend close to a straight line, wide lets it bend more freely, and the numbers on this page, 5 of 25 changepoints moving at the default versus 8 of 25 at a wider setting, show exactly what that width buys.



And you can weigh Prophet against a fable model the honest way, on a real held-out number, 27.57 GWh against 29.65 GWh here, rather than a rule of thumb, while knowing exactly what a single 14-day fold can and cannot tell you.
