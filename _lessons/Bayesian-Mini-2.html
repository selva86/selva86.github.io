Choosing priors: the decision that matters

Today let's understand one of the most contested calls in Bayesian work: setting the prior, the number you would commit to for an unknown quantity before you have touched any data at all.

Here's the scene. A coin gets flipped 5 times and lands heads all 5 times. In the same week, a colleague's brand-new trading strategy wins its first 5 trades in a row.

Both results are identical on paper, 5 out of 5. But you would not react to them the same way. Decades of evidence about ordinary coins already sit behind the coin, so five heads barely dents what you expect from it. The trading strategy carries no such track record, so the same five wins leave you a lot less certain.

That difference in reaction is not a bias to correct. It is exactly what a prior is for, and Bayesian statistics gives you a precise way to write it down and use it.

So here is what we will do. We will give the coin and the strategy two different priors that match what you actually knew about each of them, run the same 5-out-of-5 data through both, and watch the two posteriors land in different places even though the data was identical.

That is the general shape of what is about to happen: a prior meets data and becomes a posterior. Watch it for a second, then let's build the coin's and the strategy's own priors properly.

A prior is a probability distribution over what you don't yet know

Let's make "prior" precise before we do anything else with it.

In both of our examples, the unknown quantity is the same: how often the coin lands heads, or how often the strategy wins, in the long run. Call that unknown number p. It sits somewhere between 0 and 1.

A prior is not a single guess at p. It is a full probability distribution over every value p could take, saying how plausible each one is before you have seen any data.

For a number that lives between 0 and 1, like a win rate, the standard distribution to use is the Beta distribution, written Beta(a, b). The two numbers a and b control its shape: together they decide where the distribution centres and how tightly it concentrates around that centre.

The coin's prior is Beta(50, 50). The strategy's prior is Beta(3, 3). Both centre on 0.5, an even chance, because neither the coin nor the strategy has a reason to be biased one way or the other going in. What differs is how concentrated each prior is about that 0.5.

Let's plot both and see the difference directly.

RInteractive R
# Plot the coin's and the strategy's priors as densities over the win rate p coin_prior_a <- 50 coin_prior_b <- 50 strategy_prior_a <- 3 strategy_prior_b <- 3 p_grid <- seq(0, 1, length.out = 200) coin_density <- dbeta(p_grid, coin_prior_a, coin_prior_b) strategy_density <- dbeta(p_grid, strategy_prior_a, strategy_prior_b) plot(p_grid, coin_density, type = "l", col = "darkgreen", lwd = 2, xlab = "win rate p", ylab = "density", ylim = c(0, max(coin_density))) lines(p_grid, strategy_density, col = "steelblue", lwd = 2) legend("topright", legend = c("coin prior Beta(50,50)", "strategy prior Beta(3,3)"), col = c("darkgreen", "steelblue"), lwd = 2, bty = "n")

  

  





Look at the two curves. The coin's prior, in green, is a tall narrow spike sitting right on 0.5. The strategy's prior, in blue, is a low wide hill that still centres on 0.5 but spreads its belief much further out, from around 0.2 to 0.8.



Both priors say "probably close to even odds." But the coin's prior says it with far more certainty than the strategy's prior does. That difference in certainty, not the centre, is what is going to matter once real data shows up.







Same data, two posteriors: watching the update happen



Now let's actually run data through both priors and see what "updating a prior" means in practice.



Both the coin and the strategy gave you the same result this week: 5 successes out of 5 trials. For the coin that is 5 heads out of 5 flips. For the strategy that is 5 winning trades out of 5.



Updating a Beta prior with binomial data (a fixed number of trials, each one a success or a failure) turns out to be remarkably simple. You just add the counts to the prior's own two numbers: posterior = Beta(prior_a + successes, prior_b + failures).



That's the whole rule. No integrals, no simulation required, because the Beta distribution and the binomial data fit together exactly like that.



Let's apply it to both priors on the same 5-out-of-5 data.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Update both priors with the same 5-successes-out-of-5 data
coin_data <- list(successes = 5, trials = 5)
strategy_data <- list(successes = 5, trials = 5)

coin_post_a <- coin_prior_a + coin_data$successes
coin_post_b <- coin_prior_b + (coin_data$trials - coin_data$successes)
strategy_post_a <- strategy_prior_a + strategy_data$successes
strategy_post_b <- strategy_prior_b + (strategy_data$trials - strategy_data$successes)

coin_post_mean <- coin_post_a / (coin_post_a + coin_post_b)
strategy_post_mean <- strategy_post_a / (strategy_post_a + strategy_post_b)
round(c(coin_posterior_mean = coin_post_mean, strategy_posterior_mean = strategy_post_mean), 3)
#>     coin_posterior_mean strategy_posterior_mean 
#>                   0.524                   0.727 

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Read those two numbers side by side. The coin's posterior mean is 0.524, barely different from the 0.5 it started at. Five heads in a row moved it almost nowhere.



The strategy's posterior mean is 0.727, a big jump from its own starting point of 0.5. The exact same data, 5 out of 5, pulled it much further.



Nothing about the data was different. What was different is how concentrated each prior was before the data arrived. The coin's Beta(50, 50) is the equivalent of already carrying about 100 coin flips worth of evidence, so 5 more flips barely move it. The strategy's Beta(3, 3) is the equivalent of carrying only about 6 trades worth of evidence, so 5 new trades are a large share of all the evidence it carries, and it swings hard.



Key Insight
A prior's a and b add up to roughly how many pseudo-trials, pretend observations baked in before any real data arrives, it is worth. A big a+b, like the coin's 100, makes a prior hard to move. A small a+b, like the strategy's 6, makes it easy to move. That single number, not the shape of the curve, predicts how far new data will pull a posterior.



The widget below shows the same mechanic in general form: a prior meets data and becomes a posterior, and how far it moves depends on how concentrated the prior was to start with. It does not carry the coin's or the strategy's exact numbers, but you can reproduce the same pattern on it by hand.




Drag the "prior confidence" slider until the prior curve looks tight, like the coin's, and watch how little the posterior moves under the data. Then drag it the other way until the prior curve looks wide, like the strategy's, and watch the posterior chase the data instead.







Quick check: why did the coin barely move?



Both the coin and the strategy saw the exact same data: 5 out of 5. Yet the coin's posterior mean barely moved to 0.524, while the strategy's posterior mean jumped to 0.727.









Exactly. The coin's Beta(50,50) carries the weight of about 100 pretend observations, so 5 real ones barely register. The strategy's Beta(3,3) carries the weight of about 6, so the same 5 observations are a much bigger share of the total evidence.
Look back at the numbers again: the coin's posterior mean, 0.524, sits far closer to its own prior's 0.5 than to the strategy's posterior of 0.727. If posteriors ignored the prior, both would have landed in the same place, since the data was identical. What actually decided the gap was how concentrated each prior was before the data arrived, not the data itself.









The three kinds of priors: flat, weakly informative, informative



You have already met two different priors, the coin's tight Beta(50,50) and the strategy's looser Beta(3,3). There is a third kind worth naming, and together the three cover almost every prior you will ever choose.



A flat prior, also called a reference prior, is Beta(1, 1). It says every value of p between 0 and 1 is equally likely, with no value preferred over any other. This is the closest you can get to saying "I have no opinion."



A weakly informative prior rules out the extremes without committing to a specific value. The strategy's Beta(3, 3) is one: it says a win rate near 0.5 is somewhat more likely than one near 0 or 1, but it stays loose enough that a handful of real trades can move it a long way.



An informative prior encodes a real, specific reason to be confident. The coin's Beta(50, 50) is one: decades of evidence that ordinary coins are close to fair is baked directly into how tight that curve is.



Let's plot all three together and see how the shapes differ.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compare a flat, a weakly informative, and an informative prior
flat_prior_a <- 1
flat_prior_b <- 1

flat_density <- dbeta(p_grid, flat_prior_a, flat_prior_b)
informative_density <- dbeta(p_grid, coin_prior_a, coin_prior_b)
weak_density <- dbeta(p_grid, strategy_prior_a, strategy_prior_b)

plot(p_grid, flat_density, type = "l", col = "gray40", lwd = 2,
     xlab = "win rate p", ylab = "density", ylim = c(0, max(informative_density)))
lines(p_grid, weak_density, col = "steelblue", lwd = 2)
lines(p_grid, informative_density, col = "darkgreen", lwd = 2)
legend("topright", legend = c("flat Beta(1,1)", "weakly informative Beta(3,3)", "informative Beta(50,50)"),
       col = c("gray40", "steelblue", "darkgreen"), lwd = 2, bty = "n")

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The flat prior, in gray, is a perfectly straight line: every p equally plausible. The weakly informative prior, in blue, is a gentle hill around 0.5. The informative prior, in green, is the tall narrow spike you already know.



Three shapes, three amounts of prior confidence. Choosing between them is choosing how much you are willing to claim you already knew before the data showed up.







Why a flat prior is not the same as no prior



It is tempting to reach for the flat prior whenever you are unsure, treating it as the "neutral" or "safe" option. Run the numbers and you will see that is not quite right.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compare posterior mean and spread under three priors, same 5/5 data
prior_table <- data.frame(
  prior = c("flat Beta(1,1)", "weakly informative Beta(3,3)", "informative Beta(50,50)"),
  post_a = c(flat_prior_a, strategy_prior_a, coin_prior_a) + coin_data$successes,
  post_b = c(flat_prior_b, strategy_prior_b, coin_prior_b) + (coin_data$trials - coin_data$successes)
)
prior_table$post_mean <- round(prior_table$post_a / (prior_table$post_a + prior_table$post_b), 3)
prior_table$post_sd <- round(sqrt(prior_table$post_a * prior_table$post_b /
  ((prior_table$post_a + prior_table$post_b)^2 * (prior_table$post_a + prior_table$post_b + 1))), 3)
prior_table[, c("prior", "post_mean", "post_sd")]
#>                          prior post_mean post_sd
#> 1               flat Beta(1,1)     0.857   0.124
#> 2 weakly informative Beta(3,3)     0.727   0.129
#> 3      informative Beta(50,50)     0.524   0.049

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Look at the flat prior's row. After the same 5-out-of-5 data, its posterior mean is 0.857, the highest of the three. A prior built to encode no information produced the single most extreme-looking conclusion.



That happens because a flat prior is not neutral about the outcome, only about the parameter. Beta(1, 1) treats a win rate of 0.99 as exactly as plausible as a win rate of 0.50 before any data arrives. Feed it 5 straight successes and nothing else, and since the prior does not down-weight extreme values, the posterior moves most of the way to "always wins."



Warning
A flat prior is a strong claim in disguise. It says every win rate is equally plausible, including the ones you would never actually bet on. That is a specific, checkable assumption, not the absence of one.



The coin's informative Beta(50,50), by contrast, has real information behind it, decades of ordinary coins being close to fair, and its posterior mean of 0.524 reflects that restraint. Whether a flat prior is the right choice depends on whether "every value is equally plausible" is actually true for your problem. For most real questions, it is not.







Checking what a prior implies before you see any data



Here is a genuinely useful trick: you can check whether a prior makes sense before you ever look at real data.



The idea is called a prior predictive check. Draw a plausible win rate from the prior, then simulate what data that win rate would produce, and see whether the simulated data looks reasonable. If the prior predicts outcomes you would call absurd, the prior itself needs fixing, no data required.



Let's ask a concrete question: under each prior, how likely is 5 straight successes out of 5, purely from the prior's own beliefs about p, before any coin was actually flipped? For a Beta(a, b) prior, there is an exact formula for that.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Exact probability of 5 straight successes, before seeing any data, under each prior
prior_predictive_5_of_5 <- function(a, b) {
  exp(lchoose(5, 5) + lbeta(5 + a, 0 + b) - lbeta(a, b))
}
flat_pp <- prior_predictive_5_of_5(flat_prior_a, flat_prior_b)
coin_pp <- prior_predictive_5_of_5(coin_prior_a, coin_prior_b)
round(c(flat_prior_predictive = flat_pp, coin_prior_predictive = coin_pp), 4)
#> flat_prior_predictive coin_prior_predictive 
#>                0.1667                0.0344 

# The same quantity for a truly fixed, perfectly fair coin, for comparison
0.5^5
#> [1] 0.03125

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





In that formula, lchoose(5, 5) counts the one way to arrange 5 successes out of 5 trials, and lbeta(...) folds in how the prior's own Beta shape weights that outcome. Both run on the log scale so the numbers do not shrink to nothing, and exp() converts the result back to an ordinary probability at the end.



Under the flat prior, 5 straight successes has a 16.67% chance, exactly 1 in 6, before any data is seen. Under the coin's prior, it drops to 3.44%, about 1 in 29.



That coin number is worth pausing on. A truly fixed, perfectly fair coin gives 0.03125, exactly 3.125%, about 1 in 32, for 5 heads in a row, that's what 0.5 raised to the 5th power computes to above. The coin's Beta(50, 50) prior gives 3.44%, close to that fixed-fair-coin number but not identical to it, because the prior still allows the coin to be very slightly biased one way or the other. That closeness is a good sign: it means the prior is behaving the way a sensible belief about an ordinary coin should.



You do not have to trust the formula either. You can check it by simulation: draw a win rate from the prior, flip that many virtual coins, and count how often you land on 5 out of 5.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Simulate the same probability by drawing p from each prior, then flipping 5 times
set.seed(2026)
p_from_flat <- rbeta(2000, flat_prior_a, flat_prior_b)
p_from_coin <- rbeta(2000, coin_prior_a, coin_prior_b)
sim_flat <- rbinom(2000, 5, p_from_flat)
sim_coin <- rbinom(2000, 5, p_from_coin)
round(c(simulated_flat_share = mean(sim_flat == 5), simulated_coin_share = mean(sim_coin == 5)), 4)
#> simulated_flat_share simulated_coin_share 
#>               0.1745               0.0455 

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Over 2,000 simulated draws, the flat prior produced 5 straight successes 17.45% of the time, close to the exact 16.67%. The coin's prior produced it 4.55% of the time, in the same ballpark as the exact 3.44%. The gap between the simulated and exact numbers is just noise from using a finite number of draws; run more draws and it shrinks further.



Key Insight
A prior predictive check catches a bad prior before you ever touch real data. If the coin's prior had instead predicted something like a 40% chance of 5 straight heads, that would be your signal that the prior does not describe an ordinary coin at all, and you would fix it right there.







Turning real domain knowledge into an informative prior



So far the coin's informative prior, Beta(50, 50), was handed to you ready-made. Here is how you would actually build one yourself from a real piece of domain knowledge.



Suppose a colleague who has watched dozens of similar trading strategies tells you: "strategies like this typically win 55% to 60% of their trades." That is real information, and you can turn it into a Beta prior in two steps.



First, decide the centre. The middle of 55% to 60% is 57.5%, so you want a prior whose mean is 0.575.



Second, decide how much that belief is worth, in the same "pseudo-trials" units you already saw earlier. A colleague's general impression from watching several strategies is a moderate amount of confidence, worth pretending you had already seen about 40 trades' worth of evidence.



Let's turn that into numbers and plot the resulting prior.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Turn "typically wins 55-60% of trades" into a Beta prior
believed_mean <- 0.575
believed_confidence <- 40  # pseudo-trials of confidence behind that belief

domain_prior_a <- believed_mean * believed_confidence
domain_prior_b <- believed_confidence - domain_prior_a
c(domain_prior_a = domain_prior_a, domain_prior_b = domain_prior_b)
#> domain_prior_a domain_prior_b 
#>             23             17 

domain_density <- dbeta(p_grid, domain_prior_a, domain_prior_b)
plot(p_grid, domain_density, type = "l", col = "darkorange", lwd = 2,
     xlab = "win rate p", ylab = "density")

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





That gives Beta(23, 17). Multiplying the mean by the pseudo-trial count gives a, the pretend successes; the rest of the pseudo-trials go to b, the pretend failures.



The curve sits centred on 0.575 with a moderate spread: tighter than the strategy's original Beta(3,3), because a colleague's word is worth more than nothing, but far looser than the coin's Beta(50, 50), because it is still just one person's impression, not decades of evidence. That is the whole recipe: pick a plausible centre, decide how many pseudo-trials that belief is worth, and let the arithmetic set a and b.







How much data it takes to override a prior



Every example so far used just 5 trials. What happens as the sample grows?



Let's compare a flat prior, Beta(1, 1), against a moderately informative prior, Beta(10, 10), on a growing sample: the coin's own 5-of-5 result, then a 50-trial batch at 33 wins (66%), then a 500-trial batch at 290 wins (58%).




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# How posteriors from a flat and a Beta(10,10) prior converge as the sample grows
mid_data <- list(successes = 33, trials = 50)
large_data <- list(successes = 290, trials = 500)
widget2_informative_prior_a <- 10
widget2_informative_prior_b <- 10

posterior_mean <- function(prior_a, prior_b, successes, trials) {
  post_a <- prior_a + successes
  post_b <- prior_b + (trials - successes)
  post_a / (post_a + post_b)
}

convergence_table <- data.frame(
  n = c(5, 50, 500),
  flat_posterior_mean = round(c(
    posterior_mean(flat_prior_a, flat_prior_b, coin_data$successes, coin_data$trials),
    posterior_mean(flat_prior_a, flat_prior_b, mid_data$successes, mid_data$trials),
    posterior_mean(flat_prior_a, flat_prior_b, large_data$successes, large_data$trials)
  ), 3),
  informative_posterior_mean = round(c(
    posterior_mean(widget2_informative_prior_a, widget2_informative_prior_b, coin_data$successes, coin_data$trials),
    posterior_mean(widget2_informative_prior_a, widget2_informative_prior_b, mid_data$successes, mid_data$trials),
    posterior_mean(widget2_informative_prior_a, widget2_informative_prior_b, large_data$successes, large_data$trials)
  ), 3)
)
convergence_table
#>     n flat_posterior_mean informative_posterior_mean
#> 1   5               0.857                      0.600
#> 2  50               0.654                      0.614
#> 3 500               0.580                      0.577

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





At 5 trials, the two posteriors are far apart: 0.857 for the flat prior against 0.600 for the informative one, a gap of 0.257. At 50 trials they are much closer, 0.654 against 0.614, a gap of only 0.040. By 500 trials they nearly agree, 0.580 against 0.577, a gap of just 0.003.



That is convergence. No matter how different two priors start out, enough real data eventually overwhelms both of them, because the data's contribution to the posterior keeps growing while each prior's contribution stays fixed at whatever pseudo-trial count it was worth.



This is the same mechanic the widget showed you earlier, now stretched out over a growing sample instead of a fixed one. It does not carry these exact win-rate numbers, but you can rebuild the same pattern on it: hold the prior confidence slider fixed, and drag "data points n" from small to large.




Watch the posterior curve as n climbs. Early on it sits close to the prior. By the time n is large, it has pulled almost entirely onto the data regardless of where the prior started, exactly like the flat and informative posteriors above closing in on each other by 500 trials.







Checking your prior didn't drive the conclusion: a sensitivity sweep



You now have an update rule, and a prior predictive check to catch a bad prior before fitting. There is a third check worth running after you fit, on the real data: a sensitivity sweep.



The idea is simple. Refit the same data under several different, individually defensible priors, and see whether your actual conclusion survives. If it does, your finding is not just an artifact of the one prior you happened to pick.



Take the strategy's real 50-trial record: 33 wins. Sweep it through four priors, from flat to strongly informative.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Sweep four priors across one 50-trial dataset and check the conclusion survives
sweep_priors <- list(c(1, 1), c(3, 3), c(10, 10), c(50, 50))

sweep_results <- data.frame(prior = character(), post_mean = numeric(), interval_width = numeric())
for (pr in sweep_priors) {
  post_a <- pr[1] + mid_data$successes
  post_b <- pr[2] + (mid_data$trials - mid_data$successes)
  ci <- qbeta(c(0.025, 0.975), post_a, post_b)
  sweep_results <- rbind(sweep_results, data.frame(
    prior = paste0("Beta(", pr[1], ",", pr[2], ")"),
    post_mean = round(post_a / (post_a + post_b), 3),
    interval_width = round(ci[2] - ci[1], 3)
  ))
}
sweep_results
#>         prior post_mean interval_width
#> 1   Beta(1,1)     0.654          0.255
#> 2   Beta(3,3)     0.643          0.248
#> 3 Beta(10,10)     0.614          0.226
#> 4 Beta(50,50)     0.553          0.158

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The posterior mean does move as the prior gets stronger, from 0.654 down to 0.553. That is expected; a stronger prior always pulls harder. But look at what does not move: every single one of the four posterior means sits above 0.5.



That is the real question a sensitivity sweep answers. Not "does the exact number change" (it always does, at least a little), but "does the conclusion I actually care about survive." Here, the conclusion is "the strategy beats even odds," and it holds under the flattest prior and under the most concentrated one alike.



Tip
Make a sensitivity sweep a habit, not something you reach for only when someone challenges your prior. A short loop over 3 or 4 priors at the end of any Bayesian analysis tells you whether your headline conclusion is standing on real data or leaning on one prior you happened to choose.



Had even one of those four posterior means dropped below 0.5, you would have a genuinely different story to tell: a conclusion that only holds under some priors is not one you can defend without also defending your prior choice.







Quick check: how do you defend a prior choice?



You now have two different checks on a prior, from two different points in the workflow. The prior predictive check runs before you ever touch real data: it catches a prior that implies something absurd. The sensitivity sweep runs after you fit, on the real 50-trial data: it confirmed the strategy-beats-even-odds conclusion held under all four priors, from flat to strongly informative.









Exactly right, and notice the two checks sit on opposite sides of the data: one guards you before you fit, the other confirms you after.
Each check catches a different failure. A prior predictive check alone would never have shown you that the strategy's conclusion held across four different priors, and a sensitivity sweep alone would never have caught a prior implying something as absurd as a 40% chance of 5 straight heads from an ordinary coin. Run both, at the two points in the workflow where each one belongs.









Your turn: choose and defend a prior for a new coin



Here's a new situation to work through yourself. A coin from a novelty shop known for selling biased coins, typically biased around 0.7 toward one side, is flipped 8 times and lands on that marked side 6 times.



Build two priors and compare the posterior mean each one gives.



The first is a flat prior, Beta(1, 1), the same "I don't know anything" starting point you used earlier. The second is an informative prior built from what you actually know about this shop: its coins are typically biased around 0.7. A Beta(7, 3) prior has a mean of 7 / 10, exactly 0.7, so it is a reasonable way to encode that.



Update both priors with the 6-out-of-8 data, using the same rule from earlier: posterior = Beta(prior_a + successes, prior_b + failures). Then compute both posterior means and compare them.




Both posteriors computed and compared: the flat prior lands at 0.700, the informative prior at 0.722. They come out almost exactly the same, because 8 flips is not much data, so the informative prior's built-in information about the shop is doing most of the work either way.Update each prior the same way you did earlier: add the successes (6) to a and the failures (2) to b, for both flat_prior_tryit_a/b and informative_prior_tryit_a/b, then divide each posterior's a by a+b to get its mean.
Show answer
# A new coin: 8 flips, 6 land on the marked side. Build and compare two priors.
tryit_data <- list(successes = 6, trials = 8)

flat_prior_tryit_a <- 1
flat_prior_tryit_b <- 1
flat_tryit_post_a <- flat_prior_tryit_a + tryit_data$successes
flat_tryit_post_b <- flat_prior_tryit_b + (tryit_data$trials - tryit_data$successes)
flat_tryit_mean <- flat_tryit_post_a / (flat_tryit_post_a + flat_tryit_post_b)

informative_prior_tryit_a <- 7
informative_prior_tryit_b <- 3
informative_tryit_post_a <- informative_prior_tryit_a + tryit_data$successes
informative_tryit_post_b <- informative_prior_tryit_b + (tryit_data$trials - tryit_data$successes)
informative_tryit_mean <- informative_tryit_post_a / (informative_tryit_post_a + informative_tryit_post_b)

round(c(flat_posterior_mean = flat_tryit_mean, informative_posterior_mean = informative_tryit_mean), 3)
#>        flat_posterior_mean informative_posterior_mean 
#>                      0.700                       0.722 




Both priors land close together, 0.700 and 0.722. That is not a coincidence: 8 flips is a small dataset, so most of each posterior still comes from its prior, and both priors already placed more probability on the coin leaning that side. Where they would disagree sharply is if the flat prior had instead started from "no reason to expect bias" on a much smaller dataset still, exactly the gap you saw between the coin and the strategy earlier.

Choosing a defensible prior, from now on



Here is the decision rule you now have, in one place.



Match the prior to what you actually knew before the data arrived. A coin with a long history of being fair earns a tight, informative prior. A brand-new trading strategy with no track record earns a loose, weakly informative one. Reaching for a flat prior by default is not the safe choice, it is its own strong claim, and it can distort a small dataset badly.



Before you fit anything, run a prior predictive check. Simulate what your prior alone predicts, with no data involved, and make sure it does not imply something you would call absurd.



After you fit, run a sensitivity sweep across a handful of defensible priors. If your real conclusion, not just the exact number, survives every one of them, you have evidence the data is doing the work and not your prior.



Do all three, and the next time someone asks why you picked that prior, you will have an answer better than "it felt right."
