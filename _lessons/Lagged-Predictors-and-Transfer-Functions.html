Lagged predictors, transfer functions, and forecasting the predictor first

Today let's understand what to do when a predictor's effect on your outcome does not land all at once, but keeps showing up for several periods afterward.

Here's the running example. BJsales is a real sales series, 150 consecutive periods, alongside BJsales.lead, a leading indicator recorded at those same 150 periods. Both come from Box and Jenkins' 1976 time series text and ship inside base R. Over the 150 periods, sales ranged from 198.6 to 263.3, averaging 230.0.

Here's the whole sales series, one point for each period.

It isn't a straight climb. Sales start near 200, climb into the low 220s within the first thirty periods, dip back down near 210 around the midpoint, then climb again through the second half to finish near 263.

The leading indicator, and how closely it already tracks sales

A leading indicator is a series recorded alongside your target whose past values may carry information about the target's future. BJsales.lead is exactly that: it is recorded at the same 150 periods as sales, ranging from 9.75 to 13.87 and averaging 11.85.

Plot sales against the leading indicator recorded at the very same period, and the relationship is already easy to see.

The points climb from lower left to upper right, and the correlation shown above the chart, r = 0.951, says the same thing in one number: periods where the indicator sat higher are periods where sales sat higher too, at that very same period. That is already a strong relationship. But a same-period correlation is not the whole story, and that is worth checking directly, next.

Which lag fits best? Reading a cross-correlation table

To check whether the indicator's effect on sales lands only in the same period, or keeps showing up afterward, compute the correlation between sales at period t and the indicator at period t minus k, for k running from 0 up to 6 periods back.

k (periods back) correlation
0 0.951
1 0.960
2 0.967
3 0.982
4 0.983
5 0.983
6 0.981

The correlation does not peak at k = 0. It climbs sharply through k = 3, sits above 0.98 all the way through k = 5, then eases only slightly at k = 6. So the indicator recorded three periods ago lines up with today's sales even more tightly than today's own reading of the indicator does.

See that directly. Plot sales against the indicator from three periods back instead of the same period.

The points sit tighter around a line than they did in the same-period scatter two steps back, matching the jump from r = 0.951 to r = 0.982. So the indicator's effect on sales is not confined to one period; it spreads across several. A model that only uses today's indicator reading, and throws away the last several periods of it, is throwing away real signal.

Quick check: what the rising correlation means

The correlation between sales and the indicator kept climbing well past lag 0, reaching 0.982 by lag 3 and staying above 0.98 through lag 5. What does that pattern actually tell you?

Right. A same-period correlation of 0.951 is already strong, but its climb to 0.982 by lag 3 shows the indicator's influence spreads across several periods rather than landing all at once in the same one.
A correlation of 1 would mean a perfect straight-line relationship, which real data almost never shows. 0.982 is close to that ceiling and counts as a strong relationship, not a weak one.

Distributed lag models: give every recent lag its own coefficient

Since the indicator's effect spreads over several periods, the right model does not pick one best lag and discard the rest. It gives several recent lags their own coefficient, and adds them all in at once. That is a distributed lag model.

\[ \text{sales}_t = \beta_0\,\text{lead}_t + \beta_1\,\text{lead}_{t-1} + \beta_2\,\text{lead}_{t-2} + \cdots + \beta_k\,\text{lead}_{t-k} + \eta_t \]

Sales at period t is a weighted sum of the indicator at period t and at each of the last k periods, each with its own coefficient \(\beta\), plus a leftover error \(\eta_t\). That leftover error is not assumed independent; it is left free to follow its own \(\text{ARIMA}(p, d, q)\) process.

fable fits this the same way, ARIMA(), with the extra lag terms written straight into the formula using lag(). Build the tsibble from BJsales and BJsales.lead, hold out the last 6 of the 150 periods as a test set, and fit the smallest distributed lag model, one lag term beyond the same-period one, on the remaining 144.

RInteractive R
# Build the tsibble from BJsales and BJsales.lead, split it into a 144-period training set and the last 6 periods as a test set, and fit a distributed lag model with one lag term beyond lead itself library(tsibble) library(fable) library(fabletools) library(dplyr) d <- tsibble(period = 1:150, sales = as.numeric(BJsales), lead = as.numeric(BJsales.lead), index = period) train <- d |> filter(period <= 144) test <- d |> filter(period > 144) nrow(train) #> [1] 144 nrow(test) #> [1] 6 m1 <- train |> model(m1 = ARIMA(sales ~ lead + lag(lead, 1) + pdq(d = 0))) report(m1) #> Series: sales #> Model: LM w/ ARIMA(0,0,5) errors #> #> Coefficients: #> ma1 ma2 ma3 ma4 ma5 lead lag(lead, 1) intercept #> 2.0454 1.9824 1.4445 1.0046 0.3843 4.5562 6.4547 99.3046 #> s.e. 0.1006 0.2300 0.2319 0.1842 0.0933 0.4830 0.5664 11.8123 #> #> sigma^2 estimated as 4.105: log likelihood=-302.84 #> AIC=623.68 AICc=625.02 BIC=650.4

  

  





pdq(d = 0) tells fable's search that the leftover error itself needs no differencing. Read the two coefficients that belong to the predictors: lead's own coefficient is 4.5562, and lag(lead, 1), the indicator one period back, adds another 6.4547 on top. Both lags matter, and lag 1 actually carries more weight than lag 0 does.



The AICc for this one-lag model, Akaike's corrected information criterion, a fit score where a lower number means a better balance of fit and simplicity, comes out at 625.02. Is one lag term enough, or does adding more keep helping? That's next.







Choosing the lag length with AICc



AICc is what lets you compare specifications with different numbers of predictors fairly. It scores a model on how well it fits, then subtracts a penalty for every extra parameter, so adding more lag terms does not automatically win; a lag term only lowers AICc if it earns its keep.



One rule has to hold for that comparison to stay valid. The differencing order d inside pdq() has to stay the same across every specification you compare. A once-differenced series measures period-to-period changes rather than raw levels, so AICc values computed at different values of d sit on different scales and are not comparable. Fix d at 0 for every specification in this search, the same way the one-lag model above did.



Add lag columns for the indicator one through six periods back, then refit the same distributed lag model with k = 0 through 6 of those lag terms included, each time holding d at 0.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
library(dplyr)
# Add lag columns 1 through 6 for the indicator, then refit the distributed lag model with k = 0 through 6 lag terms, holding d at 0 every time, and collect each specification's AICc
for (j in 1:6) train[[paste0("lag", j)]] <- dplyr::lag(train$lead, j)

aicc_by_k <- numeric(7)
aicc_by_k[1] <- glance(train |> model(ARIMA(sales ~ lead + pdq(d = 0))))$AICc
aicc_by_k[2] <- glance(train |> model(ARIMA(sales ~ lead + lag1 + pdq(d = 0))))$AICc
aicc_by_k[3] <- glance(train |> model(ARIMA(sales ~ lead + lag1 + lag2 + pdq(d = 0))))$AICc
aicc_by_k[4] <- glance(train |> model(ARIMA(sales ~ lead + lag1 + lag2 + lag3 + pdq(d = 0))))$AICc
aicc_by_k[5] <- glance(train |> model(ARIMA(sales ~ lead + lag1 + lag2 + lag3 + lag4 + pdq(d = 0))))$AICc
aicc_by_k[6] <- glance(train |> model(ARIMA(sales ~ lead + lag1 + lag2 + lag3 + lag4 + lag5 + pdq(d = 0))))$AICc
aicc_by_k[7] <- glance(train |> model(ARIMA(sales ~ lead + lag1 + lag2 + lag3 + lag4 + lag5 + lag6 + pdq(d = 0))))$AICc

round(aicc_by_k, 2)
#> [1] 868.47 625.02 321.12 490.07 356.88 164.80 133.23

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





See that same table read as a report-ready table.




Two things stand out. AICc does not fall smoothly: it jumps back up at k = 3 (490.07, higher than k = 2's 321.12) before falling again, so a lag term earning its keep is checked, not assumed, at every step. And the lowest AICc in the table sits at k = 6, the largest lag depth tested here. That is a boundary chosen for this lesson, not a law: there is no reason to expect this indicator's effect on this series to reach back further than six periods, so the search stopped there. With a different series, testing k = 7 or beyond would be the next reasonable step.



Take the k = 6 model forward. Its coefficients are next.







The winning model's shape: a delay, then a decay



Fit the k = 6 model, the one with the lowest AICc, and read its seven coefficients: one for lead itself, and one for each of lag(lead, 1) through lag(lead, 6).




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Fit the k = 6 distributed lag model, the lowest AICc from the table above, and read its coefficients on lead and its six lags
m6 <- train |> model(m6 = ARIMA(sales ~ lead + lag1 + lag2 + lag3 + lag4 + lag5 + lag6 + pdq(d = 0)))

co <- coef(m6) |> filter(term %in% c("lead", "lag1", "lag2", "lag3", "lag4", "lag5", "lag6"))
data.frame(lag = 0:6, coefficient = round(co$estimate, 3))
#>   lag coefficient
#> 1   0      -0.195
#> 2   1      -0.283
#> 3   2      -0.361
#> 4   3       4.207
#> 5   4       2.756
#> 6   5       1.419
#> 7   6       0.688

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





See the same shape in a chart.




Read that shape left to right. Lag 0 and lag 1 are both small and even slightly negative, meaning the indicator's own same-period and one-period-back reading contribute almost nothing once the other lags are in the model. Then lag 3 jumps to 4.207, by far the largest coefficient of the seven. After that jump, each coefficient is smaller than the one right before it: 2.756, then 1.419, then 0.688.



Put a number on how fast that fall happens. Divide each coefficient from lag 4 onward by the one right before it.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compute how much each coefficient shrinks from the one right before it, from lag 4 onward
lag3 <- co$estimate[co$term == "lag3"]
lag4 <- co$estimate[co$term == "lag4"]
lag5 <- co$estimate[co$term == "lag5"]
lag6 <- co$estimate[co$term == "lag6"]

round(c(lag4 / lag3, lag5 / lag4, lag6 / lag5), 3)
#> [1] 0.655 0.515 0.484

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Each ratio lands close to one half: 0.655, then 0.515, then 0.484. So from lag 3 onward, the indicator's effect roughly halves every period. That whole shape, a delay before the effect shows up followed by a roughly steady decay, is exactly what Box and Jenkins named a transfer function when they introduced it using this same dataset. Instead of reporting seven separate coefficients, a transfer function packs that shape into two numbers: how many periods the effect is delayed, and how quickly it decays once it starts. Fitting one directly needs a specialized routine outside fable's toolkit, but you have already found its shape by hand, in the coefficients and decay ratios above.







Forecasting the predictor before forecasting with it



Now try to forecast sales for the 6 held-out periods, 145 through 150, using the k = 6 model. There is a catch. That model's formula includes lead at lag 0, so forecasting sales at period 145 needs the indicator's own value at period 145. But period 145 sits right after the end of the training window, so that value has not been observed within it.



A forecast built only from information available at the moment you are forecasting from is called an ex-ante forecast, the honest kind, the one you could actually have produced in real time. A forecast that instead uses the real, already-known future values of a predictor, useful only for checking a model's shape after the fact, is an ex-post forecast. To produce a genuine ex-ante forecast for sales, you first need an ex-ante forecast for the indicator itself.



Fit an ordinary ARIMA model to the indicator series on its own, no predictors this time, and forecast it 6 periods ahead.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Fit an ARIMA model to the leading indicator on its own, and forecast it 6 periods ahead
mlead <- train |> model(mlead = ARIMA(lead))
report(mlead)
#> Series: lead 
#> Model: ARIMA(0,1,1) w/ drift 
#> 
#> Coefficients:
#>           ma1  constant
#>       -0.4724    0.0244

#> 
#> sigma^2 estimated as 0.08066:  log likelihood=-22.02
#> AIC=50.04   AICc=50.21   BIC=58.93

flead <- forecast(mlead, h = 6)
data.frame(period = flead$period, lead_forecast = round(flead$.mean, 3))
#>   period lead_forecast
#> 1    145        13.551
#> 2    146        13.576
#> 3    147        13.600
#> 4    148        13.625
#> 5    149        13.649
#> 6    150        13.673

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





ARIMA(0,1,1) with drift found its own shape for the indicator: one difference to remove its gentle upward drift, one moving-average term, and a small constant drift of 0.0244 added back on top of that. Its forecast climbs gently and steadily, from 13.551 at period 145 to 13.673 at period 150, which is exactly what a drifting-upward series should do when projected forward with nothing else known.







Plugging the forecasted indicator into the sales model



With a forecast for the indicator in hand, build the future predictor table the k = 6 sales model actually needs: lead at each of the 6 future periods, plus its lags 1 through 6 at each of those same periods.



Here is the part worth sitting with. Lag 1 at period 145 is the indicator's real, observed value from period 144, so that part is solid. But lag 1 at period 150 is the indicator's forecasted value from period 149, not an observed one. As the future table reaches further out, more and more of what feeds the sales forecast is itself forecasted rather than observed, so whatever uncertainty sits in flead compounds into the sales forecast too, and grows with the horizon.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Build the future predictor table for periods 145-150: the indicator's forecast at each period, plus its own lags 1 through 6 built from the combined observed-and-forecasted indicator values
lead_forecast <- flead$.mean
lead_history  <- c(train$lead, lead_forecast)
n_train <- nrow(train)

future <- tsibble(period = 145:150, index = period)
future$lead <- lead_forecast
future$lag1 <- lead_history[(n_train):(n_train + 5)]
future$lag2 <- lead_history[(n_train - 1):(n_train + 4)]
future$lag3 <- lead_history[(n_train - 2):(n_train + 3)]
future$lag4 <- lead_history[(n_train - 3):(n_train + 2)]
future$lag5 <- lead_history[(n_train - 4):(n_train + 1)]
future$lag6 <- lead_history[(n_train - 5):(n_train)]

fc <- forecast(m6, new_data = future)
data.frame(period = 145:150, forecast = round(fc$.mean, 2), actual = test$sales)
#>   period forecast actual
#> 1    145   263.18  262.9
#> 2    146   263.04  263.3
#> 3    147   262.19  262.8
#> 4    148   262.38  261.8
#> 5    149   262.63  262.2
#> 6    150   263.04  262.7

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Score that forecast, and compare it against the simplest possible benchmark, a naive forecast that just repeats the last training value, 261.1, for all 6 periods.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Score the distributed lag forecast, and compare it against a naive forecast that repeats the last training value for all 6 periods
mae  <- mean(abs(fc$.mean - test$sales))
rmse <- sqrt(mean((fc$.mean - test$sales)^2))
round(c(MAE = mae, RMSE = rmse), 3)
#>   MAE  RMSE 
#> 0.415 0.438 

naive_pred <- rep(train$sales[nrow(train)], 6)
mae_naive  <- mean(abs(naive_pred - test$sales))
rmse_naive <- sqrt(mean((naive_pred - test$sales)^2))
round(c(MAE = mae_naive, RMSE = rmse_naive), 3)
#>   MAE  RMSE 
#> 1.517 1.593 

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





0.415 against 1.517, and 0.438 against 1.593. The distributed lag model misses by about 3.6 times less than a forecast that ignores the indicator completely, on both measures. All that work, the cross-correlation table, the AICc search, forecasting the indicator before the outcome, earns a real, measurable improvement over the simplest possible guess.







Quick check: why forecast the indicator first



The k = 6 sales model includes lead at lag 0, so forecasting sales at any of the 6 held-out periods needs the indicator's value at that same period. Why does that force you to forecast the indicator first, instead of just forecasting sales directly?









Right. lead is a predictor in the sales model, and a predictor needs a value supplied for every period you want a forecast for. Since periods 145 through 150 have not happened, that value has to come from an ex-ante forecast of the indicator itself.
The sales model only uses whatever indicator values you hand it; it does not generate new ones on its own. Since periods 145 to 150 have not happened, those values have to come from somewhere else, an ex-ante forecast of the indicator, which is exactly what fitting ARIMA(lead) and forecasting it did two steps back.
