Fitting and forecasting a seasonal ARIMA model

Today let's understand seasonal ARIMA models, or SARIMA: how to add a seasonal part on top of the ARIMA you already know, and use it to fit and forecast a series with a real yearly pattern.

The running example is the monthly turnover of cafes, restaurants and takeaway food services in Victoria, Australia, measured in millions of Australian dollars, from the tsibbledata package's aus_retail dataset. It runs 441 months, April 1982 to December 2018. Every December, Victorians spend noticeably more eating out than in any other month, and that repeating shape is exactly what an ordinary ARIMA(p, d, q) has no way to reach.

Here is the whole series, plotted month by month.

That climbing line carries a spike every twelve months, some years wider than others, but always there. This lesson builds a model that expects that spike, instead of being surprised by it every December.

The seasonal signature in the ACF, at lags 12, 24 and 36

Building the seasonal part of a model starts with seeing the season clearly first. Build the running example, and split it the way the whole lesson will use it: a training window to fit on, and a held-out test window to check a forecast against later.

RInteractive R
# Build the Victoria cafes, restaurants and takeaway turnover series, and split into train and test library(tsibbledata) library(tsibble) library(dplyr) library(feasts) retail <- tsibbledata::aus_retail |> filter(State == "Victoria", Industry == "Cafes, restaurants and takeaway food services") |> select(Month, Turnover) nrow(retail) #> [1] 441 train <- retail |> filter(Month <= yearmonth("2016 Dec")) test <- retail |> filter(Month > yearmonth("2016 Dec")) nrow(train) #> [1] 417 nrow(test) #> [1] 24

  

  





train holds 417 months, April 1982 through December 2016. test holds the last 24 months, January 2017 through December 2018, real turnover figures set aside so a fitted model can be checked later against numbers it never saw.



The tool for reading a repeating pattern like this one is the ACF, short for autocorrelation function. Its value at a given lag is the correlation between the series and its own value that many months earlier. A value near 1 at some lag means the series looks almost the same shape that many months apart.



Compute the ACF of the training series up to lag 36, three full years, and read off the values at exactly 12, 24 and 36 months.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compute the ACF of the training series up to lag 36
acf_raw <- train |> ACF(Turnover, lag_max = 36)
round(acf_raw$acf[c(12, 24, 36)], 3)
#> [1] 0.904 0.817 0.731

round(acf_raw$acf, 3)
#>  [1] 0.981 0.969 0.962 0.954 0.944 0.932 0.930 0.924 0.916 0.907 0.904 0.904
#> [13] 0.887 0.876 0.870 0.864 0.855 0.844 0.842 0.837 0.830 0.821 0.818 0.817
#> [25] 0.802 0.792 0.786 0.779 0.770 0.759 0.757 0.751 0.743 0.734 0.731 0.731

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Every one of the 36 values is high, since a series with a strong trend correlates with itself at almost any lag. But look specifically at 12, 24 and 36: 0.904, 0.817 and 0.731, decaying only slowly, and each one landing at an exact multiple of 12. That is the seasonal signature, a series that keeps echoing itself at the period and every whole multiple of it, long after nearby lags would normally have faded.



See the shape of it as a bar chart, with the three seasonal lags marked apart from the rest.




An ordinary ARIMA(p, d, q) has no way to produce a bar this tall out at lag 36. Its AR and MA terms only reach back p or q lags, and nobody sets p or q to 36 just to explain one distant echo. Reaching lag 12, 24 and 36 on purpose, without needing 36 ordinary terms to do it, is exactly what the seasonal part of a SARIMA model is built for.







What the seasonal difference removes



The usual fix for a series that will not sit still is differencing: subtract each value from an earlier one, and see what is left. Take an ordinary difference first, lag 1, and then a seasonal difference on top of it, lag 12, and look at the ACF again.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Take an ordinary difference, then a seasonal difference at lag 12
train_diff <- train |>
  mutate(dTurnover = difference(Turnover)) |>
  mutate(dsTurnover = difference(dTurnover, lag = 12))

acf_diff <- train_diff |> ACF(dsTurnover, lag_max = 36)
round(acf_diff$acf[c(1, 2, 12, 24, 36)], 3)
#> [1] -0.219 -0.104 -0.398 -0.046 -0.051

round(acf_diff$acf, 3)
#>  [1] -0.219 -0.104  0.031  0.054  0.017 -0.004 -0.069 -0.009 -0.023  0.052
#> [11]  0.115 -0.398  0.030  0.052  0.008 -0.094  0.031 -0.046  0.033 -0.014
#> [21]  0.047 -0.067  0.077 -0.046 -0.028  0.066  0.029 -0.015 -0.065  0.072
#> [31]  0.096  0.015 -0.032  0.013 -0.032 -0.051

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Look at lag 24 and lag 36 first: -0.046 and -0.051, both close to zero. The two ordinary differences did exactly what they were meant to on the far seasonal echoes, they collapsed. But lag 12 itself is still there, at -0.398, the largest bar left anywhere in the whole plot.



See it as a bar chart.




One seasonal difference removed enough of the season for the two distant echoes to vanish, but the season itself is not gone. It moved from a slow decay spread across three lags to one sharp spike sitting right at lag 12. Removing a spike that specific, at one exact lag and nowhere else, is a job for a term built to look exactly there, which is what the next step introduces.







The seasonal part: P, D, Q and the period m



An ordinary ARIMA is written ARIMA(p, d, q). p counts non-seasonal AR terms, how many recent lags feed into the current value. d counts ordinary differences. q counts non-seasonal MA terms, how many recent forecast errors feed into the current value.



A seasonal ARIMA adds three more letters and a number: (P, D, Q)[m]. Each capital letter counts the same kind of term as its lower-case twin, just at the seasonal lag instead of the ordinary one. P counts seasonal AR terms, values exactly m, 2m, 3m steps back. D counts seasonal differences, exactly what the last step just did at lag 12. Q counts seasonal MA terms, forecast errors exactly m, 2m, 3m steps back. And m is the period itself, the number of time steps in one season, 12 for this monthly series.



See the two rows side by side.




The lag-12 spike left over from the last step, at -0.398, is exactly the kind of pattern a seasonal MA term is built to absorb: one seasonal difference (D = 1) plus one seasonal MA term (Q = 1), reaching back exactly 12 months. The next two steps show how that seasonal part actually combines with the non-seasonal one already familiar from ARIMA(p, d, q).







How seasonal and non-seasonal terms multiply



A SARIMA model does not just add its seasonal terms next to the non-seasonal ones, it multiplies them. Seeing why starts with the backshift operator, B, a short way of writing "one step earlier": B applied to the series steps it back one month, and B raised to the twelfth power steps it back twelve months, one full season.



A non-seasonal AR(1) term is written as the factor (1 - phi B), where phi is its coefficient. A seasonal AR(1) term at period m = 12 is written the same way, just at the seasonal lag: (1 - Phi B^12), where Phi, capital phi, is its own coefficient. Multiply the two factors together and a new term appears that neither one has on its own.



\[ (1 - \phi B)(1 - \Phi B^{12}) = 1 - \phi B - \Phi B^{12} + \phi \Phi B^{13} \]



Fit both terms together on the training series, and read off phi and Phi from the report.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Fit a non-seasonal AR(1) and a seasonal AR(1) together, and read off their coefficients
library(fable)

fit_demo <- train |> model(demo = ARIMA(Turnover ~ pdq(1,1,1) + PDQ(1,1,1)))
report(fit_demo)
#> Series: Turnover 
#> Model: ARIMA(1,1,1)(1,1,1)[12] 
#> 
#> Coefficients:
#>          ar1      ma1    sar1     sma1
#>       0.2214  -0.4987  0.0787  -0.7195
#> s.e.  0.1548   0.1377  0.0660   0.0428
#> 
#> sigma^2 estimated as 245.9:  log likelihood=-1687
#> AIC=3384.01   AICc=3384.16   BIC=3404.02

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





fable names the non-seasonal AR coefficient ar1, and the seasonal one sar1: 0.2214 and 0.0787. The model also fits a non-seasonal MA term, ma1, and a seasonal MA term, sma1, since this fit asked for both an AR and an MA piece at each level, but the two AR coefficients are the ones the multiplication above is about.



Multiply phi and Phi together and see where the cross term lands.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compute the cross term the multiplication predicts
co <- fit_demo |> coef()
phi <- co$estimate[co$term == "ar1"]
Phi <- co$estimate[co$term == "sar1"]
round(phi * Phi, 3)
#> [1] 0.017

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





0.017. That number is not something either term produces alone. The non-seasonal AR(1) reaches lag 1, and the seasonal AR(1) reaches lag 12, but multiplying them together the way the formula above does creates a genuine third term, phi times Phi, sitting at lag 13, one month past the season. Nobody set out to model lag 13 directly. It falls out of the arithmetic the moment a non-seasonal and a seasonal term sit in the same model.







Quick check: reading a SARIMA multiplication



A non-seasonal AR(1) term and a seasonal AR(1) term at m = 12 are fit together, and multiplied the way the last step showed. Which lag carries the cross term their product creates?









Right. Multiplying (1 - phi B) by (1 - Phi B^12) keeps a term at lag 1 from the non-seasonal factor and a term at lag 12 from the seasonal factor, but it also creates a third term, phi times Phi, at lag 1 + 12 = 13, one month past the season.
Multiplying (1 - phi B) by (1 - Phi B^12) does not touch lag 24 at all. It keeps the two lags each factor already had, 1 and 12, and adds exactly one new one, phi times Phi at lag 13, the sum of the two exponents.









Fitting ARIMA(y ~ pdq() + PDQ()) in fable



Picking every one of p, d, q, P, D and Q by hand is possible, but fable's ARIMA() function can search for the best combination on its own. Call it with no formula at all, and let it choose.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Let ARIMA() search for the best combination of seasonal and non-seasonal terms
fit_auto <- train |> model(sarima = ARIMA(Turnover))
report(fit_auto)
#> Series: Turnover 
#> Model: ARIMA(0,1,2)(0,1,1)[12] 
#> 
#> Coefficients:
#>           ma1      ma2     sma1
#>       -0.2662  -0.0910  -0.6862
#> s.e.   0.0506   0.0525   0.0368
#> 
#> sigma^2 estimated as 245.5:  log likelihood=-1687.29
#> AIC=3382.57   AICc=3382.67   BIC=3398.58

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





ARIMA() landed on ARIMA(0,1,2)(0,1,1)[12]: one ordinary difference and one seasonal difference are already applied inside the fit, exactly the d = 1 and D = 1 from a few steps back, and no p or P terms were needed at all for this series. What is left are two non-seasonal MA terms, ma1 = -0.2662 and ma2 = -0.0910, and one seasonal MA term, sma1 = -0.6862, the same Q = 1 term pointed at earlier.



See the same three numbers restyled as a table.




AICc, at 3382.67, is the number ARIMA() actually searched on to pick this combination over every other one it tried: lower is better, and it is the same criterion the non-seasonal ARIMA() search already used before any seasonal terms were involved. With the model fitted, the next step is forecasting with it, and checking that forecast against real months nobody showed the model.







Forecasting from a fitted SARIMA, and checking it against what happened



test has been waiting since the second step: 24 real months, January 2017 through December 2018, that fit_auto never saw while it was being fit. Forecast that far ahead, and read the mean and the prediction intervals for the first and the last month.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
library(fabletools)
# Forecast 24 months ahead, then read the mean and intervals for two months
fc <- fit_auto |> forecast(h = 24)
fc_hilo <- fc |> hilo(level = c(80, 95))

jan_2017 <- fc_hilo |> filter(Month == yearmonth("2017 Jan"))
round(jan_2017$.mean, 1)
#> [1] 873.6
round(jan_2017$`80%`$lower, 1)
#> [1] 853.5
round(jan_2017$`80%`$upper, 1)
#> [1] 893.7
round(jan_2017$`95%`$lower, 1)
#> [1] 842.9
round(jan_2017$`95%`$upper, 1)
#> [1] 904.3

dec_2018 <- fc_hilo |> filter(Month == yearmonth("2018 Dec"))
round(dec_2018$.mean, 1)
#> [1] 1051.9

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





For January 2017, the forecast mean is 873.6, with an 80% interval of 853.5 to 893.7 and a 95% interval of 842.9 to 904.3. The real turnover that month, still sitting in test, was 861.4, inside both intervals. For December 2018, the last month of the test set, the forecast mean is 1051.9, against a real turnover of 1066.2.



Check the whole 24-month forecast against the real values at once.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Check the whole 24-month forecast against the real test-period turnover
acc <- fabletools::accuracy(fc, test)
round(acc$RMSE, 1)
#> [1] 28.2
round(acc$MAPE, 2)
#> [1] 2.75
round(acc$ME, 1)
#> [1] -22.3

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Across all 24 months, the average miss (RMSE) is 28.2, and as a share of the real turnover (MAPE) it is 2.75%. The mean error, -22.3, is negative and small next to turnovers running from 753.6 to 1066.2 across the test period, meaning the forecast leans slightly low on average rather than missing wildly in either direction.



See the forecast laid over the real test-period turnover, month by month.









Quick check: reading a SARIMA forecast



Look back at the forecast chart from the last step. In both 2017 and 2018, December sits well above every other month around it, 1005.9 and 1051.9 against neighbors in the 800s and 900s. What causes that?









Right. Every December in the training data was Victoria's highest month for cafes, restaurants and takeaway turnover. The seasonal difference at lag 12 plus sma1 = -0.6862 are exactly what carry that repeating shape from the training series into the forecast, one December after another.
The December bump traces back to the seasonal part of the model, not a trend, not noise, and not the two non-seasonal MA terms. The seasonal difference at lag 12 plus sma1 = -0.6862 reproduce the same December peak that showed up every year in the training data.

Quick recap



You can now do what an ordinary ARIMA(p, d, q) cannot: read a seasonal signature straight off an ACF, 0.904, 0.817 and 0.731 at lags 12, 24 and 36 here, remove it with a seasonal difference, and know that what is left over calls for a seasonal AR or MA term at the period m. You have seen why a non-seasonal and a seasonal term multiply rather than just add, producing a genuine new term, phi times Phi, 0.017 here, at lag 13.



And you fit a real one: ARIMA(0,1,2)(0,1,1)[12] on 417 months of Victorian retail turnover, forecast the next 24, and checked that forecast against what actually happened, a MAPE of 2.75% against real turnovers the fit never saw.
