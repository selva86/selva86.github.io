Difference-in-Differences and Its One Assumption

Today let's look at one of the simplest ways to work out what a change actually did: track two groups, one that got the change and one that did not, before and after it happened.

Here is that comparison already drawn, for a short setup checklist a software product turned on for half its new signups.

The blue line follows the group that got the checklist and the orange line follows the group that did not, both measured before and after. The dashed segment is where the blue group would have landed without the checklist, and the number on the right, 6, is the gap between what it actually did and that dashed line.

Two groups, one checklist, and a comparison that looks obvious

Say a software product adds a short setup checklist for new signups. Starting in month 2, a random half of new signups see the checklist, the treated group, and the rest do not, the control group. The outcome tracked is the percent of new teams that finish setup within their first week.

Month 1 (before) Month 2 (after)
Treated 18% 30%
Control 20% 26%

The obvious thing to do is compare the two groups after the checklist shipped: 30% for treated against 26% for control, a gap of 4 percentage points. It is tempting to call that 4-point gap the checklist's effect.

But look at month 1, before the checklist existed for anyone. Treated was already at 18%, two points behind control's 20%. The two groups did not start even, for reasons that have nothing to do with a checklist that had not shipped yet. So part of that 4-point gap after the checklist is really just the 2-point gap the groups started with, not anything the checklist did.

What would have happened without the checklist

To separate the checklist's real effect from that head start, you need one thing: what would treated's month 2 number have been if it had never seen the checklist? Call that number the counterfactual. It is the value you would see in a world identical to the real one, except that the treated group never got the checklist.

You cannot observe this number directly. Nobody gets to watch the same group live through month 2 twice, once with the checklist and once without. But you can estimate it from the group that is right there: control stands in for "no checklist," so whatever control did between month 1 and month 2 is a reasonable stand-in for what treated would have done too, had it also gone untouched.

Control moved from 20% to 26%, a rise of 6 points. Apply that same 6-point rise to treated's own starting point of 18%, and the counterfactual comes out to 18 + 6 = 24%.

Month 1 (before) Month 2 (after)
Treated (actual) 18% 30%
Treated (counterfactual, no checklist) 18% 24%
Control 20% 26%

The double difference, computed by hand

Now you have everything you need. Treated's actual result was 30%, and its counterfactual was 24%. The checklist's effect is the difference between those two: 30 - 24 = 6 percentage points.

There is a shorter way to reach the same 6, one that never writes down a counterfactual at all. Take each group's own change from month 1 to month 2: treated rose by 30 - 18 = 12 points, control rose by 26 - 20 = 6 points. Subtract control's change from treated's change: 12 - 6 = 6. Same answer.

This subtraction of two differences, the within-group change for treated minus the within-group change for control, is called the double difference. It removes two things at once: the fixed 2-point gap the groups started with, and the 6-point rise that both groups shared regardless of the checklist. What is left over, 6 points, is the part of the change that belongs to the checklist alone, nowhere close to the naive 4-point gap from two steps ago.

Quick check: reading the double difference

A separate team ran a similar test. Before the change, treated averaged 35 and control averaged 30. After the change, treated averaged 50 and control averaged 38. Which number is the unbiased estimate of the change's effect?

Right: treated rose by 50 minus 35 equals 15, control rose by 38 minus 30 equals 8, and 15 minus 8 equals 7. That double difference removes the fixed gap the two groups started with and any change the two groups shared, leaving only the part that belongs to whatever changed.
The double difference subtracts each group's own before-to-after change, then takes the difference of those two changes. That is the only subtraction that cancels the fixed gap between the groups and any trend shared by both periods; a group's own change alone, or the two groups' after-only gap, does not.

Difference-in-differences with lm(y ~ treat*post)

The arithmetic above is also exactly what a regression returns, once you set it up the right way. Fit lm(y ~ treat * post) on data with one row per unit per period, a 0/1 column for which group a unit is in (treat), and a 0/1 column for which period a row is from (post). The coefficient on the interaction term, treat:post, is the double difference.

Here is that same regression, fit on a larger simulated version of this setup.

This widget's code runs its own simulation: 200 units split evenly between the two groups, with a group gap, a shared time trend, and a true effect of 6 all built in on purpose so the numbers can be checked. Press Run on the code below the plot.

Toggle to naive and the simulation returns a post-only gap of 4.09, close to the clean 4 from two steps back. Toggle to difference-in-differences and it returns 6.67, close to the clean 6 you computed by hand. The small gaps, 4.09 against 4 and 6.67 against 6, are random noise added on top of the same effect, not a sign the two methods disagree.

Look at the regression output printed below the plot. The row for treat:post reads 6.67, the same number the toggle showed. A regression is not a different method from the subtraction you did by hand. It is the same double difference, computed through a model instead of a table.

The parallel-trends assumption, stated precisely

Two steps back, you used control's 6-point rise as a stand-in for what treated would have done without the checklist. That swap only gives the right answer under one condition, and it is worth saying out loud instead of leaving it buried inside a subtraction.

The condition is called the parallel-trends assumption: without the checklist, treated's change from month 1 to month 2 would have equaled control's change. Not that the two groups would have landed at the same level, they clearly started 2 points apart, but that they would have moved by the same amount.

Notice what kind of claim this is. It is a claim about a trend, a rate of change, not a level. And it is a claim about what would have happened in a world that never occurred, the one where treated never saw the checklist.

You cannot check a claim about an unobserved world directly, in the very period where you are using it. The best you can do is look for evidence from a period before the checklist existed, when both groups' changes actually were observable.

Checking parallel trends before the checklist shipped

The clearest evidence you can get for the parallel-trends assumption is to go back before the checklist shipped and see whether the two groups' changes already matched, back when nothing had happened yet to either one.

Suppose you also have the three months leading up to the checklist's launch in month 2. In month -3, treated was at 4% and control was at 10%. In month -2, treated rose to 6% and control rose to 12%. In month -1, treated rose to 8% and control rose to 14%.

Here are those six numbers plotted, one point per group per month.

Both groups rose by exactly 2 percentage points every month, treated from 4 to 6 to 8, control from 10 to 12 to 14. The two groups sit at different levels throughout, treated is always 6 points under control, but they are climbing at the identical rate. Matching slopes like this, in the period before the checklist existed, is the practical version of a pre-trends check: if the two groups moved in step before anything happened, that is evidence, not proof, that they would have kept moving in step afterward too.

A clean pre-trends check is evidence, not proof

The matching slopes you just saw are good evidence, but evidence is not the same as proof. Here is a case built to show the difference: the same matching pre-launch slopes as before, with one unrelated event added right at launch.

Keep the same three months before launch. Now suppose that in month 0, a regional push unrelated to the checklist lifts control from 14% all the way to 22%, a jump of 8 points in one month instead of its usual 2. Treated's true, unobserved counterfactual keeps climbing at its own honest rate of 2 points a month, from 8% to 10%. Treated's actual observed value in month 0, with the checklist running, is 15%.

Here is that same chart with month 0 added: control's actual jump, treated's actual value, and treated's true but unobserved counterfactual.

Work out what difference-in-differences says here, the same way as before. Control's observed change from month -1 to month 0 is 22 - 14 = 8 points, standing in, as always, for the counterfactual change. Applied to treated's last pre-launch value, that gives a naive counterfactual of 8 + 8 = 16. The double difference is treated's actual 15 minus that counterfactual 16, which equals -1.

But the true effect, using the real counterfactual of 10 that keeps the honest 2-point pace, is 15 - 10 = 5. Difference-in-differences, fed by a regional push that had nothing to do with the checklist, reports -1 when the truth is +5. It gets the sign wrong, even though the pre-launch slopes, the only check you had, matched perfectly for three straight months.

A clean pre-trends check rules out one kind of problem: groups that were already drifting apart before you started. It cannot rule out something new happening to one group only, after launch, for reasons that have nothing to do with your checklist.

Closing check: judging the assumption, not just the arithmetic

A delivery app turns on same-day delivery for a random half of its restaurant partners starting in week 5. For four weeks before launch, weeks 1 to 4, both the treated and the control partners' average weekly order volume rose by about 3 orders a week, a closely matching trend. In week 5, a city-wide driver shortage hits only the control partners, unrelated to the same-day delivery rollout. The computed double difference for week 5 comes out to +9 orders a week.

Right: a matching pre-launch trend is evidence that the two groups were moving the same way before anything happened. It says nothing about a shock that hits only one group after launch, and a driver shortage limited to the control partners is exactly that kind of shock.
A matching pre-trend is evidence that the two groups were moving the same way before the policy, not proof that they would have kept moving the same way after it. An event that hits only one group after launch breaks the assumption difference-in-differences depends on, even when the pre-launch check looked clean.

Your turn: compute a difference-in-differences by hand

Here is a fresh case to work through yourself. A product team sends a new onboarding email to a random half of new signups, and tracks 7-day retention, the percent of signups still active a week after joining.

Before the rollout, treated signups retained at 40% and control signups retained at 42%. After the rollout, treated retained at 52% and control retained at 46%. Two months before the rollout, both groups were already being tracked: treated moved 36%, 38%, and control moved 38%, 40%, one month at a time, right up to the 40% and 42% they were each already at just before the rollout.

One number, one assumption



You now have what you need to use difference-in-differences and to know when to trust it.



The double difference removes any fixed gap between two groups and any trend the two groups genuinely share, leaving the part of the change that belongs to whatever happened to only one of them.



It works only if the two groups would have kept moving together without that one thing happening, the parallel-trends assumption. A matching pre-launch trend is good evidence for that assumption. It is never proof, because an unrelated event can still hit one group only, after the period your check could see.
