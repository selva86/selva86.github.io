Lesson 2 of 7

Split Conformal Prediction

Last lesson left Rohan the real-estate agent with a warning. His model's tidy "90% prediction interval" quietly covered only 82% of his biggest listings, because it trusted the errors to be a neat, equal-variance bell curve. The moment that assumption cracked, the promise cracked with it.

This lesson builds an interval that makes no such assumption. Split conformal prediction reads its width straight off the model's own misses on held-out data, and in return it guarantees its coverage: at least the target you asked for, whatever shape the errors take, from just one held-out slice and a single quantile.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

  • Explain why an interval built on a bell-curve assumption cannot be trusted once that assumption is wrong, and what split conformal guarantees instead
  • Build a conformal band by hand in R: split into train and calibration, score the misses, take the conformal quantile as the half-width
  • Measure a band's empirical coverage, confirm the guarantee, and say exactly what it does and does not promise

Prerequisites: Lesson 1 (prediction intervals, coverage, empirical coverage), fitting a line with lm(), and the fact that a residual is actual minus predicted (OLS regression). Basic R (vectors, logical indexing, data.frame). Every new term is defined as it appears.

Rohan's market, honestly

The errors are not a bell curve

Let us rebuild Rohan's market, but this time we stop pretending the price shocks are tidy. Real sale prices have a long right tail: most homes go a little under the trend, and a few (a bidding war, a cash buyer, a dream kitchen) blow far past it. Each lesson runs in a fresh R session, so we create the data right here (run this once). The shock term below is deliberately right-skewed, and its spread still grows with size, so big homes remain the wild ones.

RInteractive R
set.seed(1) n <- 900 sqft <- round(runif(n, 600, 2600)) shock <- rlnorm(n, 0, 0.7) # right-skewed: a few homes fetch far above the trend shock <- shock - exp(0.7^2 / 2) # centre it so the average shock is 0 price <- round(60000 + 180 * sqft + (30 * sqft) * shock) homes <- data.frame(sqft, price) head(homes, 4) #> sqft price #> 1 1131 286749 #> 2 1344 276816 #> 3 1746 338282 #> 4 2416 426673

  

  





To do today's method (and to test honestly, exactly as we tested on fresh homes in Lesson 1) we hold out two slices of the data up front. Train is where the line is fit, calibration is a held-out slice where we will measure the model's misses, and test is the final untouched check. That middle slice, the calibration set, is the whole engine of conformal prediction; hold that thought for two steps. Fit the line on train, then look at its misses on the calibration homes:




  

    
RInteractive R

    
tr  <- 1:400
cal <- 401:600
te  <- 601:900
fit    <- lm(price ~ sqft, data = homes[tr, ])
misses <- homes$price[cal] - predict(fit, homes[cal, ])   # residual = actual minus predicted
round(c(mean = mean(misses), median = median(misses)))
#>   mean median
#>  -2228  -9828

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The mean miss ($-2,228) sits well above the median ($-9,828). That gap is the fingerprint of a right-skewed pile: most homes sell a touch under the line, while a handful shoot far above it and drag the mean up. You can see the same shape in a histogram of the misses, a long tail stretching to the right:




  

    
RInteractive R

    
hist(misses, breaks = 30, col = "gray80",
     main = "", xlab = "miss on a held-out home ($)")   # note the long right tail

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





This is emphatically not the symmetric bell the textbook interval assumes. Let us watch that interval fail.







The textbook interval


A formula's promise breaks when its assumption does



The prediction interval lm hands you leans on one number: \(s\), a single "typical miss" for the whole dataset, wrapped in a bell-curve formula. When the misses really are a symmetric bell, that works. When they are skewed and heavy-tailed like Rohan's, it does not. Ask lm for a 90% interval on the test homes and count how many it actually covers:




  

    
RInteractive R

    
band_lm   <- predict(fit, homes[te, ], interval = "prediction", level = 0.90)
inside_lm <- homes$price[te] >= band_lm[, "lwr"] & homes$price[te] <= band_lm[, "upr"]
round(mean(inside_lm), 3)                # we asked for 0.90
#> [1] 0.957

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





You asked for 90% and got 95.7%. That is not a happy bonus, it is a symptom. The heavy right tail inflated the single \(s\) the formula plugs in, so the band ballooned. How wide?




  

    
RInteractive R

    
round(mean(band_lm[, "upr"] - band_lm[, "lwr"]))   # average width of the 90% band, in dollars
#> [1] 227542

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





A band $227,542 wide. Rohan cannot walk into a listing meeting and say "your flat will sell somewhere in a $228,000 range." The interval is simultaneously the wrong coverage (95.7%, not the 90% on the label) and uselessly wide. The number on the label is decoration.



Key Insight
The normal-theory interval's "90%" is trustworthy only if the errors really are the equal-variance bell curve it assumes. Break that assumption and the label is fiction: sometimes too narrow (under-covering, which is dangerous), here too wide (over-covering, which is useless). Either way you cannot trust it.







The fix, in one idea


Let the misses set the width



Here is the move that repairs it. Instead of trusting a formula to tell us how wide the band should be, we measure how wrong the model actually is, on data it never trained on, and read the width straight off those misses. No bell curve, no variance formula. Just one honest question: "on held-out homes, 90% of the time the model missed by less than how much?" That amount becomes the half-width of the band.



That is split conformal prediction, and it is four steps. The diagram below is the whole method; the rest of the lesson is just doing each box in R.



Notice what is not in that diagram: any assumption about the shape of the errors. That absence is the entire point.









Steps 1 and 2


Split, then score each miss



We already did step 1, the split (tr, cal, te). Step 2 is scoring. The nonconformity score of a calibration home is just how far the model missed it, the size of the residual:



\[ s_i = \lvert\, y_i - \hat y_i \,\rvert \]



where \(y_i\) is that home's real price, \(\hat y_i\) is the model's prediction for it, and \(\lvert\cdot\rvert\) is absolute value, so a miss of \(+\$40\text{k}\) and a miss of \(-\$40\text{k}\) both score 40,000. A big score means the model was badly wrong on that home; a small score means it nailed it. We already have the misses, so the scores are one line:




  

    
RInteractive R

    
scores <- abs(misses)          # nonconformity = the size of each calibration miss
round(summary(scores))
#>    Min. 1st Qu.  Median    Mean 3rd Qu.    Max.
#>      26   11207   22633   35002   45901  277057

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Half the held-out homes were priced within about $22,600 of the model; one unlucky home missed by $277,000. That whole spread of misses is our raw material. Conformal asks exactly one thing of it: pick a width big enough that 90% of these misses fall under it.







Step 3


The conformal quantile



Now turn "90% of the misses fall under it" into a single number. Sort the calibration scores from smallest to largest and walk up to the one that leaves 90% below it. The exact rank is not the plain 90th percentile: it carries a small finite-sample correction.



\[ \hat q = s_{(k)}, \qquad k = \big\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \big\rceil \]



Here \(s_{(k)}\) is the \(k\)-th smallest score, \(n\) is the number of calibration homes, \(\alpha\) is the miss rate you will tolerate (here \(\alpha = 0.10\), for 90% coverage), and \(\lceil\cdot\rceil\) rounds up to the next whole number. The \(n+1\) instead of \(n\) is the quiet trick of the whole method: it reserves a slot for the one new home you are about to predict, and it is what upgrades "about 90%" into a real guarantee. Watch it on our numbers:




  

    
RInteractive R

    
alpha <- 0.10
n_cal <- length(cal)                       # 200 calibration homes
k     <- ceiling((n_cal + 1) * (1 - alpha))
c(n_cal = n_cal, k = k)
#> n_cal     k
#>   200   181

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





With 200 calibration homes, \((n+1)(1-\alpha) = 201 \times 0.9 = 180.9\), which rounds up to \(k = 181\). So \(\hat q\) is the 181st smallest of the 200 misses:




  

    
RInteractive R

    
qhat <- unname(sort(scores)[k])            # the 181st smallest calibration miss
round(qhat)
#> [1] 67075

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





\(\hat q = \$67{,}075\). Read it plainly: on held-out homes, 181 of every 200 misses (that is 90.5%) came in under $67,075. That single number is our band half-width.







Check yourself


Why the 181st, not the 180th?



To build a 90% band you took the 181st smallest of 200 calibration misses, not the plain 90th percentile (the 180th). Why the extra step up?








Right. The guarantee counts the new point as one of \(n+1\) equally-likely ranks; taking \(\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil\) is what makes the coverage provably reach the target rather than merely approximate it.
This confuses the quantile with the mean. \(\hat q\) is a high order statistic of the misses, not the average nudged upward; the \(+1\) is a precise finite-sample correction, not a fudge factor.









Step 4


The band, and does it really cover?



The last step: hang the band \(\hat y \pm \hat q\) on the model, then count how many fresh test homes land inside it.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
pred_te   <- predict(fit, homes[te, ])       # the model's point prediction for each test home
lower     <- pred_te - qhat
upper     <- pred_te + qhat
inside_cp <- homes$price[te] >= lower & homes$price[te] <= upper
round(mean(inside_cp), 3)                     # empirical coverage on fresh homes
#> [1] 0.9

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





0.900. Exactly the 90% we promised, delivered on homes the method never saw, without ever assuming a bell curve. And it is far sharper than the textbook band:




  

    
RInteractive R

    
round(2 * qhat)                               # conformal band width vs lm's 227542
#> [1] 134150

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





$134,150 wide against the normal interval's $227,542, about 40% narrower. Split conformal is both honest (it keeps the 90% promise) and sharp (a band Rohan can actually quote). Drag the target below and feel the trade: push it toward 95% and the band widens to keep the stronger promise, while the coverage read off the fresh test points tracks at or above your target every single time.









Why it works


Where the guarantee comes from



Why should reading a quantile off old misses promise anything about a new home? One word: exchangeability. The calibration homes and the new home must be interchangeable, drawn from the same process, so that before you see it, the new home's miss is just as likely to land at any rank among the calibration misses as any other. If that holds, the chance its miss lands beyond the \(k\)-th of \(n+1\) possible ranks is controlled, and the coverage is pinned between two lines:



\[ 1-\alpha \;\le\; P\big(Y_{\text{new}} \in [\hat y \pm \hat q]\big) \;<\; 1-\alpha + \frac{1}{n+1} \]



With 200 calibration homes that lower and upper fence are:




  

    
RInteractive R

    
c(target = 1 - alpha, upper_bound = round(1 - alpha + 1 / (n_cal + 1), 4))
#>      target upper_bound
#>       0.900       0.905

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





So the guarantee reads: coverage is at least 90% and below 90.5%, no matter what the errors look like, as long as the homes are exchangeable. That "no matter what" is the prize. The normal interval's promise evaporated the moment the bell curve was wrong; this one does not depend on the bell curve at all.



Note
Exchangeability is a real assumption, not a free lunch. It breaks under distribution shift: if next year's market stops resembling the homes you calibrated on (a crash, a boom, a brand-new neighbourhood), the guarantee lapses. Split conformal removes the bell-curve assumption, not the assumption that tomorrow looks like today.







One promise left to test


Does 90% hold for the pricey homes?



That guaranteed 90% is an average across all test homes. But Rohan's highest-stakes clients own the big, high-scatter homes, and Lesson 1 taught us to never trust an overall average without checking that subgroup on its own. Mark the big homes out:

big <- homes$sqft[te] > 1600      # the larger, higher-variance homes

#> [1] 148

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





148 test homes above 1,600 square feet. The band covered 90% overall, but does it cover 90% inside this group? Your turn.

The honest limit


Marginal, not conditional



There it is, the catch. Split conformal guarantees marginal coverage: the fraction inside, averaged over every kind of home. It says nothing about conditional coverage: the fraction inside within a particular subgroup. With one width \(\hat q\) applied to every home, the band is too generous for the calm little homes and too tight for the wild big ones:




  

    
RInteractive R

    
size <- ifelse(homes$sqft[te] > 1600, "big", "small")
round(tapply(inside_cp, size, mean), 3)
#>   big small
#> 0.838 0.961

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





96% of the small homes, 84% of the big ones, averaging out to the promised 90%. This is the exact shape of Lesson 1's failure, and vanilla split conformal, for all its guarantees, does not fix it: a constant width cannot follow a scatter that changes with size. Repairing it needs a band that widens where the model is uncertain, which is conformalized quantile regression, and that is Lesson 3.







Check yourself


What did you actually guarantee?



A colleague glances at your work and says, "the band covers 90%, so we are 90% covered for every segment and safe going forward." Which statement about the split conformal band you built is exactly right?








Exactly. Distribution-free and finite-sample on the margin, conditional on exchangeability, and silent about any single subgroup. That is precisely what the 84% big-home coverage exposed.
The opposite: split conformal makes no distributional assumption at all. That is why it kept the 90% promise on skewed errors where the normal interval's label was fiction.

Lesson 2 complete



You can now build a prediction band whose coverage is guaranteed, without trusting the errors to be any particular shape. The recipe is four steps: split off a calibration set, score each held-out miss by its absolute residual, take the conformal quantile \(\hat q = s_{(k)}\) with \(k = \lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil\) as the half-width, then hang the band \(\hat y \pm \hat q\) and confirm coverage on fresh data. On Rohan's skewed market it delivered a clean 90%, distribution-free, where the textbook interval's label was pure decoration, and it did so with a band about 40% tighter.



You also met its honest edge: the guarantee is marginal, not conditional. One global width kept the overall promise while still covering only 84% of the pricey homes, because a fixed band cannot follow a scatter that grows with size. And it rests on exchangeability, so a shifted future market can still break it.



Next, Lesson 3: Conformal Prediction for Classification. You will make the width adapt, so it stretches exactly where the model is unsure (conformalized quantile regression), pin down coverage within subgroups instead of only on average, and carry conformal from a numeric band to a prediction set of class labels that grows when the model hesitates.
