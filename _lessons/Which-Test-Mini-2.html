Which Test Do I Run?

Welch's ANOVA: the test for unequal group variances

Let's start with something that surprises most people.

Say a company asks you to compare average salaries across three departments. Support and Operations are the steady ones, where almost everyone earns something close to the same figure. Engineering is the messy one. It has twelve people, mostly juniors, plus a few specialists who are paid two or three times what a junior gets, so its salaries are scattered all over the place.

You run a one-way ANOVA on all sixty salaries and it hands back p = 0.00071. That looks settled.

The problem is that the classic ANOVA assumes the three departments have a similar spread, and Engineering breaks that assumption badly. When it breaks, the test starts giving wrong answers without printing a single warning.

Now change one argument, so that each department is weighted by its own spread, and the same sixty salaries hand back p = 0.108. The same people, the opposite verdict.

So which one deserves your trust? We are not going to settle that by arguing about it, we are going to measure it. Here is how.

That is the plan. By the end you will know which of the two answers to believe, why the other one is wrong, and how to write the result up.

The three departments and what their salaries look like

Let's get the numbers on the table first, because everything after this comes out of them.

Sixty people work across three departments. Support has 24, Operations has 24 and Engineering has 12, and each row is one person and one salary.

So the widest variance is fifteen times the narrowest. The common rule of thumb says the classic ANOVA can be trusted while the largest variance stays under about four times the smallest, and this data is nowhere near that.

What the classic F-test says about these three departments



The standard one-way ANOVA is one line of code. In base R you can get it from summary(aov(salary ~ dept, data = pay)), or from oneway.test() with var.equal = TRUE, which gives the identical F and p-value and prints them more compactly. We will use the second one, because the argument we are about to change lives there.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Run the classic one-way ANOVA, the version that assumes one shared spread
classic_fit <- oneway.test(salary ~ dept, data = pay, var.equal = TRUE)
classic_fit
#> 	One-way analysis of means
#>
#> data:  salary and dept
#> F = 8.2626, num df = 2, denom df = 57, p-value = 0.0007063

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Four numbers came back, and each one is worth naming.



    


  1. F = 8.2626 is the test statistic. It is a ratio: how far apart the three department averages are, divided by how much salaries bounce around inside a department. A big F means the gaps between departments are large compared with the noise inside them.
    2. 


  2. num df = 2 is the numerator degrees of freedom, which is the number of groups minus one. Three departments, so 2.
    3. 


  3. denom df = 57 is the denominator degrees of freedom, which is the number of people minus the number of groups. Sixty minus three, so 57.
    4. 


  4. p-value = 0.0007063 is the answer. If all three departments really paid the same on average, a spread of averages this wide would turn up about 7 times in every 10,000 companies.
    5. 





Taken at face value that is a strong result. Seven in ten thousand clears every threshold anybody uses, so you would walk into the meeting and say the departments differ.



Hold on to that 57 for a minute. It says the test counted all sixty salaries as sixty equally informative pieces of evidence about one single shared amount of noise. That is the assumption, stated as a number.







The pooled variance: one spread standing in for three



Everything that goes wrong here goes wrong in the denominator of F, so let's open it up.



The classic F divides the spread between the department averages by the spread inside the departments. There are three departments, so there are three inside-spreads. The classic test does not use three. It squashes them into one number, called the pooled variance, and divides by that.



\[ s_p^2 = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(n_i - 1)\,s_i^2}{N - k} \]



Each department's variance gets a weight of \(n_i - 1\), its own size minus one, and the weighted total is divided by the total sample size minus the number of groups. Let's work that number out for this company.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Work out the single pooled spread that the classic F-test divides by
pooled_var <- sum((dept_stats$n - 1) * dept_stats$var) / (sum(dept_stats$n) - 3)
round(sqrt(pooled_var))
#> [1] 12240

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The pooled standard deviation is 12,240. Hold that beside the three real ones. It is nearly twice Support's 6,420 and Operations' 6,282, and Engineering's 24,650 is a little over twice it. So it describes none of the three departments. It is too big for two of them and too small for the third, at the same time.



And this is not a cosmetic complaint. The pooled number decides how precisely the test believes it knows each department's average, and you can see the damage directly.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compare the standard error the pooled number gives each department against the
# one that department's own salaries support
se_table <- data.frame(
  own_sd         = dept_stats$sd,
  se_from_pooled = round(sqrt(pooled_var / dept_stats$n)),
  se_from_own_sd = round(dept_stats$sd / sqrt(dept_stats$n)),
  row.names      = rownames(dept_stats)
)
se_table
#>             own_sd se_from_pooled se_from_own_sd
#> Support       6420           2498           1310
#> Operations    6282           2498           1282
#> Engineering  24650           3533           7116

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The standard error of a group average is how much that average would wobble if you hired a fresh set of people into the same job. Small standard error means a trustworthy average.



Read the Engineering row. Its own twelve salaries support a standard error of 7,116. The pooled version hands it 3,533, which is half of that. So the test treats Engineering's average as twice as precise as its own data can justify.



Now read the two rows above it. Support and Operations get 2,498 each, when their own salaries support about 1,300. Their averages are treated as less precise than they really are.



Key Insight
The pooled variance is not an averaging of the noise. It is a claim that all three departments share one spread, and when that claim is false, the test misjudges every group at once: the noisy one becomes too trusted and the tight ones not trusted enough.



And notice which of those two mistakes matters most here. Engineering is the department whose average sits far from the other two, so over-trusting exactly that average is what pushes F up to 8.26.







Quick check: what the pooled variance assumes









Exactly. Pooling does not soften the disagreement between the three spreads, it declares that the disagreement is not there. Every standard error in the test then comes out of that one declared number.
Pooling replaces the three spreads with one number rather than averaging the noise away or filling a gap, and it does not push in a single safe direction. Here it makes Engineering's average look twice as precise as its own twelve salaries support, which pushes the test toward declaring a difference, not away from it.









Running Welch's ANOVA on the salary data and reading the output



Now the fix, and it is smaller than you would expect. Same function, same formula, same data, and one argument flips.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Run the same comparison without assuming the departments share a spread
welch_fit <- oneway.test(salary ~ dept, data = pay, var.equal = FALSE)
welch_fit
#> 	One-way analysis of means (not assuming equal variances)
#>
#> data:  salary and dept
#> F = 2.4432, num df = 2.000, denom df = 24.148, p-value = 0.1081

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





That is Welch's ANOVA, named after B. L. Welch, who published it in 1951. Let's read the output line by line, because three of the four numbers moved.



The title line now says "not assuming equal variances", which is R telling you which test you got.



F fell from 8.2626 to 2.4432. Welch builds its F out of the three separate variances instead of one pooled one, and Engineering's enormous variance now enters where it belongs, in the term that judges Engineering.



num df is still 2, because there are still three departments.



denom df is 24.148, not 57, and it is not a whole number. Nothing has gone wrong. A fractional denominator degrees of freedom is the signature of Welch's correction, and how far it falls below 57 measures how badly the three spreads disagree.



p-value is 0.1081. Above 0.05, so this test does not call a difference.



Look at what just happened. The sixty salaries did not change, only one argument did, and the p-value went from 0.00071 to 0.108. That is a factor of about 150, and the two tests now disagree about whether these departments pay differently at all.



Both answers cannot be right. Arguing about which assumption sounds more reasonable will not settle it, so let's settle it by measurement instead.







Two thousand companies where every department pays the same



The design is simple. We build companies where we already know the truth, then count how often each test gets it wrong.



Every simulated company has the same three departments with the same three sizes and the same three spreads as the real one. The only thing we change is that all three departments are given the exact same average salary of \$65,000. So in every single one of these companies there is no difference to find, and any p-value below 0.05 is a false alarm by construction.



The function below builds one such company, runs whichever test you ask for, and keeps its p-value. Two thousand of them take a few seconds.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Build 2,000 companies where all three departments pay the same on average,
# and keep the p-value that the chosen test gives each one
false_alarm <- function(sizes, sds, var_equal, runs = 2000) {
  set.seed(7)
  dept_sim <- factor(rep(c("Support", "Operations", "Engineering"), times = sizes),
                     levels = c("Support", "Operations", "Engineering"))
  replicate(runs, {
    pay_sim <- c(rnorm(sizes[1], 65000, sds[1]),
                 rnorm(sizes[2], 65000, sds[2]),
                 rnorm(sizes[3], 65000, sds[3]))
    oneway.test(pay_sim ~ dept_sim, var.equal = var_equal)$p.value
  })
}

classic_p <- false_alarm(c(24, 24, 12), dept_stats$sd, var_equal = TRUE)

hist(classic_p, breaks = 40, col = "grey85", border = "white",
     main = "2,000 companies where the three departments pay the same",
     xlab = "p-value from the classic F-test")
abline(v = 0.05, col = "red", lwd = 3)

sum(classic_p < 0.05)
#> [1] 379
mean(classic_p < 0.05)
#> [1] 0.1895

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Start with the histogram. When a test is working properly and there is nothing to find, its p-values spread out evenly from 0 to 1, so a flat picture is the healthy one and about 5% of the companies should land left of the red line at 0.05. This picture is not flat. It leans hard toward zero.



Now look at the count. 379 of the 2,000 companies came in under 0.05, which is 18.95%, or near enough 19 in 100. In every one of those 379 companies the three departments paid exactly the same average salary, and the classic test said they did not.



Warning
A test set to 0.05 is promising you a false alarm rate of 5 in 100. With these three spreads and these three group sizes, the classic F-test delivers about 19 in 100. That is not slightly off. It is wrong nearly four times as often as it claims to be.







The same two thousand companies, judged by Welch's ANOVA



Now the honest comparison. The set.seed(7) sits inside the function, so calling it again rebuilds the identical 2,000 companies, the same salaries in the same order. The only difference this time is var.equal = FALSE.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Judge the identical 2,000 companies with the version that does not pool
welch_p <- false_alarm(c(24, 24, 12), dept_stats$sd, var_equal = FALSE)

hist(welch_p, breaks = 40, col = "grey85", border = "white",
     main = "The same 2,000 companies, judged by Welch's ANOVA",
     xlab = "p-value from Welch's ANOVA")
abline(v = 0.05, col = "red", lwd = 3)

sum(welch_p < 0.05)
#> [1] 79
mean(welch_p < 0.05)
#> [1] 0.0395

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The histogram is flat now. The pile at zero is gone and the bars sit at roughly the same height from one end to the other, which is what the p-values of a properly calibrated test look like when there is nothing to find.



79 out of 2,000 came in under 0.05. That is 3.95%, and 5 in 100 was what the threshold promised.



So these are not two defensible readings of the same evidence. On data shaped like this one, the classic test raises a false alarm 19 times in 100, and Welch's raises one 4 times in 100.



Key Insight
That settles the real company. Its classic p-value of 0.00071 came from a test that, on data with these spreads and these group sizes, hands out false alarms at nearly four times the rate it advertises. The answer to carry into the meeting is Welch's 0.108.







Quick check: what the 19 in 100 was counting









Yes. The truth was fixed before the test ever ran, and it was always the same truth: no difference. So the count is a straight tally of the test's mistakes.
All three of these read the 19 in 100 as a statement about a difference, or about a particular result. It is neither. Every one of the 2,000 companies was built with all three departments paying the same \$65,000, so the number counts only how often the test announced something that was not there.









How group sizes change the false alarm rate



Unequal spreads are half the story. The other half is which department got how many people.



In the real company the noisiest department, Engineering, is also the smallest at 12. So let's keep the three spreads exactly as they are and hand every department 20 people instead, so the only thing that changed is the balance.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Keep the same three spreads but give every department 20 people
balanced_classic <- false_alarm(c(20, 20, 20), dept_stats$sd, var_equal = TRUE)
balanced_welch   <- false_alarm(c(20, 20, 20), dept_stats$sd, var_equal = FALSE)

data.frame(
  group_sizes  = c("24 / 24 / 12", "20 / 20 / 20"),
  classic_rate = c(mean(classic_p < 0.05), mean(balanced_classic < 0.05)),
  welch_rate   = c(mean(welch_p < 0.05),   mean(balanced_welch < 0.05))
)
#>    group_sizes classic_rate welch_rate
#> 1 24 / 24 / 12       0.1895     0.0395
#> 2 20 / 20 / 20       0.0680     0.0500

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





With balanced groups the classic test costs you 6.8 in 100 instead of the 5 it promises. That is worth knowing, but it is not a disaster. Make the noisy department the small one and the same test costs you 19 in 100.



The reason is in how the pooled variance is built. Each department's variance enters it with a weight of its own size minus one, so Engineering's huge variance enters with the smallest weight of the three, 11 against 23 and 23. The pooled number therefore comes out far too small for Engineering, and Engineering's average is the one it is then used to judge.



Welch's rate barely moves, 3.95 and 5.00. It never built a shared number, so there is nothing for the group sizes to unbalance.



That raises the obvious question. What happens if the noisy department is the biggest one instead of the smallest?

Make a guess before you run anything. Engineering is noisy, and pooling under-weights a small noisy group. So what should happen when the noisy group is the large one?



The helper is already loaded and dept_stats$sd still holds the three spreads.

The classic test fired 2.15 times in 100 where 5 was promised. It has not become safer, it has gone deaf. Hold on to that, because a test that fires at 2 in 100 when it should fire at 5 is also a test that will walk past real differences, and in a few minutes you are going to watch it do exactly that on a real set of salaries.

How Welch's F is built: own-variance weights and a shrunken df



We have watched Welch's ANOVA behave. Now let's take the lid off and rebuild its two numbers by hand, so nothing in that output stays a black box.



It starts by giving each department a weight, which is its size divided by its own variance.



\[ w_i = \frac{n_i}{s_i^2} \]



A department that is big and tight gets a large weight, and a department that is small and noisy gets a tiny one.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Give each department a weight built from its own size and its own variance
k <- 3
w <- dept_stats$n / dept_stats$var
names(w) <- rownames(dept_stats)
signif(w, 3)
#>     Support  Operations Engineering
#>    5.82e-07    6.08e-07    1.97e-08

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The numbers are tiny because salaries are in the tens of thousands and the variances are in the hundreds of millions, but only their ratio matters. Support's weight is 29.5 times Engineering's. So Engineering gets about a thirtieth of Support's say in the test, and it earned that by being small and scattered.



Those weights then drive two things. The first is the centre the departments are compared against, which is a weighted average rather than a plain one.



\[ \bar{x}_w = \frac{\sum_i w_i \bar{x}_i}{\sum_i w_i}, \qquad W = \sum_i w_i \]



Here \(\bar{x}_i\) is one department's own average salary and \(W\) is the three weights added together, so \(\bar{x}_w\) is the whole company's centre with each department counting in proportion to its weight.



The second is a single quantity that measures how unequal the weights are. Call it \(\Lambda\).



\[ \Lambda = \frac{3}{k^2 - 1} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{\left(1 - w_i / W\right)^2}{n_i - 1} \]



When all three departments have the same spread and the same size, the weights come out equal and \(\Lambda\) is small. The more lopsided the weights, the bigger it gets. Both of Welch's headline numbers are then built from it.



\[ F = \frac{\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_i w_i\left(\bar{x}_i - \bar{x}_w\right)^2}{1 + \frac{2(k-2)}{3}\Lambda}, \qquad \mathrm{df}_2 = \frac{1}{\Lambda} \]



A few lines of R are enough to check every one of those against what the fitted object printed.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Rebuild the weighted centre, Welch's F and its denominator df from the weights,
# then work out the F each version needs before it will call a difference
grand_w <- sum(w * dept_stats$mean) / sum(w)
lambda  <- (3 / (k^2 - 1)) * sum((1 - w / sum(w))^2 / (dept_stats$n - 1))
f_hand  <- (sum(w * (dept_stats$mean - grand_w)^2) / (k - 1)) /
           (1 + (2 * (k - 2) / 3) * lambda)

round(c(grand_mean = grand_w, F = f_hand, denom_df = 1 / lambda), 3)
#> grand_mean          F   denom_df
#>  62955.350      2.443     24.148

round(c(classic_cutoff = qf(0.95, 2, 57), welch_cutoff = qf(0.95, 2, 1 / lambda)), 2)
#> classic_cutoff   welch_cutoff
#>           3.16           3.40

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





F is 2.443 and the denominator degrees of freedom is 24.148, which is exactly what the fitted object printed. Nothing was hidden.



The weighted centre is worth a look too. It lands at \$62,955, right beside Support and Operations, not halfway out toward Engineering's \$78,748. A plain average of the three department means would sit near \$68,000. So Welch pulls the centre toward the departments it can measure precisely, which is the whole idea in one number.



Note
The denominator degrees of freedom is where the protection is paid for. With equal spreads it lands close to the classic 57. Here the spreads are wildly unequal, so it falls to 24.148, and a smaller denominator df means a larger F is needed before the test will call a difference. That is the second line the block printed: on 57 an F of 3.16 clears the 5% bar, and on 24.148 you need 3.40. The test has fewer usable pieces of information than a straight headcount suggests, and it prices that in.







Quick check: reading F(2, 24.15) = 2.44, p = 0.108









Right. It is an effective quantity rather than a headcount, which is why it comes out fractional. Watching it fall from 57 to 24.148 is watching the test admit what the unequal spreads cost.
All sixty salaries are in the test and no row was dropped. What falls is not the amount of data but the amount of usable information about the department averages, which is exactly why the number comes out fractional, and why you report it as 24.15 rather than rounding it away.









Should you test for equal variances before choosing?



The obvious routine is to check the spreads first and pick the test afterwards. R gives you a formal check for it, Bartlett's test, whose null hypothesis is that all the groups share one variance.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Ask formally whether the three departments share a single spread
bartlett.test(salary ~ dept, data = pay)
#> 	Bartlett test of homogeneity of variances
#>
#> data:  salary by dept
#> Bartlett's K-squared = 43.796, df = 2, p-value = 3.089e-10

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





On this company it is emphatic at p = 3.089e-10, so the spreads are not equal and you would switch to Welch's. The routine worked here. The question is whether it works in general.



To find that out we need a harder case than this one. Let's build companies whose spreads differ by a factor of two, 6,000 and 6,000 against 12,000, which is a variance ratio of exactly 4 and sits right on the rule of thumb. All three departments pay the same, so again every rejection is a false alarm. This time each company gets Bartlett's test as well as both ANOVAs, and we record what each of three standing routines would have decided.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compare three standing routines on 2,000 companies whose departments all pay the
# same and whose spreads differ by a factor of two
routine_check <- function(sizes = c(24, 24, 12), sds = c(6000, 6000, 12000), runs = 2000) {
  set.seed(21)
  dept_sim <- factor(rep(c("Support", "Operations", "Engineering"), times = sizes),
                     levels = c("Support", "Operations", "Engineering"))
  replicate(runs, {
    pay_sim <- c(rnorm(sizes[1], 65000, sds[1]),
                 rnorm(sizes[2], 65000, sds[2]),
                 rnorm(sizes[3], 65000, sds[3]))
    bart    <- bartlett.test(pay_sim ~ dept_sim)$p.value
    classic <- oneway.test(pay_sim ~ dept_sim, var.equal = TRUE)$p.value
    welch   <- oneway.test(pay_sim ~ dept_sim, var.equal = FALSE)$p.value
    c(bartlett = bart, always_classic = classic, always_welch = welch,
      pre_test_then_choose = if (bart < 0.05) welch else classic)
  })
}

routines <- routine_check()
round(rowMeans(routines < 0.05), 4)
#>             bartlett       always_classic         always_welch
#>               0.7555               0.1250               0.0500
#> pre_test_then_choose
#>               0.0685

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The bartlett row is not a false alarm rate. It is how often the pre-test noticed the unequal spreads that were really there, 75.55%. So roughly one time in four, Bartlett's test missed them and handed the company to the classic F-test.



The other three rows are false alarm rates for the three routines, and they line up like this. Always run the classic test and you pay 12.5 in 100. Always run Welch's and you pay 5.0 in 100, which is the promise kept. Test first and then choose, and you pay 6.85 in 100.



So the routine helps a lot and still does not get you home. It cannot, because it only protects you in the three cases out of four where it sees the problem.



Tip
Bartlett's test is also touchy about non-normal data and will flag unequal spreads that are not there when your data has heavy tails. Levene's test, in car::leveneTest(), compares distances from each group's median instead and holds up better. Either way, the cleanest routine is to drop the pre-test and run Welch's every time.







What Welch's ANOVA costs when the spreads really are equal



Running Welch's by default only makes sense if it is close to free on the data the classic test was designed for. So let's give the classic test its best possible day and measure what Welch's costs there.



Every simulated company below has all three departments on the same spread of 6,000, which is the equal-variance assumption satisfied exactly. Each test is measured twice. The first run is on companies where all three departments pay \$65,000, which measures false alarms. The second is on companies where they really do pay differently, at \$62,000, \$64,000 and \$68,000, which measures power, meaning how often a test catches a difference that is genuinely there.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Give all three departments the SAME spread, then measure both tests twice: once
# where the departments really do pay the same, once where they really differ
cost_check <- function(sizes = c(24, 24, 12), sd = 6000, runs = 2000) {
  set.seed(11)
  dept_sim <- factor(rep(c("Support", "Operations", "Engineering"), times = sizes),
                     levels = c("Support", "Operations", "Engineering"))
  run_once <- function(means) {
    pay_sim <- c(rnorm(sizes[1], means[1], sd),
                 rnorm(sizes[2], means[2], sd),
                 rnorm(sizes[3], means[3], sd))
    c(classic = oneway.test(pay_sim ~ dept_sim, var.equal = TRUE)$p.value,
      welch   = oneway.test(pay_sim ~ dept_sim, var.equal = FALSE)$p.value)
  }
  no_gap   <- replicate(runs, run_once(c(65000, 65000, 65000)))
  real_gap <- replicate(runs, run_once(c(62000, 64000, 68000)))
  data.frame(
    test         = c("classic", "Welch"),
    false_alarms = rowMeans(no_gap   < 0.05),
    power        = rowMeans(real_gap < 0.05)
  )
}
cost_check()
#>            test false_alarms  power
#> classic classic       0.0460 0.6985
#> welch     Welch       0.0485 0.6690

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





On false alarms there is nothing to choose between them. Both sit on the 5 the threshold promises, at 4.60 in 100 and 4.85 in 100.



On power the classic test wins, and by very little. It catches the real difference 69.85% of the time and Welch's catches it 66.90%, a gap of about three cases in a hundred.



So that is the whole trade, in two lines. On the classic test's best day, Welch's gives up about three points of power. On the classic test's bad day, which is any dataset shaped like the real company, it called a difference 19 times in 100 that was not there. That asymmetry is the argument Delacre and colleagues make for running Welch's by default rather than treating it as the emergency option.



Be clear about what it does not touch, though. Welch's drops the single shared spread and nothing else, so it still expects each department's salaries to be roughly normal in shape and each person to count once and independently of everyone else. If one of those is what has gone wrong with your data, this is not the repair for it.







Which departments actually differ



Whatever the omnibus test says, it says it about all three departments at once, and it never names a pair. To learn that Engineering differs from Support you have to run a follow-up, and that follow-up has two problems to solve at the same time.



The first is the pooled spread all over again. The second is that three departments make three pairs, so you are about to run three tests, each carrying its own chance of a false alarm. Drag the slider below to see what running a family of tests does to that chance, and switch the correction on to see it brought back under control.




At three tests it puts the chance of at least one false alarm near 14 in 100 rather than 5. The three pairwise comparisons on one dataset are not fully independent of each other, so the real figure here is a little lower than that, but the shape of the problem and the fix are the same. Holm's correction pulls the rate back to the 5 in 100 you asked for by testing the smallest p-value against a stricter bar, the next smallest against a slightly looser one, and so on down the family.



Now the pooled-spread problem. The usual follow-up to a classic ANOVA is Tukey's honest significant difference, and it is built on that same pooled standard deviation, so it inherits the same flaw.




  

    
RInteractive R

Tukey is confident. Engineering differs from Support at p = 0.0012 and from Operations at 0.0015. It is confident for exactly the reason the classic omnibus test was confident, and it is wrong for the same reason.



The follow-up that matches Welch's ANOVA drops the pooling. In base R that is pairwise.t.test() with pool.sd = FALSE, which runs a Welch-style t-test on each pair using only those two departments' own spreads, and p.adjust.method = "holm" to hold the family of three at one error rate.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Ask the same question with each pair judged on its own two spreads
pairwise.t.test(pay$salary, pay$dept, p.adjust.method = "holm", pool.sd = FALSE)
#> 	Pairwise comparisons using t tests with non-pooled SD
#>
#> data:  pay$salary and pay$dept
#>
#>             Support Operations
#> Operations  0.91    -
#> Engineering 0.14    0.14
#>
#> P value adjustment method: holm

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Engineering against Support is 0.14 and against Operations is 0.14, and Support against Operations is 0.91. Nothing clears 0.05, which is the same verdict the Welch omnibus gave at 0.108. The two agree because they treat the spreads the same way.



Tip
The proper named test for this job is Games-Howell, which uses Welch-type standard errors and pair-specific degrees of freedom and compares them against the studentized range distribution. pairwise.t.test(pool.sd = FALSE) uses the t distribution instead and is a close stand-in that needs no extra package. Whichever you pick, never follow a Welch's ANOVA with a pooled post-hoc such as Tukey's.







How to report a Welch's ANOVA



Two things go in the write-up. They are the group summaries, so a reader can see the spreads for themselves, and the test line. Both come straight out of objects you already have.




  

    
RInteractive R

            rownames(dept_stats), dept_stats$n, dept_stats$mean, dept_stats$sd),
    sep = "")
cat(sprintf("Welch F(%.0f, %.2f) = %.2f, p = %.3f\n",
            welch_fit$parameter[1], welch_fit$parameter[2],
            welch_fit$statistic, welch_fit$p.value))
#> Support      n = 24, mean =  62582, sd =   6420
#> Operations   n = 24, mean =  62800, sd =   6282
#> Engineering  n = 12, mean =  78748, sd =  24650
#> Welch F(2, 24.15) = 2.44, p = 0.108

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





welch_fit$parameter holds the two degrees of freedom, welch_fit$statistic holds the F, and welch_fit$p.value holds the p-value, so the line rebuilds itself if the data changes.



In prose, that becomes one sentence: Welch's ANOVA found no reliable difference in mean salary across the three departments, F(2, 24.15) = 2.44, p = .108.



Warning
Write the denominator degrees of freedom exactly as it comes out, to two decimals. F(2, 24) is a different claim from F(2, 24.15), and rounding it away hides the one number that tells your reader you did not assume equal spreads.



One more line is worth adding so a reader knows why you chose it. Say that Welch's version was used because the group spreads differ, and give the largest-to-smallest variance ratio, which here is 15.4.

Quick check: which claim about Welch's ANOVA holds up?










That is it, and both halves matter. The weights fix which department gets believed, and the shrunken degrees of freedom charge the test for how unequal those weights are.
Welch's ANOVA never touches the salaries, never needs equal group sizes, and is not free: on equal spreads it gives up about three points of power. Nor is the classic test always too eager. With the noisy department as the biggest group it went the other way and called a difference only 2.15 times in 100. What Welch's does is weight each department by its own spread and shrink the denominator degrees of freedom to match.

Quick recap



You started with two tests disagreeing about sixty salaries and finished with a measured reason to believe one of them. To summarize:



    


  • The classic F-test rests on one pooled spread, 12,240 here, which described none of the three departments and handed Engineering a standard error half the size its own twelve salaries supported.
    • 


  • On 2,000 companies where all three departments paid the same, the classic test called a difference 19 times in 100 and Welch's called one 4 times in 100. The 5 in 100 was what both had promised.
    • 


  • Which department is short-staffed decides the direction of the damage. Noisy and small pushed the classic test up to 19 in 100, and noisy and large pulled it down to 2 in 100, where it starts missing real gaps.
    • 


  • Welch's ANOVA is oneway.test(y ~ g, var.equal = FALSE). It weights each group by its own variance and reports a fractional denominator df, 24.148 in place of 57, which is the price of the protection.
    • 


  • Follow it with pairwise.t.test(pool.sd = FALSE) or Games-Howell, never with Tukey's HSD, and report the fractional df as it comes: Welch's F(2, 24.15) = 2.44, p = .108.
    • 





On equal spreads it costs about three points of power. On unequal spreads it saves you from being wrong 19 times in 100. That is why plenty of statisticians simply run it every time, and now you have seen the numbers that make the argument.
