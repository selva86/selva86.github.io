Quiz

Question 1 of 10

What a censored row means

A patient enrolls, attends every visit, and is still alive when the study closes at 18 months. Their row reads time = 18, status = 0. What does it tell you about their survival time?

Correct: right-censoring means the event had not happened by last contact, so all you know is T > 18. That "at least" is real, usable information.
You saw that they survived at least 18 months, which constrains the estimate; that is exactly what Kaplan-Meier uses.
Question 2 of 10

The survival function and the hazard

S(t) is the survival function and h(t) is the hazard. Which pair of statements is correct?

Correct: S(t) is a probability that starts at 1 and never rises; the hazard is a rate per unit time (a speedometer for risk), not a probability.
The median is where S(t) crosses 0.5, not where the hazard hits any particular value.
Question 3 of 10

Reading a Kaplan-Meier curve

On a Kaplan-Meier curve you see a vertical tick where the curve stays perfectly flat. What does that tick represent?

Correct: a death makes the curve step down; a tick with no drop is a censoring. The patient holds the curve up while observed, then exits the risk pool.
Ticks mark censoring events, not interval boundaries.
Question 4 of 10

What the log-rank test says

A log-rank test comparing two treatment arms returns p = 0.02. Which reading is correct?

Correct: that is what a log-rank p-value means, evidence against the null of identical survival, with the size of the effect left to a model like Cox.
A non-significant test never proves equality; it just fails to detect a difference.
Question 5 of 10

What a hazard ratio means

A Cox model reports a hazard ratio of 0.35 for a new drug versus standard care. Which statement is correct?

Correct: the hazard ratio multiplies the instantaneous risk. 0.35 means roughly a third of the baseline hazard at every time, which pushes the survival curve up.
HR is a rate multiplier, not a per-patient probability of failure.
Question 6 of 10

The proportional-hazards assumption

A single Cox hazard ratio summarizes a two-year trial in one number. What does the proportional-hazards assumption commit you to, and how would you check it?

Correct: proportional hazards means one fixed multiplier for the whole period. A trend in the Schoenfeld residuals (small cox.zph p) flags a violation, where the effect drifts over time.
If the hazards cross, one constant ratio is a fiction; that is exactly why you test the assumption.
Question 7 of 10

Parametric and AFT models

You fit a Weibull survreg model and its accelerated-failure-time coefficient exponentiates to a time ratio of 1.6 for the new drug; Lesson 3's Cox model gave a hazard ratio of 0.36 for the same drug. A colleague says the two models disagree. What do you tell her?

Correct: for the Weibull family the AFT and PH views are the same model. The time ratio 1.6 converts to a hazard ratio near 0.36, matching Cox; they run in opposite directions by construction.
Committing to a shape is a trade-off, not an error; when the shape fits (check it against KM), the parametric model agrees with Cox and can extrapolate beyond the data.
Question 8 of 10

Competing risks

In a transplant cohort you estimate the cumulative incidence of relapse by treating every death-in-remission as a censored observation and reading 1 - S_KM(t). Why is that relapse estimate too high?

Correct: a death in remission ends any chance of relapse, but censoring keeps the patient eligible, so 1 - KM over-attributes relapses. Summed across causes, the naive curves can exceed 100%. The CIF weights each cause by the survivors truly at risk.
The time scale is fine; the error is treating a competing event as censoring.
Question 9 of 10

Cause-specific vs Fine-Gray

A new supportive-care protocol sharply cuts death-in-remission but does nothing to the biology of relapse (the cause-specific relapse hazard is unchanged). Compared with before, what happens to the two relapse models?

Correct: the rate among those still at risk is untouched (cause-specific), but fewer competing deaths means more patients survive to relapse, raising the CIF and moving the Fine-Gray HR. Same rate, different incidence.
It is the reverse: the cause-specific hazard is fixed by assumption; the cumulative incidence, which Fine-Gray targets, is what a change in competing mortality moves.
Question 10 of 10

Scoring a survival model

Model A has the higher C-index; Model B has the lower (better) time-dependent Brier score at 24 months, both on the same held-out patients. What is the most accurate reading?

Correct: the C-index rewards getting the ORDER right; the Brier score rewards getting the NUMBERS right. They can disagree, so report both and choose by the decision at hand.
They measure different things: discrimination versus calibration/accuracy. Disagreement is expected.
Run it: Kaplan-Meier and the log-rank test

Two arms, one test

Build two arms with different survival, estimate their Kaplan-Meier medians, and test the gap with the log-rank test.

RInteractive R
library(survival) set.seed(1) n <- 60 arm <- factor(rep(c("A", "B"), each = 30)) t <- rexp(n, ifelse(arm == "B", 0.05, 0.12)) # arm B has the lower hazard (lives longer) cen <- runif(n, 0, 30) time <- pmin(t, cen) status <- as.integer(t <= cen) # 1 = event, 0 = censored survfit(Surv(time, status) ~ arm) # median survival per arm survdiff(Surv(time, status) ~ arm) # the log-rank test

  

  





Arm B's median (about 15 months) is roughly triple arm A's (about 5), and the log-rank test returns p near 0.007: the curves genuinely differ.







Run it: a Cox hazard ratio and its C-index


Effect size, then how well it ranks



Fit a Cox model on a single predictor, read its hazard ratio, and score how well it ranks patients with Harrell's C-index.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
library(survival)
set.seed(2)
n <- 200
x <- rnorm(n)
t <- rexp(n, 0.05 * exp(0.8 * x))                 # higher x means more hazard
cen <- runif(n, 0, 40)
time   <- pmin(t, cen)
status <- as.integer(t <= cen)
cox <- coxph(Surv(time, status) ~ x)
round(exp(coef(cox)), 2)                           # hazard ratio per 1-unit x
round(concordance(cox)$concordance, 3)             # C-index: fraction of pairs ranked correctly

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Each unit of x multiplies the hazard by about 2.9, and the model's C-index of about 0.75 says it orders which patient fails first correctly three times out of four.
