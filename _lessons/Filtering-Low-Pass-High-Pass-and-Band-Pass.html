Low-pass, high-pass and band-pass filtering

Today let's understand filtering: how to choose which frequencies of a series survive, and which ones get erased.

Northside Diner is a lunch restaurant that logged how many lunch covers it served every day for ten weeks, seventy days in all, averaging 119.2 a day with a standard deviation of 25.4. Two real cycles sit hidden inside that series: one repeating every 7 days, at frequency 1/7, and a smaller one repeating every 3.5 days, at frequency 2/7. Neither cycle is visible by eye in the raw line below.

Toggle between line and point to look at that raw shape one more time. Everything from here changes what this line looks like: keeping its slow rise and fall, keeping only its jitter, or keeping just a narrow slice in between.

A filter is a weighted average of nearby values

Rebuild Northside Diner's series first, so every step in this lesson works from the same real numbers.

RInteractive R
# Rebuild Northside Diner's 70 days of lunch covers set.seed(2024) day <- 1:70 baseline <- 120 fundamental <- 30 * cos(2 * pi * (day - 5) / 7) harmonic <- 12 * cos(4 * pi * (day - 5) / 7) noise <- rnorm(70, 0, 10) northside_covers <- round(baseline + fundamental + harmonic + noise) round(mean(northside_covers), 1) round(sd(northside_covers), 1) #> [1] 119.2 #> [1] 25.4

  

  





A filter turns a series into a new series where every output value is a weighted sum of nearby input values. The simplest filter is a moving average: replace each day's value with the average of that day and some of its neighbours. In base R, filter() builds one directly. Its second argument is the vector of weights, and its third argument, sides, decides where the window sits relative to each day. sides = 2 centers the window: each output uses one day before, the day itself, and one day after. sides = 1 trails it: each output uses only the day itself and days before it, never a day that has not happened yet.



Build a 3-day centered moving average, giving equal weight to a day and each of its two neighbours.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Build a 3-day centered moving average with filter()
ma3 <- filter(northside_covers, rep(1/3, 3), sides = 2)

round(ma3[1:8], 2)
#> [1]     NA 105.33 113.33 136.33 152.33 143.67 118.67  98.33

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





rep(1/3, 3) hands filter() three equal weights, each 1/3, so every output is a plain average of three days, not a scaled-up or scaled-down sum. Day 1 comes back NA, since a centered 3-day window needs a day before day 1, and there isn't one. Day 5 comes back 152.33, the average of days 4, 5 and 6: (134 + 174 + 149) / 3.







The 3-day moving average as a low-pass filter



Compare the spread of the raw series with the spread of its 3-day average, then look at what happened to the busiest stretch of days.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compare spread before and after smoothing, and look at the Friday peak
round(sd(northside_covers), 2)
round(sd(ma3, na.rm = TRUE), 2)
northside_covers[5:9]
round(ma3[5:9], 2)
#> [1] 25.36
#> [1] 16.9
#> [1] 174 149 108  99  88
#> [1] 152.33 143.67 118.67  98.33  92.67

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The standard deviation fell from 25.36 to 16.9. Averaging over 3 days pulls in the fast, day-to-day jitter, the noise that has no real pattern, while the slower weekly rise and fall survives underneath it. Days 5 through 9, Northside Diner's Friday peak and its decline, still show the same shape after smoothing: 152.33 down to 92.67, instead of 174 down to 88, smaller swings but the same rise and fall.



A filter that keeps a series' slow movement and damps its fast swings like this is called a low-pass filter: low frequencies pass through mostly intact, high frequencies get reduced. Toggle between line and point below to see the raw series and its 3-day average together across all 70 days.









The frequency response: how much of each frequency survives



Every linear filter has what is called a frequency response: a curve that says, for each frequency, how much of a wave at that frequency comes out the other side. The height of that curve at a given frequency is the filter's gain there. A gain of 1 means a wave at that frequency survives at full strength. A gain of 0 means it disappears completely. A gain of 0.5 means it comes out at half its original strength.



For an N-day centered moving average, the gain at frequency \(f\) works out to this formula.



\[ \text{gain}(f) = \left| \frac{\sin(N \pi f)}{N \sin(\pi f)} \right| \]



Compute it for the 3-day average at Northside Diner's two real frequencies, and at the fastest frequency a daily series can show, 0.5 cycles a day.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compute the 3-day average's gain at the two real cycles and at 0.5
gain_ma <- function(f, N) abs(sin(pi * f * N) / (N * sin(pi * f)))

round(gain_ma(1/7, 3), 3)
round(gain_ma(2/7, 3), 3)
round(gain_ma(0.5, 3), 3)
#> [1] 0.749
#> [1] 0.185
#> [1] 0.333

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





At frequency 1/7, the 3-day average's gain is 0.749: the weekly cycle keeps about three quarters of its strength. At 2/7, gain drops to 0.185: the faster of the two real cycles loses most of its strength. At 0.5, the fastest frequency a daily series can show, gain is only 0.333. That is the low-pass shape in numbers: gain highest at the lowest frequency, falling as frequency climbs.



Here is that same gain, plotted across every frequency this 70-day series can show, 1/70 up to 0.5.









The window-length gotcha: when a low-pass filter erases the wrong cycle



The gain formula from the last step has a sharp edge: whenever frequency times window length, \(f \times N\), lands exactly on a whole number, \(\sin(N \pi f)\) is exactly 0, and so is the gain. Northside Diner's own window length, 7 days, does exactly that to both of its real cycles: \(7 \times 1/7 = 1\) and \(7 \times 2/7 = 2\), both whole numbers.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Check the 7-day average's gain at the two real cycles, and its spread
ma7 <- filter(northside_covers, rep(1/7, 7), sides = 2)

round(sd(ma7, na.rm = TRUE), 2)
round(gain_ma(1/7, 7), 4)
round(gain_ma(2/7, 7), 4)
#> [1] 4.19
#> [1] 0
#> [1] 0

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Both gains come back exactly 0. A 7-day average does not merely smooth Northside Diner's weekly cycle, it erases it: the standard deviation collapses from 25.36 all the way to 4.19, an almost flat line. Toggle between line and point below to see the difference: the 3-day average kept the Friday peak's shape, but the 7-day average removes it entirely, not just softens it.









Quick check: reading a low-pass filter's blind spot



A moving average's gain hits exactly 0 only when frequency times window length lands on a whole number, the same rule that made the 7-day average erase both of Northside Diner's real cycles.








Right. 14 x 1/7 = 2 and 14 x 2/7 = 4, both whole numbers, so a 14-day average has gain exactly 0 at both frequencies. A 9-day or 5-day average only damps the cycles, since neither 9 nor 5 times 1/7 lands on a whole number.
A moving average's gain hits exactly 0 only when frequency times window length is a whole number: 14 x 1/7 = 2 and 14 x 2/7 = 4 both qualify, so a 14-day average erases the weekly shape completely. Neither 5 x 1/7 nor 9 x 1/7 is a whole number, so those window lengths only damp the cycles, they never erase them.









Differencing as a high-pass filter



filter() is not the only way to build a filter. Differencing, diff(), subtracting each day's value from the one that follows it, is a filter too, and a very different one from a moving average.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Difference the series, and compare its spread with the raw series
d1 <- diff(northside_covers)

round(sd(northside_covers), 2)
round(sd(d1), 2)
#> [1] 25.36
#> [1] 28.05

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The differenced series is louder than the raw one, 28.05 against 25.36. A moving average shrank the spread; differencing grew it. Its gain formula is simpler than a moving average's.



\[ \text{gain}(f) = 2 \sin(\pi f) \]




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compute differencing's gain near frequency 0, at the two real cycles, and at 0.5
gain_diff <- function(f) 2 * sin(pi * f)

round(gain_diff(1/70), 3)
round(gain_diff(1/7), 3)
round(gain_diff(2/7), 3)
round(gain_diff(0.5), 3)
#> [1] 0.09
#> [1] 0.868
#> [1] 1.564
#> [1] 2

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





At the lowest frequency this series can show, 1/70, differencing's gain is only 0.09: slow movement barely survives. At 0.5, the fastest frequency, gain reaches 2: fast movement is amplified, not just passed through. At Northside Diner's two real cycles, gain is 0.868 at 1/7 and 1.564 at 2/7, both grown past their original strength. A filter that damps low frequencies and boosts high ones like this is called a high-pass filter, the opposite of the low-pass moving average.



Toggle between line and point to see how much jumpier the differenced series looks next to the raw one.









Comparing a low-pass curve with a high-pass curve



Put the 3-day average's gain curve and differencing's gain curve side by side, at the same frequency, 1/7.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Compare the low-pass and high-pass gains at the same frequency
fseq <- (1:35) / 70
gain_lowpass <- gain_ma(fseq, 3)
gain_highpass <- gain_diff(fseq)

round(c(low_pass = gain_lowpass[10], high_pass = gain_highpass[10]), 3)
#>  low_pass high_pass 
#>     0.749     0.868 

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





Read the two curves' overall shape rather than one frequency at a time. The 3-day average's gain starts near 1 at the lowest frequency and slides down toward 0 as frequency rises: it lets slow movement through and blocks fast movement, the low-pass shape. Differencing's gain does the opposite: it starts near 0 at the lowest frequency and climbs toward 2 as frequency rises: it blocks slow movement and lets fast movement through, the high-pass shape.



Toggle between line and point to see both curves plotted together.









Building a band-pass filter from two moving averages



Build a much slower moving average, one wide enough to smooth away both of Northside Diner's real cycles, and check its own gain first.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Build a 15-day average and check its gain at the two real cycles and near 0
ma15 <- filter(northside_covers, rep(1/15, 15), sides = 2)

round(gain_ma(1/7, 15), 3)
round(gain_ma(2/7, 15), 3)
round(gain_ma(1/70, 15), 4)
#> [1] 0.067
#> [1] 0.067
#> [1] 0.9265

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The 15-day average damps both real cycles down to about 7% of their strength, 0.067 at each, but at 1/70, the lowest frequency this series can show, its gain is still 0.9265: slow drift passes through almost untouched. So the 15-day average is just another low-pass filter, one with a much lower cutoff than the 3-day average.



Subtract that slow average from the fast one, and what is left is only what the fast average sees but the slow one does not: a band of frequencies in between, neither the slowest nor the fastest.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Build the band-pass filter and rebuild the true combined wave to check it against
band <- ma3 - ma15
wave_7day <- 30 * cos(2 * pi * (day - 5) / 7)
wave_35day <- 12 * cos(4 * pi * (day - 5) / 7)
true_cycle <- wave_7day + wave_35day

round(sd(band, na.rm = TRUE), 2)
round(sd(true_cycle), 2)
round(cor(band, true_cycle, use = "complete.obs"), 3)
#> [1] 15.38
#> [1] 23.01
#> [1] 0.879

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





true_cycle is the two real cycles added together with no noise at all, buildable here only because this series was simulated and its exact formula is known. band, the filter's actual output, correlates with it at 0.879, close but not perfect: band still carries some leftover noise the two moving averages did not fully remove. This kind of filter, one that blocks both very slow and very fast movement but lets a middle band through, is called a band-pass filter. Its gain follows directly from the two averages' own gains at each frequency: 0.749 minus 0.067 is 0.682 at 1/7, and 0.185 minus 0.067 is 0.118 at 2/7, both far from 0 but nowhere near the 3-day average's gain on its own.



Toggle between line and point to see how closely band tracks the true combined wave.









The phase shift a causal filter pays for



Build the same 3-day average again, but trailing this time, using only each day and the two before it, then compare both windows around Northside Diner's first Friday peak.




  

    
RInteractive R

    
# Build a trailing 3-day average and compare it with the centered one
ma3_trail <- filter(northside_covers, rep(1/3, 3), sides = 1)

data.frame(day = 1:12, raw = northside_covers[1:12],
           ma3 = round(ma3[1:12], 2), ma3_trail = round(ma3_trail[1:12], 2))
#>    day raw    ma3 ma3_trail
#> 1    1 110     NA        NA
#> 2    2 105 105.33        NA
#> 3    3 101 113.33    105.33
#> 4    4 134 136.33    113.33
#> 5    5 174 152.33    136.33
#> 6    6 149 143.67    152.33
#> 7    7 108 118.67    143.67
#> 8    8  99  98.33    118.67
#> 9    9  88  92.67     98.33
#> 10  10  91  99.33     92.67
#> 11  11 119 125.67     99.33
#> 12  12 167 143.33    125.67

    

      
      
    

    

  

  





The raw series peaks on day 5, at 174, the real Friday. The centered ma3 peaks there too, at 152.33, matching the true wave's own peak day exactly. But ma3_trail's peak in this same stretch lands on day 6, at that identical value, 152.33, one full day later. The reason is mechanical: ma3_trail on day 6 averages days 4, 5 and 6, exactly the same three days ma3 already averaged and reported on day 5. Trailing simply reports that average a day late, on the day the last of its three inputs arrived, instead of on the middle day.



This kind of delay, where a filter's output has the right shape but arrives late, is called a phase shift. A trailing filter only ever uses values it already has, so it is called a causal filter, one that could genuinely run in real time: reporting each day's smoothed value using only that day and the past. A centered filter needs a value from the future relative to the day it reports on, so it can only be computed after the fact, never live. For a window of \(N\) days, a trailing average lags a centered average of the same window by \((N-1)/2\) days: \((3-1)/2 = 1\) day here.



Toggle between line and point to see that one-day lag repeat across the whole series.









Closing quiz: reading a filter's frequency response



A different filter has gain 0.92 at a low frequency and gain 0.05 at a high frequency, the low-pass shape from a few steps back. Run this filter on Northside Diner's series as a 9-day centered average and again as a 9-day trailing average.









Right, all three pieces. Gain near 1 at a low frequency and near 0 at a high one is the low-pass shape, the opposite of differencing's high-pass curve. A moving average's gain hits exactly 0 whenever frequency times window length is a whole number, and 9 x 1/9 = 1, so a 9-day average would erase a cycle at frequency 1/9 completely. And a trailing filter only sees past and current values, so it always lags a centered filter of the same window by (N-1)/2 days, 4 days here.
Check each piece on its own. The low-pass shape is gain near 1 at low frequency falling to near 0 at high frequency, the opposite of the high-pass curve differencing produces. A moving average's gain hits exactly 0 whenever frequency times window length lands on a whole number, not merely damped, so a 9-day average erases a cycle at exactly frequency 1/9. And a trailing filter, since it only sees past and current values, is always the one that lags: it delays its peak by (N-1)/2 days behind a centered filter of the same window, never the reverse.

Quick recap



You can now look at a linear filter and know exactly which frequencies survive after it runs, and what running it live actually costs. To summarize:



    


  • A moving average is a low-pass filter: gain near 1 at low frequency, falling toward 0 at high frequency. Pick the wrong window length, though, and its gain can hit exactly 0 on a real cycle, erasing it completely rather than merely smoothing it, the way a 7-day average erased Northside Diner's own weekly cycle.
    • 


  • Differencing is a high-pass filter: gain near 0 at low frequency, climbing toward 2 at the fastest frequency a daily series can show.
    • 


  • Subtracting a slower moving average from a faster one builds a band-pass filter: near 0 at both the lowest and highest frequencies, higher in between, 0.682 and 0.118 on Northside Diner's two real cycles.
    • 


  • A trailing, causal filter can run in real time, but it lags a centered filter of the same window by (N-1)/2 days. A centered filter has no such lag, but it needs future values it cannot have live.
    •
