This chapter is a CAPSTONE PROJECT, not a tutorial. The reader must finish it
having watched a complete, industry-grade retail demand forecasting engagement
run end to end, at the standard a consulting team would present to a client.
Both a technical reviewer and a business stakeholder must find it publish-worthy.

Mandatory arc (each phase is a top-level section with real code and real outputs):

1. BUSINESS BRIEF. Open with a concrete engagement: a named (realistic, fictional)
   retailer, the decision the forecast feeds (weekly store ordering / safety
   stock), and the cost of getting it wrong in currency terms (stock-outs vs
   markdowns). One short paragraph a category manager could read alone.
2. DATA AUDIT. Structure, coverage, gaps, calendar irregularities, outliers and
   suspected promo spikes, with the checks a practitioner runs BEFORE modeling.
   Use a real dataset (aus_retail or similar tsibble data); if promotions/price
   are not in the data, engineer a defensible proxy and say so honestly.
3. RICH EDA. Minimum three publication-grade plots, each followed by an
   interpretation paragraph that changes a modeling decision: trend + seasonal
   strength (STL), seasonal shape across years (gg_season / gg_subseries),
   and a driver relationship (e.g. December effect, promo lift). Every figure
   has a title, labeled axes, and an annotation of at least one key event.
4. STRATEGY PORTFOLIO. Fit at least FIVE genuinely different strategies, each
   introduced with WHY it is a plausible candidate for THIS problem:
   seasonal naive (the honest benchmark), ETS, ARIMA, regression with
   covariates (TSLM or dynamic regression with trend/season/promo terms), and
   a combination/ensemble of the best performers. Deterministic fits preferred;
   set.seed() before anything random.
5. HONEST TOURNAMENT. Rolling-origin cross-validation over multiple origins,
   compared across horizons (e.g. 1, 4, 8, 12 weeks ahead). Present a rich
   results table (MASE, RMSE, MAPE by model and horizon) plus one comparison
   figure, then read the table aloud in prose: who wins where, who collapses at
   long horizons, and why. Include interval quality (empirical coverage of the
   80/95% intervals or a Winkler score), not just point accuracy.
6. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY FOR STAKEHOLDERS. A section a non-technical reader can
   read in isolation: the recommendation in one sentence, expected business
   impact quantified (e.g. forecast error reduction translated into stock-out
   or markdown savings), a small results table in business language (no MASE:
   "average miss per week in units/dollars"), the decision rule handed to
   operations, and the top three caveats.
7. PRODUCTION NOTES. What to monitor after go-live (link to the Forecast
   Monitoring chapter), when to refit, and the one-line hierarchy note that
   store/SKU forecasts need reconciliation (link to that chapter).

Presentation rules: no output dump without an interpretation sentence; every
table is introduced and then summarized in prose; figures are styled for
publication, not defaults-only. The Complete Example section at the end
compresses the whole engagement into one continuous runnable script.
