VIF / Multicollinearity Calculator

When two predictors in a regression move together, their coefficients get unstable and hard to trust. The variance inflation factor (VIF) measures how much each coefficient's variance is blown up by that overlap. Paste the car::vif() output you already have, or a predictor correlation matrix, and get per-predictor VIF, tolerance, standard-error inflation, the condition number and a plain verdict. VIF in three minutes

    
One number, one question: is this predictor telling you anything the others do not?

    
When two predictors carry nearly the same information (height in inches and height in centimetres is the extreme case), the model cannot tell whose effect is whose. It splits the credit between them almost at random, so both coefficients wobble from sample to sample and their standard errors balloon.

    
The variance inflation factor measures that wobble. For predictor j it is 1 / (1 - R²), where R² comes from regressing predictor j on all the others. A VIF of 1 means no overlap. A VIF of 9 means nine times the coefficient variance you would have with none, so a standard error three times larger (SE grows with the square root of VIF). Tolerance is the same fact upside down: 1 / VIF.

    

      
VIF < 5
Low. The predictor stands mostly on its own; read its coefficient normally.
no action

      
VIF 5 to 10
Moderate. Enough overlap to inflate the standard error noticeably; watch this coefficient.
keep an eye on it

      
VIF > 10
Serious. The coefficient is unstable and its p-value unreliable; drop, combine or regularize.
fix it

    

    
These cutoffs are conventions, not laws, which is why you can move them above. In a large well-controlled study a VIF of 8 may be fine; in a small exploratory model a VIF of 4 might already worry you.

NumberWhat it isHow to read it

      
VIFVariance inflation factor, diag(solve(R))1 is ideal; bigger is worse. It is the multiplier on the coefficient's variance.

      
Tolerance1 / VIFShare of the predictor's variance that is its own. Below 0.10 mirrors VIF above 10.

When VIF is the wrong tool

    

      
SituationWhy VIF misleadsDo instead

      
Prediction-only modelCollinearity does not hurt predictive accuracy, only the interpretation of individual coefficients.Ignore VIF; judge the model on out-of-sample error.

      
Interaction or polynomial termsx and x², or x·z, are correlated by construction, so a high VIF is expected and harmless.Center the variables first, or read the VIFs of the base terms only.

      
Categorical predictorsRaw VIF on individual dummy columns is not comparable across factors with different numbers of levels.Use the GVIF and its GVIF^(1/(2·Df)) adjustment (this tool does it for you).

      
Perfect dummy trapKeeping every level of a factor plus the intercept makes a column an exact combination of others; VIF is infinite.Drop one reference level, or let R do it by using factor().

    

  


  

    
Frequently asked questions

    
What is a good VIF value?
The common conventions: below 5 is low, 5 to 10 is moderate, and above 10 points to serious multicollinearity worth addressing. A VIF of exactly 1 means the predictor is uncorrelated with the others. Because the right cutoff depends on your sample size and how central the coefficient is to your argument, the thresholds here are adjustable.

    
What does VIF actually measure?
It is the factor by which the variance of a coefficient is inflated because that predictor overlaps with the others. VIF for predictor j is 1 divided by (1 minus the R-squared from regressing predictor j on the remaining predictors). A VIF of 9 means nine times the variance you would have with no overlap, hence a standard error three times larger.

    
VIF, tolerance or condition number: which should I report?
VIF and tolerance (its reciprocal) are per-predictor and tell you which coefficient is affected. The condition number is a single whole-model figure that can catch collinearity spread across several predictors that no individual VIF flags. Reporting the maximum VIF plus the condition number covers both angles.

    
How do I fix a high VIF?
In rough order of effort: drop one of the redundant predictors; combine correlated predictors into a single index or principal component; center variables before forming interaction or polynomial terms; or move to a method that tolerates collinearity, such as ridge regression. If the model only needs to predict, high VIF is usually not a problem at all.

    
What is GVIF?
For a categorical predictor spread over several dummy columns, car::vif() reports a generalized VIF along with its degrees of freedom. To compare it against the ordinary VIF thresholds you square the last column, GVIF^(1/(2 times Df)). Paste a GVIF table into the calculator and it does the squaring and flagging for you.

    
Does this calculator match R?
Yes. The variance inflation factors are computed as the diagonal of the inverse correlation matrix, which equals car::vif(), and the condition number equals kappa(scale(X), exact = TRUE). Both are verified against R 4.6.0 across every mode and edge case, including perfect collinearity, to at least six decimal places.
