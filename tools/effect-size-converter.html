Effect Size Calculator + Converter

Live conversion as you type · d ↔ r ↔ η² ↔ OR ↔ RR ↔ NNT · R code emitted alongside · Runs in your browser

Compute an effect size from raw data, or convert between every common measure. Cohen's d for groups, r for correlations, η² for ANOVA, odds and risk ratios for binary outcomes — all computed and translated for you. We tell you what the magnitude *means*, not just the number.

Worked examples

Two classes received different teaching methods; you want to know how much of an effect the new method had on test scores.

📏 Typical d in this domain: 0.2–0.4 small to moderate ⚠️ Watch for: Cohen's "medium" is too large for most education effects
How effect sizes work
What it is

When you find a difference between two groups, "is it real?" and "is it big?" are completely different questions. The p-value answers "is it real?" An effect size answers "is it big?"

Effect size puts the difference into standardized units that don't depend on the scale you measured on. That way you can compare results from studies in dollars vs. euros, or 5-point vs. 7-point scales, or minutes vs. hours. It's also the currency of meta-analysis (combining studies) and power analysis (planning sample sizes).

How it works

Different study designs naturally produce different effect-size measures. Each one is just "how big is the difference, in standardized terms":

• Two groups with means and SDs → Cohen's d = how many SDs apart the means are
• A correlation between two numbers → r (the correlation coefficient itself)
• A 2×2 table of yes/no outcomes → odds ratio (OR) or risk ratio (RR)
• Two proportions → Cohen's h
• ANOVA designs → eta-squared (η²)

These are all measuring "how big," but on different scales. Under reasonable assumptions, you can convert between them — d to r, OR to d, etc. We compute every translation and show them side by side.

What you put in

Either:

• Raw summary stats from your study — means, SDs, group sizes, correlations, or the four cells of a 2×2 table.
• An existing effect size you want to convert — like a Cohen's d someone reported in a paper that you want as an odds ratio for your meta-analysis.

What you get out

The same effect, expressed in every standard form: Cohen's d, Hedges' g (a small-sample-corrected version of d), r, odds ratio, risk ratio, Cohen's h. Each one comes with a "small/medium/large" label per Cohen's 1988 conventions and the formula used to compute it.

How to interpret it in practice

Cohen's small/medium/large labels (d = 0.2 / 0.5 / 0.8) are anchors, not verdicts. They were calibrated for psychology in 1988. In your field they may mean different things. In education research, a d of 0.2 is often a meaningful intervention worth investing in. In drug trials, the same d might be too weak to matter.

Always translate effect size back into your domain's natural scale. An odds ratio of 1.5 sounds modest, but it depends on the baseline:

• Baseline rate is 1%: an OR of 1.5 means going from 1% to 1.5% — barely visible
• Baseline rate is 50%: an OR of 1.5 means going from 50% to 60% — a much bigger deal

Effect size should always come with uncertainty. A small effect with a tight confidence interval is real but possibly unimportant. A big effect with a wide CI might be exciting but you need more data to confirm.

When to use this

• Reading or writing a meta-analysis — effect sizes are how studies are combined
• Reporting your own results — always include effect size alongside the p-value
• Comparing your finding to prior work that used a different scale
• Power analysis — you need an expected effect size to plan sample size
• Translating between fields' conventions — clinicians like odds ratios, education people like d

Common pitfalls

Treating "small/medium/large" as universal verdicts. They aren't. Calibrate to what's typical in your field.

Reporting effect size without a confidence interval. A point estimate alone hides how much you know.

Confusing OR with RR when outcomes are common. When the event happens in more than ~10% of cases, OR overstates the relative risk. Use RR (or risk difference) instead.

Using Cohen's d on heavily skewed data. The conversions between d and other measures assume rough normality; with severe skew, the translation can mislead.

Anatomy of the answer

d = (M₂ − M₁) / s_pooled
Cohen's d. Effect in standard-deviation units. Pooled SD = √[((n₁−1)s₁² + (n₂−1)s₂²) / (n₁+n₂−2)]. Cohen's conventions: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large (1988).
g = d · J, J = 1 − 3 / (4(n₁+n₂) − 9)
Hedges' g. Small-sample correction. Use g instead of d when total n < 20 — d is biased upward at small n.
r = d / √(d² + 4) (equal n)
d ↔ r. Conversion accumulates rounding error if you chain through several measures, so we always convert directly from your input.
d = ln(OR) · √3 / π
RR = OR / (1 − p₁ + p₁·OR)
OR ↔ d, OR ↔ RR. The OR–d conversion assumes a logistic distribution. The OR–RR conversion depends on the base rate; OR alone doesn't.
h = 2 · (arcsin√p₂ − arcsin√p₁)
Cohen's h. For proportions. Unlike a raw difference, h corrects for the fact that going from 1% to 2% is much harder than from 50% to 51%.

When this is the wrong tool

If you have…
Use instead
Means + SDs but the SDs differ a lot
Glass's Δ (control SD only) — Cohen's d assumes roughly equal variances.
Raw data, not summary stats
effsize::cohen.d(g1, g2) in R — handles the small-sample correction automatically.
Repeated-measures (paired) design
dz or dav depending on inference target — see Lakens (2013).
Want a robust effect size (outlier-resistant)
Wilcox's robust d or a median-based measure — out of scope here.
