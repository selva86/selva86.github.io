How A/B testing works
What it is

You have two versions of something — your current website (A) and a new design (B). You want to know which one gets more people to click, sign up, or buy. An A/B test is the structured way to find out: show A to some users, B to others, count what happens, and decide if the difference you see is a real improvement or just random luck.

How it works

The trick is randomness. If you randomly assign each visitor to see A or B, then any difference at the end comes from one of two sources: your design change, or just chance. Statistics gives you a way to tell those two apart.

Before the test, decide three things:

1. The metric — what counts as "winning"? (Conversion rate, click-through, signup rate.)
2. The smallest improvement you'd care about — a 1% lift? 10%? Powering for tiny effects costs huge samples.
3. How careful you want to be — usually 95% confidence (willing to be wrong 1 time in 20) and 80% power (chance of catching a real effect).

These three numbers determine how many users you need in each group. Run the test until you hit that number, then compare.

What you put in

• Your current rate (e.g., 10 out of 100 visitors buy = 10%)
• The lift you want to be able to detect (e.g., a relative 12% improvement)
• Confidence and power levels — the defaults (95% / 80%) are fine 99% of the time
• After the test runs, plug in the actual results (successes and total visitors per version) to get the verdict

What you get out

Before the test: the sample size you need per version. ("You need ~7,800 visitors per group.")

After the test: the observed lift, a confidence interval for it, a p-value, and a Bayesian probability that B beats A — usually the easiest number to act on. ("96% probability B is better.")

How to interpret it in practice

The single most actionable number is "probability B beats A." It's intuitive — most people understand "96% confident" without needing a stats degree.

• Above 95% → ship B
• Below 80% → B isn't winning
• Between 80–95% → leaning toward B but not certain

Look at the confidence interval too. A range of [+0.5%, +5%] is a real win even though it's wide. A range of [−1%, +3%] crosses zero — your test was inconclusive (not a tie, just not enough data).

If results look "almost significant," it's tempting to run a few more days and check again. Don't. Peeking inflates false positives dramatically. Pick your sample size, run to that size, then look once.

When to use this

Anywhere you can randomly route visitors to one of two versions and measure a clear short-term outcome:

• Landing pages and signup flows
• Email subject lines
• Button colors, copy, layouts
• Pricing and packaging
• Recommendation algorithms

It doesn't work as well for slow-moving outcomes like annual retention or lifetime value, where you'd need other designs (switchback tests, holdouts, geo experiments).

Common pitfalls

Peeking and stopping early. Looking at the p-value mid-test and stopping when it dips below 0.05 multiplies your false-positive rate. Decide n upfront and stick to it.

Testing many variants without correction. Run 10 variants and one will look "significant" by chance even if all are identical. Use multiple-testing correction.

Changing too many things at once. If button color and copy and layout all change between A and B, you can't tell which one drove the result.

Calling an inconclusive test a "tie." Inconclusive doesn't mean "A = B"; it means "you don't have enough data to tell." Either pick the cheaper option or run longer.

What does the p-value mean here?
A p-value of 0.03 means: if the true conversion rates were identical, you'd see a difference at least this large by random chance about 3% of the time. A small p-value is evidence against "no difference," but it doesn't tell you how big the real difference is — that's what the confidence interval shows.

How is this different from the frequentist p-value?
Frequentist: "If A and B were truly identical, what's the chance I'd see a difference this big by accident?"
Bayesian: "Given my data, what's the chance B is genuinely better than A?"

The Bayesian view answers the question most people *think* they're getting from the p-value. Both views are valid; they just answer different questions. The credible interval has a clean interpretation: there's a 95% chance the true lift falls inside it (under our prior). 
# Bayesian via beta-binomial conjugate
# Prior Beta(1,1) is uniform / non-informative
set.seed(42)
post_a <- rbeta(1e5, 1 + 50, 1 + 1000 - 50)
post_b <- rbeta(1e5, 1 + 65, 1 + 1000 - 65)

# Probability B beats A
mean(post_b > post_a)

# 95% credible interval for the lift
quantile(post_b - post_a, c(0.025, 0.975))

Anatomy of the answer

h = 2 · (arcsin√p₂ − arcsin√p₁)
Cohen's h. An effect-size measure tailored to comparing two proportions. Unlike a raw difference, h corrects for the fact that going from 1% to 2% is much harder than from 50% to 51%.
n = (z1−α/2 + z1−β)² · [ p₁(1−p₁) + p₂(1−p₂) ] / (p₂ − p₁)²
Sample size. The standard two-proportion formula. Matches R's pwr::pwr.2p.test() exactly.
z = (p̂₂ − p̂₁) / √[ p̂(1−p̂) · (1/n₁ + 1/n₂) ]
Two-proportion z-test. No continuity correction by default. The 95% CI uses pooled variance under H₀ for the test and unpooled variance for the CI — matches prop.test(..., correct=FALSE).
pᵢ | data ~ Beta(α₀ + cᵢ, β₀ + nᵢ − cᵢ)
Beta-Binomial conjugate. A Beta(1,1) prior is uniform on [0,1]. After data, each variant's posterior is Beta(1 + conversions, 1 + non-conversions). We draw 100,000 samples per arm to compute P(B>A) and the credible interval. Matches rbeta() simulation in R.

When this is the wrong tool

If you have…
Use instead
Very small samples (n < 30 per variant)
Fisher's exact test — z-approximation breaks down at this scale.
Continuous outcomes (revenue, time-on-site)
Power Analysis tool — continuous data needs a t-test, not a proportion test.
Three or more variants
Multiple Testing Correction — or consider a multi-armed bandit.
You "peeked" at results mid-test
Sequential testing tutorial — standard p-values assume a single look at the end.
You want to control for covariates
CUPED tutorial — covariate-adjusted estimators reduce variance.
