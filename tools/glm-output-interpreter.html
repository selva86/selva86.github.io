glm() Output Interpreter

Logistic · Poisson · Gaussian · Gamma · quasi-families · Log odds → odds ratios in plain English · Runs in your browser

Paste the output of summary(glm(...)) from R; we detect the family and link, exponentiate where it helps, translate every coefficient (odds ratio, rate ratio, latent-scale effect, etc.), and flag family-specific traps — separation in logistic, overdispersion in Poisson, dispersion drift in quasi-families.

Worked examples
🚢 Titanic survival

A canonical logistic example: predicting survival on the Titanic from passenger class, sex, and age. All three predictors matter; the sex coefficient is the dominant one in odds-ratio terms.

📐 What you'll see: Sex (male) and class drop survival odds dramatically; age has a smaller but real effect. McFadden pseudo-R² ≈ 0.32.
How GLM output works
What it is

Linear regression (lm) works when the outcome is a regular continuous number — like price, weight, or temperature. But many real-world outcomes don't look like that:

• Yes/no outcomes: did the customer buy? did the patient survive? did the user click?
• Counts: how many tickets did this user file? how many defects per batch?
• Always-positive numbers: how long until churn? what dollar amount?

GLMs (generalized linear models) handle these. Logistic regression is for yes/no, Poisson is for counts, Gamma is for positive continuous. The output of summary(glm(...)) looks similar to linear regression — but coefficients are on a transformed scale, and reading them like ordinary regression is the classic beginner mistake.

How it works

A GLM has three pieces:

1. The family — the type of outcome (binomial for yes/no, Poisson for counts, Gaussian for continuous)
2. The linear part — same `β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + ...` as linear regression
3. The link function — what connects them

The link function is the key. It maps a transformed version of the outcome onto the linear scale.

• Logistic regression uses the logit link. Coefficients are log-odds. To get a meaningful number, exponentiate: exp(coefficient) = odds ratio. An odds ratio of 2.3 means "the odds of the outcome are 2.3× higher per unit increase in this variable."
• Poisson regression uses the log link. Coefficients are log-rates. Exponentiate to get the rate ratio. A rate ratio of 1.4 means "expected count is 40% higher per unit increase."

We auto-detect the family and link from your output and translate to meaningful numbers.

What you put in

Paste the verbatim output from summary(glm(formula, family=..., data=...)). The Call line, Coefficients table, Null/Residual deviance, AIC. We can override family detection if needed.

What you get out

For each coefficient, a family-aware plain-English read:

Logistic: "The odds of [outcome] are multiplied by 2.3 for every 1-unit increase in X (p = 0.001)."
Poisson: "The expected count is multiplied by 1.4 for every 1-unit increase in X."

Plus:

• McFadden pseudo-R² — the GLM equivalent of R²
• Likelihood-ratio test — the GLM equivalent of the F-test (does the whole model do anything?)
• Family-specific warnings — separation flag for logistic (model is unstable), overdispersion check for Poisson (standard errors are too small)

How to interpret it in practice

For logistic regression: focus on the odds ratio (the exponentiated coefficient), not the raw log-odds. Tell the story like:

"Smokers have 2.3× the odds of disease compared to non-smokers."

Not "the log-odds are 0.83 higher." Most audiences think in odds, not in log-odds. (And nobody outside stats class thinks in log-odds.)

If your outcome is rare (< ~10%), an odds ratio is roughly equal to a relative risk and the two readings agree. If the outcome is common, the odds ratio overstates the relative risk — be careful when you communicate it.

For Poisson regression: talk in rate ratios. "The expected count is 1.4× higher per unit of X."

About the pseudo-R²: McFadden's pseudo-R² of 0.2–0.4 is "very good" for logistic by standard benchmarks. Don't compare directly to OLS R² — they're on different scales. For logistic regression, R² = 0.30 is excellent, not mediocre.

About AIC: only useful for comparing nested models on the same dataset. By itself, it tells you nothing about whether your model is good.

Always check overdispersion in Poisson. If residual deviance / df is well above 1 (say, > 1.5), your SEs are too small and your "significant" findings are over-stated. Switch to quasipoisson or negative binomial (glm.nb()).

When to use this

• Logistic regression: binary outcomes — purchased / didn't, churned / didn't, click / no click
• Poisson regression: count data — calls per day, defects per item, accidents per intersection
• Negative binomial: count data with overdispersion
• Gamma regression: positive continuous outcomes — wait times, dollar amounts

Common pitfalls

Reading log-odds as probabilities. They aren't. To convert log-odds to a probability, use 1 / (1 + exp(-x)).

Forgetting to exponentiate. A coefficient of 0.83 on the log-odds scale is meaningless to readers. exp(0.83) = 2.3, "odds multiplied by 2.3," is meaningful.

Ignoring overdispersion in Poisson. Default Poisson assumes the variance equals the mean. If your data has higher variance, your standard errors are too small, and "significant" results aren't really significant.

Comparing AIC across different families or datasets. AIC only works within one family on one dataset.

What we look for in summary(glm)

Call: glm(formula = y ~ ..., family = binomial(link = "logit"), data = …)
The Call. We extract the family and link function from this line. Family + link decide whether each coefficient is read as a log odds, log rate, latent-scale shift, or just a plain effect.
Estimate Std. Error z value Pr(>|z|)
Coefficients table. For binomial(logit) we also show exp(estimate) as the odds ratio next to each row. For poisson(log) the same column holds the rate ratio. Plain-English read uses the multiplicative form ("odds are multiplied by 2.3").
Null deviance: D₀ on n − 1 df Residual deviance: D on n − k df
Deviances. Roughly the GLM analogue of total and residual SS. We compute McFadden pseudo-R² = 1 − D/D₀ and run a likelihood-ratio test of "any predictor matters" (χ² = D₀ − D, df = k − 1).
AIC: 2k − 2 log L
AIC. Useful for comparing nested or non-nested models on the same dataset; lower is better. By itself, AIC doesn't tell you whether the model is good — only whether it's better than another model.
Dispersion ≈ D / (n − k)
Dispersion check. For binomial/Poisson the assumed dispersion is 1. If residual deviance ≫ residual df (rule of thumb: ratio > 1.5), you have overdispersion — switch to quasi- or glm.nb(). We flag this automatically.
