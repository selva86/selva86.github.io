Power Analysis Calculator

Live recompute as you type · t-test, ANOVA, regression, correlation, χ² · Solve for n, power, or effect · R code emitted alongside

Plan how many subjects your study needs (or check whether the n you have is enough). Pick a design, fix two of the three quantities (effect size, sample size, power), and we'll compute the third — exactly the way R's pwr package does, with code emitted alongside.

Worked examples
🧪 Two-group experiment

A standard two-group between-subjects experiment: one group gets the treatment, one doesn't, you compare an outcome between them with a t-test.

📏 Typical d: 0.3–0.5 in social science ⚠️ Watch for: Don't compute power post-hoc from observed effect — it's circular
How power analysis works
What it is

Before running an experiment, you'd like to know: "if there really is an effect, will I have enough data to detect it?" That's what power measures. Specifically, power is the probability your study will spot a real effect of a given size, if one exists. The standard target is 80% — meaning 80% of the time you'd catch a real effect of that size, and 20% of the time you'd miss it.

Power analysis is the calculation that figures out how big your sample needs to be to reach that target — or, with the sample you have, what size effect you could realistically detect.

How it works

Four things are connected, and fixing any three determines the fourth:

1. Effect size — how big is the effect you want to detect (in standardized units like Cohen's d)
2. Sample size (n) — how many observations
3. Significance level (α) — false-positive tolerance, usually 5%
4. Power — true-positive rate, usually 80%

The intuition: bigger effects are easier to detect with smaller samples. Smaller effects need bigger samples. Stricter α (e.g., 1% instead of 5%) needs bigger samples. Higher power (e.g., 90% instead of 80%) needs bigger samples.

This tool lets you fix three values and solve for the fourth. Most often: fix effect size + α + power, solve for n ("how many do I need?"). Sometimes the reverse: fix n + α + power, solve for the effect size you could realistically detect.

What you put in

• The test you're planning (one-sample t-test, two-sample t-test, paired t, ANOVA, correlation, proportion comparison)
• The effect size that would matter to you — ideally from a pilot study or prior literature, not made up
• α (default 5%)
• Power (default 80%)
• Leave one of these blank — that's what we solve for

What you get out

The missing piece — usually the required sample size, but it could be the achievable power at a given n, or the smallest effect you could detect. We also draw a curve showing how the answer shifts as you wiggle the inputs, so you can see how sensitive your design is.

How to interpret it in practice

If the required sample size is way more than you can collect, you have three honest options:

• Reduce scope — focus on the single most important comparison, not five secondary ones
• Accept lower power and document it ("we have 60% power for our primary effect")
• Don't run the study — sometimes that's the right call

What you should not do: inflate the assumed effect size to make the sample size look feasible. Power for a fantasy effect tells you nothing useful.

If you've already run the study and got a non-significant result, don't compute "post-hoc power" based on the effect you observed — that's just rephrasing the p-value with extra steps. Instead, report the minimum detectable effect at your achieved n: "with our 50 participants, we could only have reliably detected effects of d ≥ 0.42." That's an honest answer to "what could we have seen?"

The 80% convention is just convention. For clinical trials, expensive interventions, or anything irreversible, bump it to 90% or 95% so you're less likely to miss a real effect.

When to use this

• Before any experiment — especially expensive or hard-to-rerun ones
• Grant proposals (often require formal power analysis)
• Pre-registration documents
• After a non-significant result — to communicate honestly what your study could and couldn't see
• Comparing study designs — within-subjects designs often need fewer participants than between-subjects designs, and power analysis tells you how many fewer

Common pitfalls

Plugging in an optimistic effect size to make n feasible. If you assume d = 0.8 but the truth is 0.3, your real power is much lower than calculated. Use realistic estimates from prior work.

Confusing α and power. α is the false-positive rate (the chance of "finding" something that isn't there). Power is the true-positive rate (the chance of finding something that is there). They control different errors.

Computing post-hoc power from your own data. It's mathematically just a function of your p-value — gives you no new information. Use minimum detectable effect instead.

Matching the wrong analysis. Power for a simple t-test ≠ power for the same data analyzed with a regression that controls for covariates. Match the analysis you'll actually run.

# Two-sample t-test power analysis
library(pwr)

pwr.t.test(
  d         = 0.5,
  power     = 0.80,
  sig.level = 0.05,
  type      = "two.sample",
  alternative = "two.sided"
)

Anatomy of the answer

power = P(reject H₀ | H₁ true)
Power. The probability your test correctly rejects a false null. It's a function of effect size, sample size, α, and the design. Increase any of effect, n, or α and power goes up.
ncp² = d² · n/2 (two-sample t)
ncp = f² · k · n (one-way ANOVA)
ncp = w² · N (chi-squared)
Noncentrality parameter (ncp). Captures how far the alternative is from the null. Larger ncp means more power. Each design has its own form of ncp.
power = 1 − P(stat < crit | ncp)
Computed via noncentral F or χ². We use Poisson-weighted sums of central distributions — the standard approach. Matches R's pf(..., ncp=) and pchisq(..., ncp=).
find n*: power(n*) ≈ target
(via bisection, tol=1e-4)
Solve-for inversion. To get sample size we bisect the power function: try n=10, 100, 1000, narrow until power(n) hits the target. Same idea for "solve for detectable effect."
verified against pwr ≥ 30 cases
Cross-checked. Output matches R's pwr package across all 6 designs and ≥ 30 input combinations.

When this is the wrong tool

If you have…
Use instead
Observed data and want "post-hoc power"
Confidence intervals — observed power adds no information beyond the p-value (Hoenig & Heisey, 2001).
Clustered or hierarchical design
Survey Design-Effect calculator (Batch 8) or simulation — standard pwr formulas overstate power when ICC > 0.
Survival / time-to-event outcome
Survival Power calculator (Batch 8) — hazard-ratio power is different from t/F.
Bayes factor or sequential design
Bayesian power tutorial — different framework.
Many simultaneous hypotheses (100+)
Multiple Testing Correction — α=0.05 alone understates the multiplicity problem.
