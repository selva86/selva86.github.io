Z-Score & Percentile Calculator

Five modes · High-precision Wichura inverse normal · Inline shaded density visual

Convert between raw scores, z-scores, and percentiles. Compute the area between two z-scores or beyond one (one- or two-tailed). Inline visual shows the standard normal density with the relevant region shaded so you can see what the number actually means.

Worked examples

How z-scores and percentiles work
What it is

A z-score tells you how many standard deviations a value is above or below the mean. A z = +1.5 means "1.5 SDs above average." A z = −2 means "2 SDs below average." It's a unit-free way to say where a single value stands in a distribution.

A percentile tells you what fraction of people are at or below a value. The 90th percentile means 90% of the population scored at or below that point.

For a normal (bell-shaped) distribution, z-scores and percentiles are different ways of saying the same thing. This tool lets you convert between raw scores, z-scores, and percentiles — and compute tail areas (one-tailed and two-tailed p-values).

How it works

Raw → z: z = (x − mean) / SD. If your test score is 75, the class mean is 70, and the SD is 10, your z-score is 0.5.

z → percentile: look up Φ(z), the cumulative normal. Some useful anchors:

• z = 0 → 50th percentile (right at the average)
• z = 1 → 84th percentile
• z = 2 → 97.5th percentile
• z = −1 → 16th percentile
• z = 1.96 → 97.5th percentile (the magic number for 95% CIs)

Percentile → z: invert Φ. The 95th percentile corresponds to z ≈ 1.645.

Tail areas (one-tailed or two-tailed p-values) are simple math on Φ:

• One-tailed (right): 1 − Φ(z)
• Two-tailed: 2 × (1 − Φ(|z|))

What you put in

One of five things, depending on the mode:

• Raw → z: a value, plus the population mean and SD
• z → percentile: a z-score
• Percentile → z: a percentile (auto-detects 0–1 vs 0–100)
• Area between: two z-scores
• Area beyond: a z-score plus a tail choice (left, right, or two-tailed)

What you get out

The translated value (z, percentile, or area), plus the related quantities for context (left tail, right tail, two-tailed), and an inline shaded plot of the normal curve showing exactly which area you computed.

How to interpret it in practice

Z-scores are directly comparable across distributions as long as both are normal. A z = 2 SAT score and a z = 2 height both put you in the top 2.5% — they mean the same thing in their own contexts.

For skewed distributions (income, wealth, time-to-event), be careful. Percentiles still describe your relative position correctly, but z-scores can mislead. A z = 1 in a heavily right-skewed income distribution puts you much further above average than a z = 1 in heights.

For hypothesis testing:

• |z| > 1.96 ↔ two-tailed p < 0.05 (the standard 5% level)
• |z| > 2.58 ↔ two-tailed p < 0.01

For confidence intervals: 1.96 is the multiplier for a 95% CI under normality. 2.58 for a 99% CI.

For industry thresholds:

• z = 3 (three-sigma) → about 1 in 370
• z = 4 → about 1 in 30,000
• z = 6 (six-sigma) → about 2 in a billion

When to use this

• Standardizing scores so you can compare across different scales
• Finding critical values for confidence intervals
• Computing one-tailed or two-tailed p-values from a z-statistic
• Sanity-checking whether a value is unusually large
• Reporting "this is in the 92nd percentile"

Common pitfalls

Assuming the distribution is normal when it isn't. A percentile derived from a z-score on skewed data can be wildly wrong. Use empirical percentiles for skewed data.

Confusing one-tailed and two-tailed. A two-tailed p is twice the smaller one-tailed p. Mixing them up halves or doubles your reported result.

Reporting p-values smaller than ~10⁻¹⁵ as if they were precise. Numerical noise dominates beyond that. Just say "p < 10⁻¹⁵."

Anatomy of the standard normal

z = (x − μ) / σ
percentile = 100 · Φ(z)

    
Z-score from a raw value. How many standard deviations above (or below) the mean a raw score sits. Comparable across distributions with different units.
INx = your Raw score x, μ = your Mean μ, σ = your Standard deviation σ.OUTz-SCORE headline + Percentile card in the result grid.

  

  

    
P(Z ≤ z) = Φ(z)
percentile = 100 · Φ(z)
Right tail = 1 − Φ(z)
Two-tailed = 2 · (1 − Φ(|z|))

    
From z to percentile / tail area. The standard normal cdf Φ. We use Abramowitz–Stegun 26.2.17 for the cdf (max abs error 7.5e-8) and Wichura's AS 241 for the inverse.
INz = your z-score.OUTHeadline Percentile; left/right/two-tailed in the result cards.

  

  

    
z = Φ⁻¹(p)
For α-level two-sided crit:
z_crit = Φ⁻¹(1 − α/2)

    
Inverse: percentile to z. Used for confidence-interval critical values: 95% CI uses Φ⁻¹(0.975) ≈ 1.96; 99% uses Φ⁻¹(0.995) ≈ 2.576.
INp = your Percentile (auto-detects 0–1 fraction or 0–100%).OUTz-SCORE headline; if p > 0.5, also a two-tailed critical at α = 1 − p.

  

  

    
P(z₁ < Z < z₂) = Φ(z₂) − Φ(z₁)
P(outside) = 1 − P(z₁ < Z < z₂)

    
Area between two z's. Useful for "what fraction of the population is between μ ± k SDs" — the 68–95–99.7 rule comes from this.
INz₁ = your Lower z₁, z₂ = your Upper z₂ (auto-swapped if reversed).OUTHeadline area = Φ(z₂) − Φ(z₁); also the outside-interval card.

  

  

    
Left tail:   P(Z ≤ z)  = Φ(z)
Right tail:  P(Z ≥ z)  = 1 − Φ(z)
Two-tailed:  P(|Z| ≥ |z|) = 2(1 − Φ(|z|))

    
Tail probabilities. Two-tailed p-values, "fraction of population beyond k SDs", and the long-tail risk all use these formulas.
INz = your z-score, direction = your Tail selector.OUTHeadline tail probability; the three cards always show all three for comparison.
