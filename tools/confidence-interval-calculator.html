Confidence Interval Calculator

8 flavors · one tool · Wilson · Clopper-Pearson · Newcombe · Welch · exact Poisson · Runs in your browser

Pick the parameter you want a CI for — mean, proportion, difference, regression coefficient, Poisson rate, variance, correlation. We compute the right kind of interval for it (with the proper exact methods where they matter), explain what it does and doesn't say, and emit reproducible R code.

Worked examples
📐 Mean test score

A class of 30 students took a test; mean = 72, SD = 11. We want a 95% CI for the true class-level mean.

How confidence intervals work
What it is

You measure something on a small group (say, the average satisfaction score for 50 customers = 7.2 out of 10) and you really want to know the answer for everyone — the whole population — but you can't measure them all. A confidence interval (CI) is a range that's likely to contain the true answer.

If your tool says "95% CI: [6.8, 7.6]", read it as: "the true average is plausibly somewhere between 6.8 and 7.6." A wider range means more uncertainty; a narrower range means more precision.

Quick technical note: "95%" is a statement about the method. If you repeated your experiment many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the true value. For your specific interval, the truth is either inside or outside — you can't say "there's a 95% chance" for that one interval. (That's the Bayesian reading, which is a different framework.)

How it works

Most confidence intervals follow the same recipe:

estimate ± multiplier × standard error

• Estimate = the number from your sample (e.g., your sample average, 7.2)
• Standard error = how much that number would change if you collected new data. Smaller sample → larger standard error → wider CI.
• Multiplier = comes from a probability table. For 95% confidence under a normal distribution, it's about 1.96. For 99%, about 2.58.

So a 95% CI for a mean is roughly: sample_mean ± 1.96 × (sample_SD / √n). As your sample size n grows, the standard error shrinks — and so does the CI.

For some cases — proportions near 0% or 100%, Poisson rates with small counts — the simple ± formula misbehaves. We swap in better formulas (Wilson, Clopper-Pearson) that always stay between 0 and 1.

What you put in

Pick what you want a CI for from the list (mean, proportion, difference of means, etc.) and enter the relevant summary stats:

• For a mean: sample size n, sample mean, sample SD
• For a proportion: successes (x) and total trials (n)
• For a regression coefficient: the estimate, its standard error, and residual df
• Plus the confidence level — almost always 95%

What you get out

The lower and upper bound, the half-width (also called "margin of error"), and a plain-English statement.

For proportions, you also get a side-by-side comparison of five different methods (Wilson, Clopper-Pearson, Agresti-Coull, Jeffreys, Wald). They mostly agree, but disagree near 0% or 100% — and the wrong method can give nonsense like a CI of [-0.05, 0.10].

How to interpret it in practice

Read the interval as "the truth is plausibly in this range." Width matters as much as the bounds:

• Tight CI like [0.40, 0.45] → confident estimate
• Wide CI like [0.20, 0.70] → you don't really know yet — collect more data

If a "boring" value (often 0 for a difference, 1 for an odds ratio) is outside the 95% CI, you'd call the result "significant at the 5% level" — there's evidence the truth is different from boring. If the boring value is inside the interval, you can't conclude "no effect"; only "you don't have enough data to rule out no effect."

Two studies can both report "p < 0.05" yet be very different:
• CI = [0.40, 0.45] → you're nailing the answer
• CI = [0.001, 0.50] → barely better than noise

Want a tighter interval? You need 4× the sample size to halve the width. That's the practical lever — the cost of precision.

When to use this

Anytime you'd report a single number to summarize data, also report its CI. "The conversion rate is 28%" is incomplete; "28% with 95% CI [25%, 31%]" tells the audience how much to trust the number. Especially important in A/B test reports, surveys, regression coefficients, scientific papers — anywhere the audience asks "but how sure are you?"

Common pitfalls

Reading "95% CI" as "95% probability the truth is in here." Technically wrong (that's a Bayesian credible interval). The correct reading is about the method, not this specific interval.

Using the simple Wald formula for proportions near 0 or 100%. It can give intervals like [-0.02, 0.05] (impossible — proportions can't be negative). Wilson and Clopper-Pearson are the right defaults.

Comparing two separate CIs and concluding "they don't overlap so they're different." Not the right test. To compare two groups, compute a CI for the difference and check whether 0 is inside.

Reporting CIs from a model with broken assumptions. A CI for a variance assuming normal data is fragile if your data is heavily skewed.

Anatomy of a confidence interval

  
Live recap — your inputs plugged in
Enter inputs above to see the derivation chain.

  

    
CI = estimate ± critical · SE

    
The general form. Most CIs decompose into a point estimate, a critical value from a sampling distribution (z, t, χ², F), and a standard error. The critical value depends on confidence level and (for some flavors) df; the SE depends on the data.
INα from your Confidence level (α = 1 − conf), critical from the appropriate distribution, SE from the formula below.OUTLower/upper bounds shown in the big CI display; half-width shown as the "± margin of error" in the result panel.

  

  

    
SE_mean = s / √n
df = n − 1
critical = t_{1−α/2, df}
CI = x̄ ± critical · SE

    
Mean (one sample). If you don't know σ — and you almost never do — use the t-distribution. For n > 30 the t and z critical values are essentially identical.
INx̄ = your Sample mean x̄, s = your Sample SD s, n = your Sample size n.OUTSE and t-critical printed under "point = … · half-width = …"; CI bounds in the headline.

  

  

    
Welch:  SE = √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂)
Pooled: sp² = ((n₁−1)s₁² + (n₂−1)s₂²)/(n₁+n₂−2)
        SE  = sp · √(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)
df_Welch ≈ Satterthwaite
CI = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) ± t_{1−α/2, df} · SE
Paired: d̄ ± t · sd/√n,  df = n−1

    
Difference of means. Welch (default) doesn't assume equal variances and adjusts df. For paired data (same subject measured twice), it reduces to a one-sample t-CI on the within-pair differences.
INIndependent: Group 1 + Group 2 with the Use Welch toggle. Paired: Number of pairs n, Mean difference, SD of difference.OUTMean diff is the point shown; SE and df in the aux line below the bounds.

  

  

    
p̂ = x / n
Wilson score CI:
center = (p̂ + z²/(2n)) / (1 + z²/n)
half   = z · √(p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/(4n²)) / (1 + z²/n)
CI = center ± half

    
Wilson is the right default for proportions. Stays inside [0, 1], performs well when p̂ is near 0 or 1, and matches what prop.test(..., correct = FALSE) reports. Wald is the textbook formula and is misleading at extremes — we still show it, but boxed for comparison.
INx = Successes x, n = Trials n, z from your Confidence level.OUTWilson row in the "All methods compared" table is the highlighted recommended one.

  

  

    
Clopper-Pearson exact:
lower = qbeta(α/2,    x,   n−x+1)
upper = qbeta(1−α/2,  x+1, n−x)

    
Clopper-Pearson is the exact CI for a proportion via the binomial distribution. Always covers at least the nominal level (sometimes more) — conservative but never anti-conservative. Use when guaranteed coverage matters (regulatory, medical).
INSame Successes x and Trials n; α = 1 − conf level.OUTSecond row of the methods table.

  

  

    
Newcombe (independent):
w₁ = Wilson(x₁, n₁);  w₂ = Wilson(x₂, n₂)
lo = (p̂₁ − p̂₂) − √((p̂₁−w₁_lo)² + (w₂_hi−p̂₂)²)
hi = (p̂₁ − p̂₂) + √((w₁_hi−p̂₁)² + (p̂₂−w₂_lo)²)
McNemar (paired):
p̂_diff = (b − c) / n
SE = √((b+c) − (b−c)²/n) / n
CI = p̂_diff ± z · SE

    
Difference of proportions. For independent groups, Newcombe's score CI stays in [-1, 1]. For paired (same subjects under two conditions), use the McNemar-style discordant-pair formula.
INIndependent: Group 1 x₁, n₁ + Group 2 x₂, n₂. Paired: b (yes-no) + c (no-yes).OUTRecommended row of the methods table; for paired, also the McNemar χ² and p-value in the summary.

  

  

    
CI = β̂ ± t_{1−α/2, df} · SE(β̂)

    
Regression coefficient. Same skeleton, but the SE comes from the model's variance-covariance matrix and df is the residual df. confint() in R does exactly this for an lm fit.
INβ̂ = Estimate β̂, SE = Standard error SE(β̂), df = Residual df.OUTIf 0 lies outside [lo, hi], the coefficient is "significant" at α; the summary states this in plain English.

  

  

    
Exact (Garwood / χ² inversion):
λ̂ = x / T
lower = qchisq(α/2,   2x)     / (2T)
upper = qchisq(1−α/2, 2(x+1)) / (2T)

    
Poisson rate. The exact CI inverts the χ² distribution and works at any count, including x = 0. Wald (= λ̂ ± z·√x/T) is fine only for large x.
INx = Event count x, T = Exposure (person-time) T.OUTExact row (highlighted) in the methods table; λ̂ is the point estimate displayed.

  

  

    
CI for σ²:
[(n−1)s² / χ²_{1−α/2, n−1},
 (n−1)s² / χ²_{α/2,   n−1}]
CI for σ = √CI for σ²

    
Variance / SD. Built on the χ² distribution; assumes underlying normality. Highly sensitive to that assumption — a single outlier can dominate s². Don't trust this CI if a Q-Q plot looks bad.
INn = Sample size n, s = Sample SD s.OUTTwo rows: σ² and σ in the methods table; the SD row is highlighted as the more interpretable one.

  

  

    
Fisher z-transform:
z_r = atanh(r),  SE_z = 1/√(n−3)
CI_z = z_r ± z_{1−α/2} · SE_z
CI_ρ = tanh(CI_z)

    
Correlation. r's sampling distribution is skewed near ±1, so we transform to Fisher's z (where it's roughly normal), build the CI there, then back-transform.
INr = Sample correlation r, n = Sample size n.OUTBounds (asymmetric on the r-scale) in the headline; Fisher z and SE_z appear in the aux line.
