Normality Test Picker

Recommends a test from sample size + use case · Runs Shapiro-Wilk, Anderson-Darling, Jarque-Bera, Lilliefors · Q-Q plot included

Pick the right normality test for your data — we recommend one based on sample size and what you're checking, then run it. The Q-Q plot is the centerpiece: for n > ~5000, every formal test will reject normality, and the plot is the only honest answer.

Worked examples
📊 Normal sample

A standard normal sample (n=50). Tests should fail to reject; Q-Q plot should look like a clean diagonal.

How normality tests work
What it is

A normal distribution is the classic bell-shaped curve. Many statistical tools — t-tests, ANOVA, regression CIs — work best when your data (or more often, the residuals from a fitted model) follow this shape.

A normality test is a formal check: it gives you a p-value where small means "this doesn't look normal" and large means "we don't have strong evidence against normality." Several different tests exist; each is sensitive to different ways data can depart from normal.

The Q-Q plot (quantile-quantile plot) is the visual version. It shows your sorted data against what it would be if your data were perfectly normal. A clean diagonal line = normal. Curves and S-shapes mean trouble.

For most real decisions, the Q-Q plot is more honest than any p-value. This tool helps you pick the right test for your situation, runs it, draws the Q-Q plot, and tells you what to do next.

How it works

Different tests measure different ways of "not being normal":

• Shapiro-Wilk: compares your sorted data to expected normal positions. Best small-sample power. The default for n ≤ 5000.
• Anderson-Darling: a version that puts extra weight on the tails. Most sensitive to outliers and tail behavior.
• Jarque-Bera: combines two simpler measures — skewness (lopsidedness) and kurtosis (tailedness). Cheap and intuitive but needs n ≥ 30 to be reliable.
• Lilliefors: a tweaked version of the classic Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, adapted for the case where you estimated the mean and SD from the same data.

What you put in

Any of these:

• A list of numeric values, pasted comma- or space-separated
• Just the sample size n, if you only want a recommendation without running anything
• Optionally, the use case: confirmatory (assumption check before another test), exploratory (does my data look normal?), or tail-sensitive (worried about outliers)

What you get out

• A recommended test based on your n and use case, with the reason
• If you pasted data: the test runs — statistic, p-value, plain-English verdict
• A Q-Q plot to inspect the shape
• A comparison table of the other tests, so you can see whether they agree

How to interpret it in practice

Small samples (n < 30): tests have low power. Failing to reject doesn't mean your data are normal — you just don't have enough data to tell. Trust the Q-Q plot more than the p-value.

Large samples (n > 5000): tests reject almost everything. Even data that's normal "for all practical purposes" will flag as non-normal, because the test detects tiny irrelevant deviations. Again — trust the Q-Q plot.

For most actual uses — a t-test, ANOVA, or regression CI — residual normality matters less than people think. These methods are robust to mild deviations once n > 30 (thanks to the Central Limit Theorem). Skew and outliers tend to matter more than slight non-normality. If you're computing something that depends directly on the normal distribution (like a CI for variance, or a precise tail probability), normality matters more.

Read the Q-Q plot for shape:

• Points on a straight diagonal → normal ✓
• Curves up at the right → right-skewed (long tail to the right)
• Curves down at the left → left-skewed
• Both ends curving away from the line → heavy tails (possible outliers)
• S-shape → bimodal or strongly skewed

When to use this

• Before a t-test, ANOVA, or regression CI, to check the normality assumption
• After fitting a model, to inspect the residuals
• Sanity-checking data during cleanup
• Documenting an assumption check in a paper or methods section
• Deciding between a parametric test (assumes normality) and a non-parametric alternative (Wilcoxon, Mann-Whitney)

Common pitfalls

Applying these tests to discrete or count data. They assume continuous data; use a χ² goodness-of-fit test instead.

Forgetting to look at the Q-Q plot. At small n the test misses real deviations; at large n it flags noise. The plot is the visual sanity check at both extremes.

Treating "non-rejection" as proof of normality. It's just absence of evidence — different from evidence of absence.

Testing the raw outcome when what matters is the residuals. For regression, normality of residuals is the assumption — run shapiro.test(residuals(fit)), not shapiro.test(y).

How the tests work

  

    
W = (Σ aᵢ x₍ᵢ₎)² / Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²

    
Shapiro-Wilk. Compares the order-statistics-weighted sum to the sample variance. The aᵢ weights are the expected normal order statistics. W = 1 means perfect normality; W < 1 means deviation. Best small-sample power (Royston AS R94, n ≤ 5000).

  

  

    
A² = −n − (1/n) Σ (2i−1)·
       [ln Φ(zᵢ) + ln(1 − Φ(z₍ₙ₊₁₋ᵢ₎))]

    
Anderson-Darling. A weighted KS-style distance, weighted to be more sensitive in the tails. The modified A* (Stephens 1986) corrects for finite sample size. Use when you specifically care about tail behaviour.

  

  

    
JB = (n/6)·(S² + (K−3)²/4)
JB ~ χ²(2) under H₀

    
Jarque-Bera. Combines skewness S and excess kurtosis K−3. Asymptotic — needs n ≥ 30 to be reasonable. Cheap and intuitive, but lower power than SW at small n.

  

  

    
Q-Q plot:
sample order stats vs. Φ⁻¹((i − 0.5)/n)

    
The Q-Q plot is the test you should always look at. Points on a straight line ⇒ normal. Curves at the ends ⇒ heavy / light tails. S-shape ⇒ bimodal or skewed. For very large n, formal tests will reject everything; the plot is the only honest verdict.
