lm() Output Interpreter

Paste · parse · interpret · Coefficient by coefficient, in plain English · Runs in your browser

Paste the output of summary(lm(...)) from R; we'll parse every coefficient, R², F-statistic and residual line, then write a plain-English read of each one. The tool tells you what's significant, how big the effect actually is, and what diagnostics to chase next.

Worked examples
🚗 mtcars

A classic teaching example from R's built-in mtcars dataset. Predicting fuel economy (mpg) from car weight (wt) and horsepower (hp) — both expected to reduce mpg.

📐 What you'll see: Both wt and hp have negative coefficients with strong p-values; R² ≈ 83%.
How linear regression output works
What it is

A linear regression predicts a number (like price, weight, or test score) from one or more inputs (like square footage, age, or hours studied). When you fit one in R with lm(...), then call summary(), you get a printout that tells you everything the model learned. This tool reads that printout for you and explains it in plain English.

If you're new to it, the output looks intimidating — three different tables and lots of numbers. It's actually just answering three questions:

1. How much does each input matter? (the coefficients table)
2. How well does the whole model fit? (R²)
3. Does the model do anything at all? (the F-test)

How it works

The model finds the "best" weights to put on each input by minimizing the squared distance between its predictions and the actual values. (This is called ordinary least squares.) Then it reports:

• Coefficients: the weight on each input. A coefficient of 3 on "horsepower" means "for every 1-unit increase in horsepower, the predicted output goes up by 3" (holding other inputs fixed).
• Standard error (SE): how uncertain that coefficient is. Smaller SE = more precise.
• t-statistic and p-value: is the coefficient meaningfully different from zero? p < 0.05 traditionally means "yes."
• R²: the fraction of variation in the outcome that the model explains. R² = 0.6 means the model captures 60% of the variation; the other 40% is noise.
• F-statistic: a single test of "do any of these inputs matter?" Useful as a global sanity check.

What you put in

Paste the verbatim output from summary(lm(formula, data=...)) directly from your R console. The Call line, residuals, coefficients table, R-squared, and F-statistic. Stray prompts (>) and extra whitespace are fine — we strip them.

What you get out

For each coefficient, a plain-English sentence:

"Holding other predictors fixed, a 1-unit increase in horsepower is associated with a 0.03 mpg decrease in fuel efficiency (p = 0.001)."

Plus a model-level verdict — solid / weak / broken — based on R², the F-test, degrees of freedom, and red flags like extreme t-values or perfect fits (which usually mean a bug, not a great model). Plus reproducible R code for the natural follow-ups (confint(), plot(), etc.).

How to interpret it in practice

Read in this order:

1. F-statistic first. Does the model do anything overall? If the F-test's p-value is large (> 0.10), nothing about individual coefficients is reliable.

2. R² next. How much variation does the model explain? Low R² doesn't mean the model is broken; it means the outcome is noisy. R² = 0.10 is totally normal in human-behavior data. R² = 0.99 is suspicious — usually a data leakage bug, not a great model.

3. Each coefficient in plain language. Translate the units back to natural scale. "wt has coefficient −3.88" means nothing to a non-stats audience. "Every extra ton of car weight reduces predicted fuel economy by ~3.9 mpg" is the version they can act on.

Watch for these traps:

• High R² but no single coefficient is significant → your inputs are too correlated to tell apart (multicollinearity). Run vif().
• Significant ≠ important. With huge samples, tiny effects become "significant" but may not matter in practice. Look at the size of the coefficient, not just the p-value.
• Coefficient ≠ cause. "Holding everything else constant" doesn't undo confounding. If you didn't randomize, you're describing associations, not causes.

When to use this

• Any time you fit a linear regression in R and want a beginner-friendly read of the output
• Teaching regression — paste textbook examples and walk through the interpretation
• Communicating regression results to a non-stats audience
• Sanity-checking your own analysis before reporting

Common pitfalls

Treating coefficients as causal. They're conditional associations. "Holding all else constant" doesn't fix confounding.

Reading R² in isolation. A "good" R² depends entirely on the field. R² = 0.05 is normal for individual-level outcomes; R² = 0.95 is normal for engineering data.

Skipping diagnostic plots. summary() doesn't tell you if the model has wrong shape, outliers, or unequal variance. Always run plot(model) too.

Comparing coefficient sizes across predictors. Only valid if predictors are on the same scale. Otherwise, standardize first or use β-weights.

What we look for in summary(lm)

Call: lm(formula = y ~ x1 + x2, data = …)
The Call. Confirms which formula and dataset produced this output. We extract the outcome name and use it in the plain-English read.
Min Q1 Median Q3 Max
Residuals quantiles. A quick check on whether residuals are roughly symmetric and centered at zero. Min and Max far from |3·SE| flag outliers.
Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|)
Coefficients table. We translate each row into "for every 1-unit increase in X, Y changes by …" plus a p-value verdict: p > 0.10 no evidence · 0.05–0.10 weak · 0.01–0.05 evidence · < 0.01 strong.
R² = 1 − SS_res / SS_tot
R² and Adjusted R². Fraction of variance explained. We flag suspiciously high (> 0.99 → check for leakage) and effectively zero (< 0.02 with high F-test p → no signal).
F = (SS_reg / df_reg) / (SS_res / df_res)
F-statistic. Overall test of "any predictor matters." A non-significant F-test with a significant individual coefficient is suspicious — usually means underpowered or near-collinear.
