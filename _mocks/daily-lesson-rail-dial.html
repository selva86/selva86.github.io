The dial, rebuilt

Same idea, a third of the geometry. The ring now appears only on a lesson you can still open, which is the only row where a countdown means anything, and everything else in the rail went quiet.

What changed and why each change follows from the last

The ring was doing two jobs. On an open row it meant time left. On a closed row it meant finished or not finished. Same shape, two meanings, seventeen of them down one column. That is what made it loud, and the off-centre tick was a symptom: a check mark nudged into place with hand-picked pixels, inside a ring that should not have been there.

So the ring is now reserved for the one thing it is good at. Open lessons only, at most three, sorted soonest first, so the first ring in the list is always the nearest deadline. It is smaller as well: 18px with a 1.75px stroke, against 22px and 2px.

The header ring went entirely. It was a second copy of the first row's ring, sitting two centimetres above it. Removing it took out the single largest element in the rail and lost nothing.

Closed lessons carry no geometry at all. One line, muted ink, and one word on the right: finished, or not opened. Open rows lost their course-and-part subtitle for the same reason: it cost a line each to say something the title mostly implies, and there are only ever three of them to choose between. The course is still there on hover. The way back in appears in the bottom right of the row on hover, reading "Reopen with Pro", so the lock is offered rather than worn on every row. The record stays where it is while that happens, and the strip is reserved on every closed row, so nothing under the pointer moves. Anyone who never hovers still gets the same offer at the foot of the rail. A reader on day 60 sees three rings and a long quiet list, not forty spent circles.

The tick is now flex-centred around an inline SVG instead of a pseudo-element positioned by hand, so there is nothing left to nudge. It appears on one row only: a lesson you finished whose window is still open, where a countdown is no longer the point.

Why they are not listed.

A list of things you cannot open is the only part of the rail with no action attached to it, and it is the longest part. It pushes the closed group, which is the whole commercial argument, off the first screen.

Naming tomorrow's lesson lets a reader decide in advance that they are not interested. Today they open the email because they do not know what is in it; that is the mechanism working.

Thirty of the eighty-seven calendar entries have no lesson behind them yet. A count of what is built is a fact you control; a list of titles is a promise that ages badly if you reorder, insert or cut.

A middle option existed and has also gone. One line at the foot reading "Tomorrow morning brings the next one, 36 more after that" would have kept the retention fact without promising any particular lesson. The owner cut it, and the rail is quieter for it: every line in the list is now about something that has already happened to this reader.

If that retention fact is ever wanted back, the place for it is the header, under "Your daily lessons", where "Day 13 of the series" already sits. It would cost no rows and add no scroll.

Dates beside the lessons only where one is still sharp

Not on the open rows. They already carry a time, and it is the one that matters. A second date on the same line competes with the only number in the rail a reader can act on.

Not on most of the closed ones either. Every lesson in that group is equally shut. "21 September" changes nothing a reader can do with it, and nine dates down a column is nine numbers sitting beside prose, which is exactly what came out of the open rows.

But one of them is the sharpest fact in the rail, and it is currently invisible. ACF and PACF closed at six o'clock this morning, four hours before this reader sat down. In the list it looks identical to one that closed on the nineteenth. A near miss is the most honest argument the group contains, and flattening it into the same grey row as three-week-old history throws it away.

So recency appears where it is sharp and nowhere else: anything that closed today or yesterday says so, everything older says nothing. It sits in the strip at the foot of the row that is already reserved for the hover link, so it costs no height, and the link takes its place when the pointer arrives.

If you ever want the whole span, the place for it is the group header, beside the count, not on nine rows: "Earlier, 9 since 16 September" costs one line instead of nine.

Collapsed open by default, one click away from gone

The control sits at the top right of the rail, the only button in it, so there is nothing to confuse it with. It is the panel glyph rather than a chevron: a chevron on its own reads as "go back" at least as readily as "collapse", while a rectangle with one column divided off is what VS Code, Linear, Notion and Vercel all settled on. The filled column is the rail, so when the rail goes the column empties and the icon carries the state without a label. Collapsed, the rail is a 52px strip carrying one thing: a ring for each lesson still open, soonest first, with the title and the time left on hover. The deadline stays glanceable while the lesson gets another 240px of width.

The research band for a collapsed sidebar is 56 to 72px, which assumes icons and labels. This one holds three rings and nothing else, so it can be tighter. In the real build the state would live in localStorage, so a reader who collapses it once does not have to do it on every lesson.

The rings are grey here, and that is the rule rather than a preference. Colour is for exceptions, not for states: a surface that is permanently coloured trains the reader to stop seeing it, which costs you the one moment the colour was for. Collapsing the rail is also an explicit request to be left alone. So the rings desaturate to slate, the strip sits back at 70 percent and comes forward on hover, and the arc stays, so how much of each window is left is still there to read.

One exception survives. A window inside its last hour keeps a muted coral. Grey for everything would mean watching a lesson lapse in silence, and saying when one closes is the whole promise of the thing. The threshold is deliberately strict: at twenty hours the reader does not need interrupting, and you can see that by switching the control above to 35 minutes.

My honest read on option four

Rebuilt like this, it is the right pick, and the fix came out of your own objection rather than around it. A rail that sits beside prose should have one loud element used where the action is, and the dial now does exactly that: the first ring you see is the nearest deadline, and it is the only thing in the rail asking for attention.

It also survives scale better than the version you saw. On day 60 the closed group is forty quiet lines and there are still only three rings. The original would have had forty-eight.

Where I would still push back, honestly. The dial says nothing about the shape of the series. The timeline did: a spine with a node per morning told the reader, without a word, that this is a daily thing that has been running a fortnight and carries on. The dial has to say that in prose at the foot of the list, which is weaker.

If retention worries you more than conversion, the timeline is still the better instrument. If conversion is the job, which is how you framed it, the dial rebuilt is better: it puts the clock exactly where the decision is and keeps everything else out of the way.

The one thing I would not do is split the difference. Rings on open rows and a spine down the side is two ideas competing for the same 260 pixels, and it would read as busier than either.
