Courses dropdown — four directions

Each option is a full replacement for the current three-column panel. All use Inter Tight, the per-track colors, and the real seven tracks. The failures they fix: ragged whitespace, four tracks demoted to gray "tutorial curriculum," no credential visualization, no guidance for the undecided.

A · The Ladder

One vertical path, seven rungs. Tracks in progression order on a connected rail, so the menu answers the visitor's actual question: "where do I stand, and what comes next?" Start-free appears on hover per rung. No columns, no raggedness, every track equal weight.

Trade-off: taller panel; slightly slower for returning users who already know their track.

B · Preview pane

Calm list left, living detail right. Hovering a track fills the pane: description, three real stats, the first sections as a mini-path, and both CTAs. Information appears on demand instead of seven cards shouting at once. The pattern Stripe and Linear use for exactly this problem.

Trade-off: needs a default selection and a touch fallback (tap once to preview, tap again to go); the most JS of the four.

C · Certificate-forward cards

The credential leads. Symmetric 4+3 grid, each card opened by a color-coded badge mark and closed by its certificate line with a check. The header sells the outcome, and the dashed footer row catches the undecided with a placement quiz. Strongest conversion posture of the four.

Trade-off: visually busiest; the placement quiz is a promise you'd need to build (or link the compare page until it exists).

D · Editorial minimal

Pure typography, zero chrome. Two quiet columns, small-caps group labels, name + one-liner + count per row, and exactly one accent: the recommended entry point. The Vercel school of menus — sophistication through restraint. Loads nothing, breaks nowhere.

Trade-off: least persuasive per row (no certificate line, no per-row CTA); everything rides on the typography being flawless.

My recommendation

B as the destination, C's certificate line grafted in. The preview pane carries the most decision-support per pixel and feels the most like a mature product; adding C's "Certificate: X" line and check into the preview stats keeps the credential visible where B is weakest. A is the strong second if you prefer zero-JS robustness with the same grouped ordering. D fits best if the whole site later moves toward the quieter aesthetic.
