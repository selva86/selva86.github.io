Lesson 1

Meet the decision tree

A random forest is just many of one simple thing: a decision tree. So before we can build the forest, you need to truly understand the tree, including why a single one is a bad idea on its own.

Read a tree as a chain of yes/no questions
See how a tree chooses each split (it minimizes impurity)
Watch a single tree overfit, live, and feel why we need a forest
Concept

A tree is a flowchart of questions

To predict whether a customer churns, a tree asks a sequence of yes/no questions about the data, narrowing down until it reaches a confident answer at a leaf.

Each internal node is a question; each leaf is a prediction. To classify a row, you walk from the top to a leaf.

Simple to read, simple to follow. The only real question is: how does the tree decide which question to ask, and where to split?

How a tree chooses a split

A tree tries to make each side of a split as pure as possible, mostly one class. It measures messiness with Gini impurity (0 = perfectly pure). Drag the split and find the cut that makes both sides cleanest.

The flaw

One tree is jumpy

You just saw it: push the depth up and train accuracy marches to 100% while test accuracy peaks and then falls. The tree starts carving tiny regions around individual noisy points, memorizing the training set instead of learning the pattern.

Worse, a single tree is unstable: change a handful of training rows and you can get a completely different tree. In machine-learning terms, a deep tree is low bias but high variance, accurate on average, but wildly inconsistent.

The problem in one line

A single decision tree is powerful enough to fit anything, which is exactly why it overfits and can't be trusted on new data.

The big idea ahead

So what do we do with a jumpy expert?

Here is the move that turns this weakness into a strength. If one tree is noisy and inconsistent, don't trust one, grow hundreds, each a little different, and let them vote.

The errors of individual trees tend to cancel out, while their shared signal reinforces. The average of many high-variance trees can be remarkably stable and accurate. That ensemble is the random forest, and building it properly is Lesson 2.

Aha

A forest doesn't need good trees. It needs many different trees whose mistakes are uncorrelated. Lesson 2 is all about how to make trees differ on purpose.
