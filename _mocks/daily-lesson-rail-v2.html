Five ways to build the daily lesson rail

One reader, one morning, five designs. The controls above are live: switch to a Pro member and every rail drops its locks and its clocks, switch the nearest window and every countdown moves together.

What this actually is

The instinct is to copy a course sidebar. That is the wrong reference. A course sidebar lists a fixed syllabus you own forever, and its whole job is progress. Here the reader owns nothing permanently: each lesson is lent to them for three days by the email that carries it, and then it goes back on the shelf.

That is a lending library, and two products have already solved it properly. Libby organises a borrower's world as loans, holds and a timeline, sorts by due date, and returns everything automatically so there is never a penalty to dread. MUBI gives each film a fixed run and makes the schedule itself the product. Both treat the deadline as a plain fact of the service rather than a threat, which is exactly the register this needs.

It matters commercially too. The research on urgency is blunt: a countdown earns its lift only when the deadline is real and the offer genuinely changes at zero. This one is real, it is set by an email that was actually sent, and it never resets. So it can be stated plainly and put on the row it belongs to, which is also where the decision gets made. What it must never do is float, nag or reappear.

One deliberate departure. The standard advice sets group headers in 11px uppercase with 2px of letter spacing. That is on your own list of tells, so every group header here is sentence case at 12.5px. It costs a little scannability and buys a lot of credibility.

One. The loan shelf Libby, on paper

A light rail against the player's light stage, set in the same serif the lessons use, so the list reads as a shelf of borrowed things rather than a control panel. Due dates sit right-aligned in tabular figures. There is not a single icon. Hover a returned lesson and the date quietly swaps for the way back in, so the lock is offered rather than displayed.

Two. The timeline the schedule is the product

The series is a calendar, so the rail is drawn as one. A hairline spine runs down the left with a node for every morning: filled and green for today, hollow behind, dashed ahead. Each lesson hangs off the day it arrived, and an open one carries a 2px bar that empties across its three days. Nothing is sorted by state, because the reader already knows time.

Three. The reel MUBI, one thing at a time

The rail opens on a single card: whichever unfinished lesson closes soonest, with the hours left set large, the title in serif, and a real figure from that lesson drawn behind it. Everything else compresses to one line each. This is the only direction that makes a recommendation rather than a list, and the only one with an image in it.

Four. The dial time as geometry

One device, used twice. A 62px ring in the header shows the nearest window with the hours inside it; a 22px ring in place of each open row's marker shows that lesson's own. A finished lesson closes its ring and takes a check. Nothing needs reading to be understood, which is the point: the reader glances at the rail while thinking about statistics.

Five. The ledger nothing but type

No icons, no bars, no rings, no dark ground. A ruled white list where every row is one line and the only difference between a live lesson and a returned one is the weight of the ink. The right column is a single column of times and dates in tabular figures, so the whole state of the series can be read straight down it. The most restrained direction, and the hardest to get wrong.

Which one

Build the timeline, with the shelf's hover. The series is a schedule, the reader experiences it as a schedule, and the spine is the only direction that tells them the truth about it without a legend. It also degrades best: when a reader is on day 60 with 40 closed lessons, a state list becomes a wall of grey rows, and a dated list just scrolls.

Take one thing from the shelf: closed rows should offer the way back in on hover rather than wearing a padlock. A rail with nine padlocks in it is a rail that argues with the reader every time they open a lesson.

The dial is the runner-up and the most immediately delightful, but a ring per row is a lot of geometry for a surface that sits beside prose, and it has nothing to say once the lesson is closed. The reel is the strongest single screen of the five and the weakest list: it is a merchandising surface, and it would be a better dashboard module than a rail. The ledger is the safe one; if the timeline ever feels busy in build, this is where it lands.
