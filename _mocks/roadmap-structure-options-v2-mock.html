The R Roadmap · structure

Four ways to shape the journey

Each fixes the same two problems: statistics moves into the shared core where it belongs, and the path is framed around what you become, not a list of topics. Same upgraded ribbon art throughout. Pick the structure, then we refine the one you choose.

1. Core, then specialize

T-shaped · recommended

A strong shared core, including statistics, that everyone completes and earns a real credential for. Then three equal specializations branch off. Fixes the ordering bug and keeps the appeal of "go deep in what your job needs." The cleanest, most defensible structure.

one core (with stats) · then three equal specializations

The sell to a prospect

"Master the complete core, get a credential for it, then specialize in the role you want." It is honest about prerequisites, gives a win early (the Data Analyst credential), and lets people pay to go deeper in exactly one direction. The T-shaped story recruiters already understand.

Watch out

Three branches feel slightly less "epic" than four. Advanced R as a specialization is niche; it may convert worse than ML or Time Series and could be positioned as the engineering elective.

2. Role paths

become a __

Drop "topics" entirely. The product is three named destinations a prospect self-selects by goal. Each path bundles exactly the modules that role needs (statistics included where it matters), in the right order. Highest conversion clarity: people buy the job title.

pick who you want to become · each path carries its own modules

The sell to a prospect

"Which one do you want to be?" The landing page becomes three doors, each a career. A visitor instantly sees the exact path to their goal and what they will be able to call themselves at the end. This is how the strongest bootcamps and DataCamp tracks convert.

Watch out

Modules get duplicated across paths (Statistics appears in two), so the catalog must make shared modules obvious or it looks like you are paying twice. Best when each path maps to a credential people actually list on LinkedIn.

3. Mastery levels

stackable credentials

One horizontal progression in four named levels, a credential earned at each. Specialization happens inside the upper levels as optional focus. Every level is a milestone and a reason to continue. Gamified momentum without a vertical ladder.

four levels, one baseline, a credential at every gate

The sell to a prospect

"Climb from Beginner to Specialist, earning a credential at every level." Four milestones means four dopamine hits and four reasons to renew. Each credential is independently shareable, so even partial completers get value, which lowers the perceived risk of starting.

Watch out

Levels imply ranking, the thing you wanted to avoid. Kept strictly horizontal here, but the copy must say "stages of mastery," not "tiers," or beginners feel labeled. Specialization gets squeezed into the late levels.

4. Project spine

build a portfolio

Organize the whole roadmap around five real deliverables. Skills exist to serve the next project. You graduate with a portfolio a hiring manager can open, not just a certificate. The strongest differentiator against theory-heavy competitors.

five shipped projects · skills feed each one

The sell to a prospect

"You will leave with five projects you can show an employer." For career-changers, that is the single most persuasive promise. It reframes the purchase from "watching lessons" to "building proof," and the projects double as shareable marketing when learners post them.

Watch out

Most work to build and maintain (real datasets, graded rubrics, review). Specializations become "tracks of projects" rather than the spine, so the branching story is weaker. Best layered on top of structure 1 or 2, not instead of them.
