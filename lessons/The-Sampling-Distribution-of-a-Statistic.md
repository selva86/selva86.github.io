=== step === concept
## The standard error is the spread of the statistic, not of the data

Kiran's bookstore models its daily order values as exponential with a rate of 1/35. That single rate pins down two numbers at once: the population's mean order value, $35, and for this particular distribution, its standard deviation too, also $35.

Draw the spread of Kiran's 30 orders, then draw the spread of their mean once you repeat the sampling many times, and set the two side by side.

```r
# Compare the spread of one day's 30 orders with the spread of the mean's sampling distribution
set.seed(1)
orders_day <- rexp(30, rate = 1/35)
round(sd(orders_day), 2)
#> [1] 37.37

set.seed(2)
means30 <- replicate(2000, mean(rexp(30, rate = 1/35)))
round(sd(means30), 2)
#> [1] 6.42

round(35 / sqrt(30), 2)
#> [1] 6.39
```

`orders_day` holds that one day's 30 orders, and `sd()` on them comes out to 37.37, close to the population's own standard deviation of $35. That is the data's spread: how far a single order tends to sit from the average order.

`means30` is a different thing entirely. `replicate(2000, mean(rexp(30, rate = 1/35)))` draws a fresh 30 orders, averages them, and repeats that 2000 times, so it holds 2000 separate daily averages, one per simulated day. Its spread, 6.42, is nowhere near 37.37, because an average of 30 orders moves around far less than a single order does.

This spread of the mean itself, not of the raw data behind it, has a name: the **standard error**. For the mean, it follows a simple formula, the population's standard deviation over the square root of the sample size. 35 divided by the square root of 30 comes out to 6.39, matching the simulated 6.42 closely.

[KEY INSIGHT]
A statistic's standard error is the standard deviation of its OWN sampling distribution, the spread you would see if you could compute that statistic over and over on fresh samples. It is not the spread of the data that went into it. Kiran's orders spread by 37, their mean spreads by only 6.4.

The mean's sampling distribution tightens into a bell as n grows, on any population, however lopsided it starts out. === step === concept
## The same recipe works for any statistic

Nothing about `replicate()` cares what function sits inside it. It ran `mean()` over and over to build the mean's sampling distribution. It will just as happily run any other function over and over, and whatever comes out is that statistic's own sampling distribution.

Three candidates, all computed from the same kind of 30 daily orders, are worth checking against the mean's clean result:

- the share of orders over $50, a proportion
- the median order
- the variance of the orders

Each one gets the same treatment: draw 30 orders, compute the statistic, repeat 2000 times, then look at what comes out.

=== step === concept
## A proportion is a mean of 0/1 values

About 24% of Kiran's orders run past $50 (`1 - pexp(50, rate = 1/35)` gives 0.2397). Pick one order at random and mark it 1 if it is over $50 and 0 otherwise. The share of 1s in a sample of 30 orders is a sample proportion, and a sample proportion is nothing more than a mean of those 0s and 1s.

Build its sampling distribution the same way means30 was built.

```r
# Build the sampling distribution of the share of orders over $50, replicated 2000 times
set.seed(3)
props30 <- replicate(2000, mean(rexp(30, rate = 1/35) > 50))
round(mean(props30), 3)
#> [1] 0.239

round(sd(props30), 3)
#> [1] 0.079

round(sqrt(0.24 * (1 - 0.24) / 30), 3)
#> [1] 0.078
```

`rexp(30, rate = 1/35) > 50` turns the 30 orders into 30 TRUE/FALSE values, and `mean()` over TRUE/FALSE values counts the share that are TRUE, exactly like counting the share of 1s. `props30` holds 2000 of those shares, one per simulated day, averaging 0.239, close to the population's own 0.2397.

Because a proportion is a mean, it inherits the mean's own standard error formula for free, just swapping in the version built for a 0/1 population: the square root of p times 1 minus p, over n. Plugging in p = 0.24 gives 0.078, matching the simulated 0.079 closely.

See its shape for yourself: 500 shares of orders over $50, each from a fresh batch of 30 orders.

## The median's sampling distribution, and what sets its spread

The median is a completely different kind of statistic. It is not a sum of anything divided by n, it is just whichever order sits in the middle once the 30 are sorted from smallest to largest. Build its sampling distribution anyway. The replicate() recipe does not care which statistic sits inside it.

```r
# Build the sampling distribution of the median, replicated 2000 times
set.seed(4)
medians30 <- replicate(2000, median(rexp(30, rate = 1/35)))
round(mean(medians30), 2)
#> [1] 24.8

round(sd(medians30), 2)
#> [1] 6.34
```

`medians30` holds 2000 median orders, one per simulated day, averaging 24.8, close to the population's own median of $24.26 (`qexp(0.5, rate = 1/35)`). Its spread, 6.34, happens to look a lot like the mean's own 6.42 from two steps back, but that is not because the same sigma-over-square-root-of-n formula applies here. The median runs on a different formula altogether, built from how tightly the orders bunch up right around that $24.26 middle value.

The **density** of a distribution at a point says how tightly values bunch up right there, read straight off the population's own curve with `dexp()`. A population with a tall, narrow density right at its median packs its sample medians close together from one sample to the next. A population with a low, flat density right at its median lets its sample medians wander further.

The exact formula for the median's standard error is 1 divided by twice the population's density at the median, times the square root of n.

```r
# The median's standard error formula: 1 over twice the population's density at the median, times sqrt(n)
f_median <- dexp(qexp(0.5, rate = 1/35), rate = 1/35)
round(f_median, 4)
#> [1] 0.0143

round(1 / (2 * f_median * sqrt(30)), 2)
#> [1] 6.39
```

`dexp(qexp(0.5, rate = 1/35), rate = 1/35)` first finds the population's median with `qexp()`, then reads the density right at that point with `dexp()`: 0.0143. Plugging that into the formula gives a predicted standard error of 6.39, matching the simulated 6.34 closely. Not sigma over the square root of n, a completely different formula built from the population's density, that happens to land near the same number for this particular population.

=== step === concept
## The variance's sampling distribution does not look like the others

The sample variance is a third kind of statistic again, built from squared distances from the mean. Build its sampling distribution the same way.

```r
# Build the sampling distribution of the variance, replicated 2000 times
set.seed(5)
vars30 <- replicate(2000, var(rexp(30, rate = 1/35)))
round(mean(vars30), 0)
#> [1] 1245

round(sd(vars30), 0)
#> [1] 652

round(range(vars30), 0)
#> [1]  218 8372
```

`vars30` holds 2000 sample variances, one per simulated day, averaging 1245, close to the population's own true variance of 1225 (35 squared). But look at its range: from 218 all the way to 8372. Set that spread, 652, against the mean's 6.4 and the proportion's 0.08 from earlier. The variance's sampling distribution is far more spread out, and far less tidy.

Two things make it behave differently. Squaring a distance exaggerates it, so the handful of unusually large orders in any sample, like the $154.84 order from the cover chart, pull the sample variance up hard when they land in a sample. And a variance can never go below zero, so its sampling distribution cannot spread evenly on both sides of its mean the way the mean's and the median's do.

=== step === concept
## Deciding by simulation whether the normal approximation is close enough

A bell shaped normal distribution makes a specific claim about its tails: 2.3% of its area sits beyond 2 standard errors above the mean, and another 2.3% sits beyond 2 standard errors below. Check that claim against what the variance and the proportion actually did over their 2000 simulated days.

```r
# How far 2 standard errors sits above and below the mean, for the variance and the proportion
v_upper <- mean(vars30) + 2 * sd(vars30)
v_lower <- mean(vars30) - 2 * sd(vars30)
round(c(lower = v_lower, upper = v_upper), 0)
#> lower upper
#>   -60  2549

p_upper <- mean(props30) + 2 * sd(props30)
p_lower <- mean(props30) - 2 * sd(props30)
round(c(lower = p_lower, upper = p_upper), 3)
#> lower upper
#> 0.081 0.397

# The share of the 2000 simulated values landing beyond each cutoff
round(c(vars_above = mean(vars30 > v_upper), vars_below = mean(vars30 < v_lower),
        props_above = mean(props30 > p_upper), props_below = mean(props30 < p_lower)) * 100, 1)
#>  vars_above  vars_below props_above props_below
#>         4.0         0.0         4.2         1.7

# What normal theory claims for both tails beyond 2 standard errors
round((1 - pnorm(2)) * 100, 1)
#> [1] 2.3
```

The variance's lower cutoff, -60, is already impossible, because a variance can never be negative. So of course 0.0% of the 2000 simulated values land below it. Its upper cutoff, 2549, catches 4.0%, nearly double what normal theory claims for that side alone.

The proportion does better: 1.7% below its lower cutoff and 4.2% above its upper one, both reasonably close to the 2.3% normal theory predicts on each side, nowhere near as far off as the variance's one sided failure.

This comparison, a statistic's own simulated tail against the normal distribution's claimed 2.3%, is the general check. Run it on any statistic you build. If the simulated shares land close to 2.3% on both sides, the normal approximation is safe to lean on. If one side comes back badly wrong, the way the variance's did, it is not.

=== step === concept
## What makes a statistic's sampling distribution go normal, and what does not

[KEY INSIGHT]
A statistic built as a plain average, like the mean or a proportion, inherits the central limit theorem automatically. Its sampling distribution goes normal as n grows, no separate check required. A statistic built any other way, like a median or a variance, does not get that guarantee for free. The median still has its own formula, built from the population's density, and still turned out close to normal here. The variance has no such formula handed to it at n = 30, and its simulated tails showed exactly how far it was from normal: one side at 4.0% against a claimed 2.3%, the other side flatly impossible for a normal distribution to produce at all.

The rule to carry forward: before leaning on a normal approximation for any statistic, ask whether it is built as a plain average. If it is, the guarantee is automatic. If it is not, simulate it and compare its tails to what normal theory claims, the way the variance and the proportion just were.

## What today's simulations showed

Four statistics, all built from the same 30 daily orders, behaved in three different ways. To sum it up:

- The mean and the proportion are both plain averages, a proportion is a mean of 0s and 1s, so both inherited the central limit theorem automatically. Their standard errors matched simple formulas, 35 over the square root of 30 for the mean, the square root of p times 1 minus p over 30 for the proportion, and both already looked close to a bell at n = 30.
- The median is not an average, but it has its own formula, built from the population's density at the median rather than from sigma. That formula predicted 6.39, matching the simulated 6.34 closely, and its shape looked close to a bell too.
- The variance is not an average either, has no such formula handed to it here, and its simulated tails proved it: 4.0% beyond 2 standard errors on one side, a flat impossible 0.0% on the other, against a normal distribution's claimed 2.3% on each side.
- The minimum never went bell shaped at all, at any sample size, because it stays exactly exponential by construction, just squeezed into an ever narrower range as n grows.

So before trusting a normal approximation for any statistic, ask whether it is built as a plain average. If it is, lean on the shortcut. If it is not, simulate its sampling distribution and compare its tails to what normal theory claims, the way today's lesson just did for the variance.
