## Regression Assumptions and Residuals

In Lesson 1, Priya's iced-coffee cart got its line: each warmer degree buys about two more cups, and temperature explained 96% of her daily swing. A great fit. But a line that fits well can still be lying to you, because every regression quietly leans on four assumptions, and `lm()` will hand you slopes and p-values whether or not they hold.

The good news: you already met the tool that checks all four. The **residual**, the gap between what actually happened and what the line predicted, is a regression's lie detector. This lesson teaches you to read it. The plot below is where we are headed; by the end you will diagnose a model from its shape in seconds.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

- Name the four assumptions behind a linear regression and say what each one means
- Read a residuals-vs-fitted plot: tell a healthy fit from a funnel and from a curve
- Check normality with a Q-Q plot, and check independence, and know which fix each problem needs

**Prerequisites:** Lesson 1 (you can fit a line with `lm()` and you know a residual is actual minus predicted). You can run R and read its output. Every new term is defined as it appears.

## The four promises a regression makes

When you fit `lm(cups ~ temp)`, you are not just drawing a line. You are assuming the data was generated by

\[ y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i \]

Read it out loud: each day's cups \(y_i\) equals the true intercept \(\beta_0\) plus the true slope \(\beta_1\) times that day's temperature \(x_i\), plus an **error** \(\varepsilon_i\), the part no straight line could ever capture (weekend crowds, a passing tour group, rain). The slope and p-values R reports are only trustworthy if that error term behaves. It must keep four promises, easy to remember as **LINE**:

| Assumption | What it means for Priya | How you check it |
|---|---|---|
| **L**inearity | The real relationship between temp and cups is a straight line, not a curve | residuals vs fitted (look for a bend) |
| **I**ndependence | One day's miss tells you nothing about the next day's miss | residuals in time order (look for runs) |
| **N**ormality | The misses pile up in a symmetric bell around zero | a Q-Q plot of the residuals |
| **E**qual variance | The misses are the same size for cool days and hot days alike | residuals vs fitted (look for a funnel) |

Notice the right column: you check almost everything by **looking at the residuals**. So let us learn to look.

## The residual is what the line could not explain

A residual is the leftover gap for one day: the actual cups minus the cups the line predicted for that day.

\[ e_i = y_i - \hat y_i \]

Here \(y_i\) is day \(i\)'s real cups, \(\hat y_i\) (said "y-hat") is the line's prediction for that day, and \(e_i\) is the leftover. Here is the key idea the whole lesson rests on: **if the line has captured everything systematic, the residuals should be pure noise.** No pattern, no drift, no fanning out. Just a featureless cloud scattered around zero. Any pattern you can SEE in the residuals is structure the line failed to capture, a promise being broken.

So we plot them. The standard diagnostic is **residuals vs fitted**: each day's residual \(e_i\) on the vertical axis against the line's prediction \(\hat y_i\) on the horizontal axis. A healthy model gives a flat, even band hugging the zero line. Let us build Priya's residuals and look.

```r
# Priya's 12 days from Lesson 1, rebuilt (a fresh R session starts empty).
coffee <- data.frame(
  temp = c(15, 17, 18, 20, 21, 23, 24, 26, 27, 29, 30, 31),
  cups = c(30, 36, 33, 42, 40, 47, 44, 52, 55, 56, 61, 60)
)
fit <- lm(cups ~ temp, data = coffee)

e <- residuals(fit)        # the 12 leftover gaps, one per day
round(sum(e), 8)           # least squares forces them to cancel to zero
#> [1] 0
round(mean(e), 8)          # so their average miss is exactly zero too
#> [1] 0

plot(fit, which = 1)       # residuals vs fitted: we WANT a flat, even band
```

The residuals summing to zero is guaranteed by least squares, it is not the assumption. The assumption is about their *shape*. Toggle the widget above (or below): "Healthy fit" is the target, a flat random band with no trend and even spread. The next two shapes are the warnings.

## Equal variance, and the funnel that breaks it

The **E** in LINE is equal variance, the formal name is **homoscedasticity**: the spread of the errors is the same size everywhere.

\[ \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2 \quad \text{for every day } i \]

where \(\operatorname{Var}\) is the variance (the typical squared size of a miss) and \(\sigma^2\) is one constant value that does not change with temperature. Priya's misses on a cool 16-degree day should be about as big as her misses on a scorching 31-degree day.

When they are not, you get the classic **funnel**. Picture Priya's hot days: sometimes a heatwave sells her out by noon, sometimes a thunderstorm clears the platform and she sells almost nothing. Her cool days are boringly predictable. So her misses are tiny on the left of the plot and fan out wider and wider to the right, a funnel pointing right. Toggle the widget to "Funnel" and watch the band open up.

::widget residual-plot {"start":"funnel"}

[WARNING]
A funnel does NOT bias the slope, the line still sits in the right place. What it wrecks is the standard errors: `lm()` assumes one constant spread, so when the spread actually grows, every p-value and confidence interval it prints is untrustworthy. The fixes are a transform of the response (a log often tames a funnel) or robust / weighted standard errors.

## Linearity, and the curve that breaks it

The **L** in LINE says the true relationship is a straight line: on average, cups really do rise by a constant amount per degree. Formally, the mean of \(y\) is linear in \(x\):

\[ E[y_i] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i \]

where \(E[y_i]\) is the expected (average) cups at temperature \(x_i\). When that is false, the residuals betray it with a **U-shaped curve** instead of a flat band.

Why does a bend show up as a U? Suppose Priya's sales actually *accelerate* in the heat: on a cool day an extra degree barely adds a cup, but once it is hot every extra degree brings a bigger rush, so cups curve upward instead of climbing in a straight line. A straight line, forced through that upward-bending data, sits too high in the middle and too low at the ends, so the residuals dip negative in the middle and rise positive at both ends. Here is what that curved relationship looks like as a scatter:

::widget chart-plotter {"data":[{"x":15,"y":30},{"x":18,"y":32},{"x":21,"y":35},{"x":24,"y":39},{"x":27,"y":44},{"x":30,"y":50},{"x":33,"y":57},{"x":36,"y":65},{"x":39,"y":74}],"geoms":["point"],"x":"temp","y":"cups"}

The dots clearly bend upward, a straight line cannot follow them. Now toggle the residual widget to "Curve" to see the giveaway U-shape that bend leaves behind in the residuals. The fix is to give the model the curvature it needs: add a squared term (`temp + I(temp^2)`) or transform a variable.

## Normality, and the Q-Q plot

The **N** in LINE says the errors follow a normal (bell-shaped) distribution centered at zero:

\[ \varepsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2) \]

read as "each error is drawn from a normal bell curve with mean 0 and variance \(\sigma^2\)." You do not check this by squinting at a histogram of 12 points. You use a **Q-Q plot** (quantile-quantile), which sorts the residuals and plots them against the values a perfect bell curve would produce. If the residuals are normal, the points fall along a straight diagonal line. Systematic curving away from the line, especially at the ends, flags skew or heavy tails.

```r
# Two ways to look at the residuals' shape:
qqnorm(residuals(fit))                 # residual quantiles vs normal quantiles
qqline(residuals(fit), col = "red")    # the line they should hug if normal

plot(fit, which = 2)                   # R's built-in normal Q-Q plot of the same thing
```

Run that and read it: points hugging the red line means the bell-curve assumption is fine. For a formal companion, `shapiro.test(residuals(fit))` returns a p-value, where a small p (below 0.05) flags non-normality, though it gets fussy and over-eager on large samples.

[NOTE]
Normality is the *least* critical of the four. With a healthy sample size the Central Limit Theorem makes the coefficient estimates behave well even when the errors are not perfectly normal. Worry about it most for small samples and for prediction intervals; worry about linearity and equal variance first.

## Independence, and patterns in time

The **I** in LINE says the errors are independent: knowing one day's miss tells you nothing about the next day's.

\[ \operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_j) = 0 \quad \text{for } i \neq j \]

where \(\operatorname{Cov}\) is covariance, a measure of how two things move together; zero means the misses do not track each other at all. This is the assumption residuals-vs-fitted cannot see, because it is about *order*, usually time or space.

Priya's 12 rows are in calendar order. Suppose a heatwave ran Monday through Thursday: if the line under-predicts on Monday it will likely under-predict Tuesday and Wednesday too, so the misses come in **runs** of the same sign instead of flipping randomly. That clustering is **autocorrelation**, a violation of independence. The check is to plot the residuals in their natural order and look for runs or waves rather than a random zig-zag.

```r
# Plot the residuals in the order the days were recorded.
plot(residuals(fit), type = "b",
     xlab = "day (in order)", ylab = "residual")
abline(h = 0, lty = 2)                 # random zig-zag around 0 = independent

acf(residuals(fit))                    # bars near zero (except lag 0) = no autocorrelation
```

A random scatter above and below zero is what you want. Long runs on one side, or a regular wave, mean today's error leaks into tomorrow's; the formal test is the Durbin-Watson statistic, and the fix usually means a time-series model rather than plain `lm()`.

## Which plot for normality?

You want to check whether a model's residuals are roughly normal. Which diagnostic do you read, and what does a healthy result look like?

::quiz {"correct":2,"gate":true,"difficulty":"intermediate"}
- The residuals-vs-fitted plot; healthy means a flat band ::no That plot checks linearity and equal variance, not normality. A flat band is exactly what you want there, but it says nothing about whether the residuals are bell-shaped.
- The normal Q-Q plot; healthy means the points fall close to the straight diagonal line ::ok Right. The Q-Q plot lines the residuals' quantiles up against a normal curve's quantiles; if the errors are normal the points hug the diagonal, and systematic curving at the ends flags skew or heavy tails.
- The raw scatter of cups against temperature; healthy means a strong correlation ::no A strong cups-temp correlation is about how well the line fits, not about residual normality. You judge normality from the residuals' Q-Q plot, never from the raw scatter.

## Lesson 2 complete

You can now interrogate a regression instead of trusting it. The line assumes four things, **L**inearity, **I**ndependence, **N**ormality, **E**qual variance, and the residuals tell you whether each holds: a flat residuals-vs-fitted band (no curve, no funnel) covers linearity and equal variance, a Q-Q plot on the diagonal covers normality, and residuals with no runs in time order cover independence. You know what each broken promise costs and the first fix to reach for.
